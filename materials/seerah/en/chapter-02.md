A Child Who Began Life as an Orphan

In the year that remained in Arab memory as the Year of the Elephant, a child was born in Makkah into Banu Hashim. His name was Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Abd al-Muttalib, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him. The family into which he was born was well known among Quraysh, and his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib was one of Makkah’s leading men. Yet the child entered the world after his first loss had already occurred: his father, Abdullah, did not live to see him. The best-known account in the books of Seerah is that Abdullah died before his son’s birth, so Muhammad began life without a father.

Allah’s Messenger was asked about fasting on Monday, whereupon he said: “It is (the day) when I was born and revelation was sent down to me.”

Sahih Muslim 1162e — published English translation on Sunnah.com

It is authentically established that he was born on a Monday. The exact date in Rabi al-Awwal, however, has long been disputed by historians. Several dates were reported, and the twelfth became especially famous, but the exact date remains a matter of historical disagreement. What the Prophet himself clearly connected to his birth was the day of the week: Monday.

Muhammad belonged to Banu Hashim, a clan of Quraysh, and his established genealogy extends back to Adnan. Tribal identity in Makkah was more than a family label. It shaped kinship, protection, alliances, and responsibility. Those relationships would repeatedly shape events when his mission began many years later.

During his earliest years he did not remain in the home of his mother, Aminah bint Wahb, all the time. Some Makkan families customarily sent their young children to women of the desert tribes to be nursed and to live for a period outside the city. His mother nursed him; Thuwaybah, a servant woman of Abu Lahab, also nursed him for a time; and the best-known foster mother who cared for him in the desert was Halimah al-Sadiyyah of Banu Sad, a branch of Hawazin. From the beginning, then, the orphaned child knew more than one household and more than one form of care.

The books of Seerah contain many reports about blessings that appeared in Halimah’s household and livestock after she took him with her. These reports are famous, but their individual details are not all equally strong. Their popularity does not make every detail certain. What is well known is that Muhammad spent part of his childhood among Banu Sad under Halimah’s care, and that foster relationship remained a recognized part of his biography.

Anas ibn Malik (may Allah be pleased with him) reported:
“Jibreel came to the Messenger of Allaah (peace and blessings of Allaah be upon him) when he was playing with the other boys. He took hold of him and threw him to the ground, then he opened his chest and took out his heart, from which he took a clot of blood and said: ‘This was the Shaytaan’s share of you.’ Then he washed it in a vessel of gold that was filled with Zamzam. Then he put it back together and returned it to its place. […] Anas said: I used to see the mark of that stitching on his chest.”

Sahih Muslim 162 — excerpt from the English translation published by Islam Q&A, answer 89869 (21 April 2007).

While he was still living among Banu Sad, the event known as the splitting of the chest took place. The basic account of this event is authentically narrated in Sahih Muslim from Anas ibn Malik. Muhammad was playing with other boys when Gabriel, peace be upon him, came to him, opened his chest, removed his heart, washed it with Zamzam water, and returned it. Anas later said that he could still see the mark of the stitching on the Prophet’s chest.

The splitting of the chest was an extraordinary event established by an authentic report. Physical explanations or further details beyond the account are not established by that report. It happened during his childhood, one of the unusual events that preceded revelation by many years.

After his years in the desert, Muhammad returned to his mother Aminah. When he was about six years old, she took him north to Yathrib, the city that would later be known as Madinah. Abd al-Muttalib’s family had ties with Banu Adi ibn al-Najjar there, so the young boy visited relatives with his mother. Then they began the journey back to Makkah. It did not end as it had begun.

On the return journey, at a place called al-Abwa between Makkah and Madinah, Aminah died. Muhammad was about six. He had left Makkah beside his mother and now had to return without her. His orphanhood had deepened: first the father he never had the chance to know, then the mother whose care he had known directly. Among the people associated with his care in these years was Barakah, better known as Umm Ayman.

After Aminah’s death, his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib became his guardian. The books of Seerah describe a special affection between the grandfather and the boy. Abd al-Muttalib had a cushion placed for him in the shade of the Kaabah which his own sons would not normally sit on out of respect. When young Muhammad came and sat there and his uncles tried to move him, Abd al-Muttalib would tell them to leave the boy, swearing by Allah that the boy would have a great future. The man who had stood at the center of Quraysh during the Elephant crisis was now sheltering his orphaned grandson.

That shelter did not last for many years. When Muhammad was about eight, Abd al-Muttalib died. After the loss of his father and mother came a third loss: the man who had become his direct protector. His guardianship then passed to his uncle Abu Talib. Abu Talib and Abdullah, the Prophet’s father, were full brothers, sharing the same father and mother, and Abu Talib now took responsibility for his brother’s son. He remained close to him in the years that followed.

Did He not find you an orphan then take good care of you?

Qur’an 93:6 — QuranEnc, Rowwad Translation Center

These successive losses were profound: a father dies before the child can form memories of him; a mother dies when he is around six; a grandfather dies when he is around eight. The losses themselves were deeply painful. Yet when the Qur’an later reminded the Prophet of his childhood, it did not mention orphanhood alone. It mentioned the shelter that accompanied it.

Shelter does not mean that the pain never happened. Aminah really died. Abd al-Muttalib really died. Orphanhood was real. What it means is that hardship did not become abandonment: after his mother there was his grandfather, after his grandfather there was Abu Talib, and there were other people around him who cared for him.

The life of Moses, peace be upon him, also shows how trials and Allah’s care can accompany one another. In Surah Ta-Ha, Allah reminds him of what he had passed through, from infancy to his arrival at the appointed time:

When your sister was going along and said, ‘Shall I show you someone who will nurse him?’ Thus We brought you back to your mother, so that she may rejoice and not grieve. Then you killed a man [by mistake], but We saved you from distress, and We put you through several trials. You stayed among the people of Midian for several years, then you came [here] at a destined time, O Moses.

Qur’an 20:40 — QuranEnc, Rowwad Translation Center

Joseph, peace be upon him, was cast into a well by his brothers, then taken out and sold, far from his family. Later he was imprisoned despite the signs of his innocence. After his release, the king drew him close, and Joseph was entrusted with the storehouses of the land. These stages are related in Surah Yusuf, verses 15, 19–20, 35 and 54–56. His position of authority came after hardship; divine care did not mean that hardship never occurred. Allah says:

This is how We established Joseph in the land

Qur’an — excerpt 12:56 — QuranEnc, Rowwad Translation Center

Hajar (may Allah be pleased with her), the mother of Ishmael, asked Abraham, peace be upon him, “Has Allah ordered you to do so?” He said, “Yes.” She said, “Then He will not neglect us.”

Sahih al-Bukhari 3364 — excerpt from the English translation published on Sunnah.com.

The same distinction appears in the story of Hajar (may Allah be pleased with her) and her infant son Ishmael, peace be upon him, when Abraham, peace be upon him, left them in an uncultivated valley by Allah’s command. Once Hajar (may Allah be pleased with her) understood that the command was from Allah, she did not conclude that the road would be painless; she understood that Allah would not abandon them. The report is preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari and helps separate two things that can easily be confused: experiencing severe hardship and being left without divine care.

None of this means that orphanhood produced prophethood, or that Muhammad’s later message can be reduced to a psychological explanation based on childhood. Prophethood is Allah’s choosing and revelation. The losses of his childhood were real, as was the care he received. Other forms of work, experience, and preparation will come later, before he reaches forty and revelation begins.

So Muhammad entered the next stage of his life in the household of Abu Talib, in the same Makkah whose Sacred House had been preserved in the Year of the Elephant. He had known orphanhood, life in the desert, travel, bereavement, and changes in who cared for him, yet he had not been left without shelter. The world in which he would grow was shaped by the religion, customs, tribal relationships and commerce of Makkah and Arabian society, with its virtues and injustices—the complex order later described as Jahiliyyah.
