Chapter Twelve

When the Harm Became Direct: From the Markets of Makkah to Supporting the Vulnerable

Quraysh had failed to stop the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, through Abu Talib, and its attempt to keep people from hearing about him had also failed. The leaders had agreed on descriptions with which to warn visiting Arab delegations, yet news of the call still left Makkah with those who came to it.

Abu Talib's position, meanwhile, became clearer. Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib [two major branches of the Prophet's relatives within Quraysh] gathered around the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, to protect him, both those among them who were Muslim and those who remained on the religion of Quraysh. Abu Talib also declared in his poetry that he would not hand his nephew over to his enemies.

This prevented Quraysh from reaching the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, in the way it wanted, but it did not mean that they stopped harming him. After negotiation, character attacks, and warnings, the confrontation entered a stage in which the harm became more direct.

The Call Reaches the Arab Markets

The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, did not confine his call to the houses of Makkah. The Arabs had seasonal markets, such as ‘Ukaz, Majannah, and Dhu al-Majaz. Held at set times of the year, these gatherings brought tribes together for trade. These were not merely places for buying and selling. Tribes from different regions met there, so a man might hear news in the market and carry it back to his people when he returned home. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, would go out to these gatherings and call people to Allah.

Tariq ibn ‘Abdullah al-Muharibi, may Allah be pleased with him, preserved for us a scene from those days. He said that he saw the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, in the market of Dhu al-Majaz wearing a red hullah [a two-piece garment worn together and known among the Arabs], calling out:

“O people: Say that none has the right to be worshiped except Allāh; you will succeed.”

But a man was walking behind him and throwing stones at him until his heels and the backs of his ankles bled [the rear of the foot and the area near the Achilles tendon], while telling the people:

“Do not listen to him! He is a liar!”

Tariq asked who the man calling the people was. He was told that the caller was a man from Banu ‘Abd al-Muttalib. Then he asked about the man following him and throwing stones. He learned that the stone-thrower was ‘Abd al-‘Uzza, Abu Lahab, the uncle of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him.

Other reports likewise describe Abu Lahab following the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, among the camping places of the tribes during the season [the places where tribes stayed while gathering for pilgrimage and the markets]. Whenever the Messenger of Allah called them to Allah, his uncle would come behind him, warning them against him and telling them not to abandon the religion of their forefathers.

Abu Lahab’s hostility was not confined to the incident at al-Safa. Abu Lahab did not merely curse the Prophet once and leave the matter there. He continued pursuing him to obstruct his call. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, would reach visitors from across Arabia, and Abu Lahab would come after him, trying to reach the same people with the opposite message.

More troubling still, a visitor from outside Makkah might see a man calling to Allah and then see that man's own uncle following him and accusing him of lying. Such a visitor might wonder: If Muhammad is truthful, would not his own uncle be among those who know him best?

For this reason, Abu Lahab's conduct caused harm in several ways: through kinship, through public denial before strangers, and through physical abuse itself.

Yet the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, continued to say:

“Say that none has the right to be worshiped except Allāh; you will succeed.”

Quraysh understood this statement. They knew that “There is no god worthy of worship except Allah” did not merely mean acknowledging that Allah exists; they already knew of Allah and acknowledged Him as Creator. Rather, it meant that worship belongs to Him alone, and that al-Lat, al-‘Uzza, Manat, Hubal, and the other objects they worshipped had no right to any act of worship.

The Qur'an describes their objection:

“Has he made all gods into one God? Indeed, this is something strange!”

— Qur’an 38:5 — Rowwad Translation Center.

They understood what he was asking of them. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, also knew that this was the point of disagreement, yet he did not change the foundation of the message to make it more acceptable to them.

Even Prayer Became a Target of Threats

The harm did not stop at attempts to prevent the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, from speaking to people. Even when he prayed by the Ka‘bah, he was exposed to threats.

Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, reported that Abu Jahl asked some men of Quraysh:

“Does Muhammad place his face on the ground in your presence?”

In other words: Does he place his face on the ground in prostration while you are watching?

They said: Yes.

Abu Jahl then swore by al-Lat and al-‘Uzza that if he saw him doing so, he would step on his neck or rub his face in the dust.

He then approached the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, while he was praying, intending to carry out his threat.

But the people were surprised to see him retreating backwards and shielding his face with his hands.

They asked him: “What is the matter with you?”

He said that he had seen between himself and the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, a trench of fire, terror, and wings.

The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said:

“If he had come near me, the angels would have torn him to pieces.”

— Sahih Muslim 2797. Published translations of the quoted questions and the Prophet’s reply: HadeethEnc 10558 (excerpts).

The hadith also mentions the words of Allah:

“Have you seen the one who prevents

a slave [of Allah] from praying?

What if this [slave] is rightly guided,

and enjoins righteousness?

How about the one who denies and turns away?

Does he not know that Allah is watching all?

No indeed; if he does not desist, We will surely drag him by the forelock

a lying, sinful forelock.

So let him call his associates;

We will call the wardens of Hellfire.

No indeed; do not obey him, but prostrate and draw near [to Allah].”

— Qur’an 96:9–19 — Rowwad Translation Center.

The nadi [the council or gathering place where the men of a people met] was where the men of Makkah gathered and discussed their affairs. Hence came the challenge:

So let him call his associates; We will call the wardens of Hellfire. — Qur’an 96:17–18 — Rowwad Translation Center.

The zabaniyah [the angels entrusted with punishment in the Fire] are the wardens referred to here.

The man who threatened the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, with his own people was thus answered with the reminder that the power he boasted of was not the highest power.

Yet Allah's protection of His Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, did not mean that he lived untouched by fear and pain.

The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said concerning some of what he endured:

“I have been intimidated in the path of Allah at a time when no one else was intimidated, and I have been harmed for the sake of Allah at a time when no one else was harmed […]”

He then mentioned a period when he and Bilal, may Allah be pleased with him, had only the small amount of food that Bilal could carry.

— Jami‘ al-Tirmidhi 2472 — published translation: HadeethEnc 10966 (excerpt).

He was protected so that he could deliver the message, but on the path of delivering it he still faced threats, hunger, and harm.

The Harm Was Not Equal for Every Muslim

As hostility increased, Quraysh began placing pressure on the Muslims themselves. But the Muslims were not all in the same social position. Makkan society was tribal. The protection of a person’s clan, the group of relatives to whom he belonged, greatly affected how much an enemy could do to him. A man backed by a powerful clan was not like someone who had no protection. A free man of lineage and standing was not in the same position as a client affiliated with a tribe or family through alliance or manumission rather than blood, nor as an enslaved person legally under an owner’s authority in that society.

This does not mean that people from strong tribes never felt fear or suffered harm.

Even ‘Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him — after his conversion at a later stage — was described by his son ‘Abdullah as being one day in the house, afraid, while men of Quraysh had gathered against him. Then al-‘As ibn Wa'il al-Sahmi granted him protection [publicly announced that he was under his protection and prevented people from attacking him], told the people that ‘Umar was under his protection, and they left.

— Sahih al-Bukhari 3864.

But the harshest forms of torture fell upon vulnerable people who lacked power or a clan capable of protecting them. Quraysh could subject them directly to physical violence.

On the Burning Ground of Makkah

Among the most famous of those tortured was Bilal ibn Rabah, may Allah be pleased with him. Bilal was enslaved at that time, so he had no clan standing between him and the man who owned him. Seerah reports state that he was taken out in the intense heat onto the sun-scorched ground of Makkah. He would be thrown on his back on that burning ground, a heavy stone would be placed on him, and he would be ordered to renounce Islam.

We encountered earlier the words preserved in the reports:

“One, One.”

By these words, he affirmed that Allah is One and remained firm in refusing to worship anyone besides Him.

Bilal did not enter Islam because it offered him immediate new social protection. He entered it knowing that the man who owned him could drag him onto the burning earth and torture him.

Among those who suffered harm were also Yasir’s family: Yasir, his wife Sumayyah, and their son ‘Ammar, may Allah be pleased with them. The books of Seerah report that Sumayyah, may Allah be pleased with her, the mother of ‘Ammar ibn Yasir, was the first martyr in Islam — that is, the first Muslim reported to have been killed because of steadfastness upon the faith. For some of the earliest believers, the word “believed” was therefore beginning to carry a meaning beyond conviction in the heart. Faith could become a test of how much suffering a person could endure without abandoning the truth he had come to believe.

What Could a Muslim Who Had Wealth Do?

At this stage another form of supporting the call appeared. Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, may Allah be pleased with him, was not enslaved, nor was he a poor man unable to act. He was a man of Quraysh with trade, wealth, and social connections, and he had entered Islam from its earliest days. When some Muslims began to be tortured because they had no way to escape their masters, Abu Bakr began using what he possessed to remove them from that situation. He would purchase some of these enslaved people and then free them from slavery.

Ibn Abi Shaybah narrates from ‘Urwah ibn al-Zubayr — in a mursal report [a report in which a narrator relates an event from before his own time without naming the Companion or direct transmitter through whom it reached him] — that Abu Bakr, may Allah be pleased with him, freed seven people who were being tortured for the sake of Allah:

‘Amir ibn Fuhayrah, Bilal, Zinnirah, Umm ‘Ubays, al-Nahdiyyah and her daughter, and an enslaved woman belonging to Banu ‘Amr ibn Mu'ammil.

The word jariyah here means [an enslaved woman in that society].

— Musannaf Ibn Abi Shaybah, in the virtues of Abu Bakr.

What is striking about this list is that most of those named were women or socially vulnerable people. Abu Bakr was not purchasing strong men in order to build a group that could protect him. He was spending his wealth to remove people from torture.

Among them was Bilal, may Allah be pleased with him, who years later would become the mu'adhdhin of the Messenger of Allah [the person who gives the call to prayer and summons Muslims to the prayer]. Yet when Abu Bakr sought to free him, he was not dealing with a man of office or power. He was dealing with a Muslim being tortured because he said: My Lord is Allah.

But the most righteous will be saved from it, — Qur’an 92:17 — Rowwad Translation Center.

Surat al-Layl contrasts two paths:

“As for the one who gives in charity and fears Allah,

and truly believes in the best reward,

We will make easy for him the way of salvation.

But as for the one who is miserly and deems himself self-sufficient,

and denies the best reward,

We will make easy for him the way of perdition.”

— Qur’an 92:5–10 — Rowwad Translation Center.

Allah then describes those whom He will keep away from the Fire:

“But the most righteous will be saved from it,

who spends his wealth to purify himself,

not in return for someone’s favors,

but to seek the pleasure of his Lord, the Most High.

He will surely be well pleased.”

— Qur’an 92:17–21 — Rowwad Translation Center.

It is reported from ‘Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, may Allah be pleased with him and his father, and from other early scholars of tafsir that these verses were revealed concerning Abu Bakr, may Allah be pleased with him. A number of Qur'an commentators connected them to his spending and to his freeing of vulnerable believers.

These reports are not all at the level of authentic hadith directly attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him. We therefore do not need to treat the specific sabab al-nuzul [the incident or person connected with the revelation of a verse] as certain in order to understand the connection.

The action described by the verses is clearly visible in the life of Abu Bakr:

who spends his wealth to purify himself, — Qur’an 92:18 — Rowwad Translation Center.

He spent not because these people would return a benefit to him, nor because he wanted service from them after their manumission.

Rather:

but to seek the pleasure of his Lord, the Most High. — Qur’an 92:20 — Rowwad Translation Center.

That is, through his spending he sought the pleasure of Allah.

Why Would Abu Bakr Continue to Hold This Position?

Many years would pass after these days. The migration, Badr, Uhud, and the other events of Madinah would come. But near the end of his own life, the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, would remind people of something that had begun in this early period. He said:

“No doubt, I am indebted to Abu Bakr more than to anybody else regarding both his companionship and his wealth. And if I had to take a Khalil from my followers, I would certainly have taken Abu Bakr […]”

— Sahih al-Bukhari 3904 — published translation: Sunnah.com (excerpt).

Here, being indebted to Abu Bakr means that he gave the greatest service to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, through companionship and wealth. The closest friend mentioned in the hadith is a beloved companion whose love has reached the highest degree of closeness.

Abu Bakr's rank was therefore not built upon one late event. From the days when Islam had few supporters, he believed the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, called others to Islam, spent from his wealth, and then, when he saw Muslims being tortured, made his wealth a means of freeing some of them. Quraysh used the influence it possessed to pressure weak people until they abandoned their religion. Abu Bakr used the wealth he possessed to remove some of Quraysh's power over those same vulnerable people.

The call had now entered a stage in which the conflict was no longer merely words spoken in the gatherings of Makkah. Abu Lahab followed the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, in the markets with stones and accusations of lying. Abu Jahl threatened him while he prayed. The vulnerable were dragged onto the burning ground of Makkah. Some were killed. On the other side, the believers began offering whatever support they could: protection, steadfastness, spending, and freeing enslaved people.

And this was only the beginning of the persecution. As Islam continued to spread, Quraysh would look for harsher ways to prevent people from following it, and some Muslims would soon have to ask a question that had not been present at the beginning of the call: Could they leave Makkah itself while preserving their religion?
