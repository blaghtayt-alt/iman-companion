Chapter Twenty-Two

When the Qur'an Reached Umar

Accounts of His Acceptance of Islam and the First Effects of the Change

1. More than one account of Umar’s conversion

In the previous chapter, we saw why Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, was a man whose acceptance of Islam the Muslims could hope for, and why the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, prayed that Allah would strengthen Islam through him.

The reports differ over how Umar came to accept Islam.

Here we need some precision, because the story has not reached us through one single account whose every detail is agreed upon. There are authentic reports in Sahih al-Bukhari connected to his conversion and the events around it. There are also two famous accounts preserved by Ibn Ishaq in the Seerah: one transmitted through narrators of Madinah and another through narrators of Makkah. Some details may fit together, but others describe the very moment of conversion in two different ways. After relating both, Ibn Ishaq stated that Allah knows best which account is correct.

The differing details should therefore not be combined into a single story presented as fully established. Each account needs to be read according to the strength of its evidence, while noting the broader themes they share.

2. Something Umar heard before Islam

Imam al-Bukhari includes, in the chapter on Umar’s acceptance of Islam, a report narrated by Abdullah ibn Umar from his father. After becoming Muslim, Umar was known for keen discernment: the ability to notice signs in people or situations that could lead to an accurate judgment. Abdullah ibn Umar recalled that whenever he had heard his father express a judgment about something, events had borne it out. — Sahih al-Bukhari, 3866 (meaning of the report).

On one occasion, a man passed by. Umar looked at him and thought he had been a soothsayer before Islam, someone who claimed knowledge of hidden matters through jinn. The man was brought and admitted that he had indeed been his people’s soothsayer before Islam. Umar asked him about the strangest thing that had come to him during his practice. The man mentioned words he had heard from his female jinn companion, speaking about a great change that had taken place.

Umar then remembered something he himself had experienced before accepting Islam. He said he had been near the people’s idols when a man brought a calf and slaughtered it. Umar heard a loud voice speaking in rhymed phrases, including: “An eloquent man is saying: None has the right to be worshipped except Allah.” Not long afterward, people began saying that a prophet had appeared. — Sahih al-Bukhari, 3866; published English translation, Sunnah.com (excerpt).

This report is established in Sahih al-Bukhari. But the text itself does not say that this incident was the direct cause of Umar’s conversion. Ibn Hajar, in Fath al-Bari, mentions other reports that connect it with what happened later, including an account in which Umar set out intending to reach the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, before eventually arriving at his sister’s house. Those additions, however, are not at the same evidentiary level as what al-Bukhari himself records. We therefore distinguish between two things: that Umar experienced this incident is established, while the claim that it directly led him to Islam comes through other, weaker routes.

3. He set out intending to reach the Messenger of Allah

In the well-known Madinan version recorded by Ibn Ishaq, Umar went out one day with his sword hanging from him, heading toward the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him. At that time the Prophet was with a number of his Companions in a house near al-Safa, the house known as Dar al-Arqam. Muslims who remained in Makkah used to gather there, while another group had already migrated to Abyssinia.

In this account, Umar was heading toward the Prophet intending to kill him. On the way he met Nuaym ibn Abdullah al-Nahham, a man from Banu Adi who had secretly accepted Islam and concealed his faith out of fear of his people.

Nuaym asked Umar where he was going. Umar told him he was going to Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, and repeated Quraysh’s accusations that he had abandoned their religion, divided their people, and criticized their gods.

Nuaym understood the danger. Without revealing his own faith, he challenged Umar’s assumption that Banu Abd Manaf would leave him unharmed after he killed Muhammad. He then directed Umar’s attention to his own household, telling him that his sister Fatimah bint al-Khattab and her husband Said ibn Zayd had accepted Islam. Umar changed direction and went to his sister’s house.

4. The Qur'an was being read inside the house

Said ibn Zayd and Fatimah bint al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with them, were inside. Khabbab ibn al-Aratt was teaching them the Qur’an from a written sheet containing part of Surah Ta-Ha.

As Umar approached, he heard recitation. When they realized he was coming, Khabbab hid and Fatimah concealed the sheet. Umar entered and asked about the low murmuring he had heard. The Arabic word used in Ibn Ishaq’s account, haynamah, means a quiet sound whose words cannot be clearly distinguished.

They tried to deflect the question, but Umar confronted them with what he had learned: Fatimah, his sister, and her husband, Said, had followed Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him. Umar attacked Said ibn Zayd. Fatimah intervened to defend her husband, and Umar struck her until she bled. At that point Fatimah no longer concealed her faith. She and her husband openly told Umar that they had accepted Islam and believed in Allah and His Messenger. They told him he could do as he wished.

The scene had changed. He was no longer facing someone who retreated from him in fear. His own sister, with blood on her face, was telling him plainly that she had chosen this faith.

5. When he saw the blood, he stopped

When Umar saw what had happened to his sister, he calmed down. The report describes him drawing back from his anger and stopping what he had been doing. This matters: Umar’s strength did not mean that once he rushed into something he was incapable of turning back. When he saw something that made him reconsider, he stopped.

He asked for the sheet they had been reading so that he could examine what Muhammad had brought. His sister feared he would tear it. When he assured her that he would return it, she told him, according to the report, that he was still a polytheist and that only someone who was purified could touch it. He washed and then took the sheet, which contained part of Surah Ta-Ha.

The report does not identify particular verses; it states that he began to read from the surah.

6. Recognizing the beauty and nobility of the words

Umar read the opening of the surah and expressed his admiration for the beauty and nobility of its words. Khabbab was still hiding. On hearing this, he came out and expressed his hope that Allah had singled Umar out in answer to the Prophet’s prayer. He recalled the Prophet’s supplication for Allah to strengthen Islam through one of the two men.

Umar then decided to accept Islam and asked Khabbab to show him where Muhammad was so that he could go to him. The account describes no request for days of deliberation or delay to gauge Quraysh’s reaction. Once something of the Qur’an’s truth became clear to him, his force of character turned in a new direction. The intensity that had opposed Islam did not disappear; it was now preparing to serve it.

7. At the door of Dar al-Arqam

Khabbab told him that the Messenger of Allah was in the house near al-Safa. Umar took his sword and went there. When he knocked, the Companions recognized his voice. One of them looked out and saw Umar carrying his sword, then went back inside in fear to tell the Prophet who was at the door and that he was armed.

Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib, may Allah be pleased with him, urged them to let Umar enter. He said that if Umar came seeking good, they would give it to him; if he came seeking harm, they would kill him with his own sword. The Prophet allowed Umar in, rose to meet him, took hold of his garment and asked what had brought him there.

Umar explained that he had come to believe in Allah, His Messenger and what had come from Allah. The Messenger of Allah proclaimed Allah’s greatness, and those in the house knew that Umar had accepted Islam.

This is the best-known version in the books of Seerah. It is transmitted through several routes, but not every detail rests on a fully connected authentic chain at the level of the hadiths of Sahih al-Bukhari. We therefore present it as Ibn Ishaq does: a famous Seerah account, not an authenticated hadith in every detail. — Ibn Hisham, account of Umar’s acceptance of Islam; dialogue reported in meaning from the Arabic.

8. A completely different night

Ibn Ishaq records a second account from the people of Makkah, describing the moment differently. In this version, Umar recalled that before Islam he drank wine. One night he went looking for the companions with whom he used to drink, but found no one.

Umar decided to try the wine seller, but could not find him either. He then decided to go to the Ka‘bah and walk around it in worship. On reaching the Sacred Mosque, he found the Messenger of Allah standing in prayer and decided to listen.

He approached the Ka‘bah from the side of al-Hijr, the area enclosed by the low wall beside it. This area includes part of the Ka‘bah’s original footprint that Quraysh left outside when rebuilding it, as explained in Sahih Muslim 1333. Umar moved close to the Prophet, with the Ka‘bah’s covering between them, and listened to the Qur’an.

9. The Qur’an softened his heart

In Ibn Ishaq’s account, Umar described how hearing the Qur’an softened his heart, moved him to tears and opened his heart to Islam. He stayed where he was until the Prophet finished his prayer. The Messenger of Allah then left for his home, and Umar followed him.

When the Prophet heard his footsteps and recognized him, he thought Umar had followed him intending harm. He asked what had brought Umar there at that hour. Umar explained that he had come to believe in Allah, His Messenger and what had come from Allah. The Prophet praised Allah and prayed that Umar would remain firm. They then parted.

After relating this account, Ibn Ishaq stated that Allah knows best which account is correct. — Ibn Hisham, the second account of Umar’s acceptance of Islam; dialogue reported in meaning from the Arabic.

That statement matters. Ibn Ishaq himself did not try to turn the two reports into one story. In the first, the decisive sequence begins at his sister’s house and ends at Dar al-Arqam. In the second, the change begins when he hears the Qur'an at the Ka'bah. To insist that every element occurred as one continuous sequence requires evidence that Ibn Ishaq’s own presentation does not provide.

10. One element almost all the reports share

Despite their different details, the reports share a clear theme: the Qur’an. In the well-known account, Umar reads from Surah Ta-Ha; in the Makkan account, he hears the Prophet reciting beside the Ka‘bah. Other reports also place hearing the Qur’an within this transformation.

This is what can safely be drawn from the collection of reports without forcing every detail into one version: Umar had spent years on the opposite side of Islam, but when the Qur'an reached him directly, his position changed.

This helps explain something we have repeatedly seen in the Makkan period. Not everyone who opposes a message has actually heard it clearly. A person may be surrounded by an image of a religion created by society without ever having read its text. In the report, Umar wanted to examine for himself what Muhammad had brought.

That request is significant. Years of the mission had already passed, yet he now had something before him that he could read for himself and judge directly. Once he did, the outcome changed.

11. Who was the fastest man in Makkah at spreading news?

If the reports differ over the exact moment Umar accepted Islam, the reports about what happened afterward reveal something consistent with the personality we already know: he did not want his Islam to remain secret.

Ibn Ishaq narrates through Nafi from Ibn Umar that, after accepting Islam, Umar asked who among Quraysh spread news most quickly, making it known throughout Makkah. He was directed to Jamil ibn Ma‘mar al-Jumahi.

Umar went to him in the morning. Abdullah ibn Umar, then a young boy, followed to see what his father would do. Umar told Jamil that he had accepted Islam and entered the religion of Muhammad.

Jamil did not stop to ask for details. He went straight to the Sacred Mosque, where Quraysh were sitting in their meeting places around the Ka‘bah, and loudly announced that Ibn al-Khattab had abandoned their religion. He used the Arabic word saba’a, which Quraysh applied to someone who left his people’s religion for another.

Umar followed behind him, rejected that description as false, and declared that he had accepted Islam. He affirmed that none is worthy of worship except Allah and that Muhammad is His servant and Messenger.

He did not even allow the man spreading the news to define his Islam using Quraysh’s language. He stated himself what had happened.

12. Publicly accepting Islam did not make Umar stronger than all of Quraysh

Men of Quraysh rushed at him. Ibn Ishaq’s report says they fought him and he fought them until the sun rose high. Umar was strong, but he was still one man. The fighting exhausted him until he sat down while they surrounded him.

Umar told them to do as they wished, then swore by Allah that if the Muslims numbered three hundred men, one side would have had to leave Makkah to the other. — Ibn Hisham, report through Nafi from Ibn Umar; Umar’s words are reported in meaning.

Umar understood the difference between courage and the actual balance of power. Being courageous did not make him an army by himself.

This is important for understanding Umar’s conversion. The Muslims did not become the dominant power in Makkah overnight. But Umar’s entrance into Islam changed something in their ability to appear openly and defend themselves. Abdullah ibn Masud, may Allah be pleased with him, therefore said in an authentic report: “We have been powerful since `Umar embraced Islam.” - Sahih al-Bukhari, 3863.

He did not say: “From the moment Umar accepted Islam, Quraysh stopped harming us.” He said: we had strength and honor. That is different.

13. A polytheist protects Umar

While Umar was in that situation, a prominent man of Quraysh came and asked what was happening. On learning that Umar had accepted Islam, he challenged their interference in a man’s choice and warned that Banu Adi would not simply hand over their tribesman. He told them to leave Umar alone.

Years later, Abdullah ibn Umar asked his father who had protected him from the crowd. Umar identified the man as al-As ibn Wa’il al-Sahmi. — Ibn Hisham, report through Nafi from Ibn Umar; dialogue reported in meaning.

The core of this event is established even more strongly in Sahih al-Bukhari. Abdullah ibn Umar narrates that after accepting Islam, Umar was in his house, afraid his people might kill him. Al-As ibn Wa'il came. Banu Sahm and Banu Adi were bound by an alliance of mutual support and protection.

Al-As asked him what was wrong. Umar explained that his people had threatened to kill him if he accepted Islam. Al-As assured him that they would not be able to harm him, and Umar felt safe. — Sahih al-Bukhari, 3864 (meaning of the report).

Al-As went out and found that people had filled the valley, heading toward Umar. He asked where they were going. They replied that they wanted Ibn al-Khattab, who had abandoned their religion. Al-As said: “There is no way for anybody to touch him.” The people then turned back. — Sahih al-Bukhari, 3864; published English translation, Sunnah.com (excerpt).

14. Courage and fear existed in the same man

It is easy to create a simple image of Umar: a man who feared nothing. But the authentic reports do not present that image. Al-Bukhari explicitly describes him in his house as afraid.

Fear did not diminish Umar’s courage; it helps explain what courage meant in his situation. He knew Quraysh, their treatment of Muslims and his own recent part in that persecution. He also knew that numbers were against him. Yet he declared his faith, sought out the man who would spread the news and accepted the consequences. He recognized the danger without allowing it to determine his response to the truth.

15. He went personally to Abu Jahl

Another account preserved by Ibn Ishaq says that, after becoming Muslim, Umar considered who in Makkah was most hostile to the Messenger of Allah. He thought of Abu Jahl and went to his house the following morning to knock on his door.

Abu Jahl came out and welcomed him because there was kinship between the two families. Genealogists differed over Umar’s mother’s precise relationship to Banu Makhzum: whether she was Hantamah bint Hashim ibn al-Mughirah or, as sometimes stated, bint Hisham. Ibn Abd al-Barr preferred the first view. We therefore should not state categorically, as some popular retellings do, that Abu Jahl was Umar’s direct maternal uncle.

Umar told Abu Jahl that he had come to inform him of his faith in Allah and His Messenger Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, and his acceptance of the truth of what the Prophet had brought. Abu Jahl slammed the door in his face and condemned him harshly. — Ibn Hisham, account of Umar’s visit to Abu Jahl; dialogue reported in meaning.

The message had been delivered. According to the report, Umar did not merely tell a friend or a neutral man. He chose one of the Prophet’s most hostile opponents and informed him personally.

16. Protection is not the same as victory

The story of al-As ibn Wa’il brings back a concept we have encountered before, but here it appears in a new form. Al-As was not Muslim. Yet the social customs of Quraysh allowed him to place Umar under his protection and prevent the crowd from harming him. The crowd withdrew.

This was protection backed by social standing or strength, preventing an opponent from reaching someone easily. Such protection was not the same as complete victory.

In Makkah, the Muslims passed through different levels of support. At first the Prophet found people who believed him. Then forms of protection emerged that prevented enemies from doing everything they wished to him and to some Muslims. But the Muslims still did not possess political authority that could stop Quraysh from persecuting every believer. Later, in Madinah, the Prophet would have a community and organized society capable of defending him. After that would come the conquest and wider empowerment.

Umar’s story makes these distinctions clear. A strong man entered Islam and the Muslims gained greater dignity and strength, but they still remained a vulnerable minority within a society stronger than them.

17. His old strength did not disappear; it came under a new standard

If Umar’s story ended on the first day he accepted Islam, we would simply see a strong man moving from one side to another. Yet the effects of guidance extend beyond that first moment: how did faith shape his strength over the years?

Reports from Umar’s later caliphate illustrate how faith continued to shape his character. These selected episodes are not a complete account of his rule. His determination, severity and commanding presence remained, but he had to govern them justly: distinguish firmness from oppression and consider whether the awe he inspired had prevented someone in need from speaking.

18. Gentleness, firmness and the fear of Allah

Ibn Sa’d reports that a number of senior Companions, among them Ali, Uthman, Talhah, al-Zubayr and Sa’d, felt that the awe Umar inspired as caliph prevented some people from bringing their needs to him. They asked Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf to raise the matter because he was among those most able to speak openly with Umar.

Abd al-Rahman entered and asked the Commander of the Believers to be gentler with people. He explained that a man might come with a need, feel too intimidated to speak, and return without even presenting it.

Umar realized that this was not merely Abd al-Rahman’s individual opinion and asked who had sent him.

Umar’s reply reveals the struggle of a just ruler with the power in his hands. He swore by Allah that he had been so lenient with people that he feared Allah over his leniency; then he had been so severe that he feared Allah over his severity. He ended by asking: “So where is the way out?” — Ibn Sa’d, Kitab at-Tabaqat al-Kabir, vol. III, translated by Aisha Bewley, Ta-Ha, 2013, p. 222 (short excerpt). The preceding dialogue is related by meaning.

His problem was not: How do I make people fear me? They already did. What he feared was: How can I be strong without injustice, and gentle without neglecting responsibility?

When Abd al-Rahman heard this, he rose in tears. The man whose severity others feared was himself afraid of Allah because of that severity.

19. Wealth before him - and he remembered the life of the Prophet

Ibn Sa'd narrates from Ibn Abbas that a large amount of wealth once came to Umar. He summoned Uthman ibn Affan and Abdullah ibn Abbas and gave them some of it to distribute among the people.

Ibn Abbas and Umar spoke about this wealth and the hardship in which the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and his Companions had lived. Umar asked whether this wealth had not already been with Allah when Muhammad ﷺ and his Companions were eating the hide of young sheep or goats, known as al-qadd, which was eaten in times of scarcity. — Ibn Sa’d, al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, Dar Sadir edition, 3:288; the account is related by meaning.

Ibn Abbas replied that if such wealth had been opened to the Prophet, he would have eaten from it and fed his Companions. Umar was moved and wept. He said that he hoped to leave the responsibility of rule with neither gain nor loss - seeking no personal benefit from the caliphate, but hoping not to meet Allah carrying its burden as sin.

The wealth before him was unlike what Muslims had possessed in Makkah. Its abundance did not make his responsibility easier: it increased the need to examine where it should go, who was entitled to it and on what grounds.

20. A man people feared, yet one of those who wept the most

Other reports collected by Ibn Sa'd present a side of Umar that can seem, at first glance, very different from the man known for awe and severity. He used to check on people at night.

One report describes Umar passing a group in the mosque after the Isha prayer. Ubayy ibn Ka’b was among them. Umar asked why they had remained after prayer and learned that they had stayed to remember Allah. He sat with them, and they made supplication in turn. When one man’s turn came, he felt so overawed by Umar’s request that he could not speak.

Umar gently explained that a simple prayer for forgiveness and mercy would suffice: “O Allah, forgive us. O Allah, have mercy on us.” Then Umar’s turn came. According to the Arabic report, he supplicated and wept more intensely than anyone else in the group. — Ibn Sa’d, al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, Dar Sadir edition, 3:294; prayer quoted from Aisha Bewley’s translation, vol. III, p. 227 (short excerpt).

These are among the historical reports cited by Ibn Sa'd, and we do not place them at the same level as the authentic reports from al-Bukhari mentioned earlier. But they belong to a wider group of reports that reveal a side of Umar that disappears if he is reduced to the image of a stern man alone.

21. Devotion was not, for him, a way of walking

On one occasion al-Shifa bint Abdullah saw young men walking and speaking slowly. She asked about them and was told that they were known for their worship and ascetic devotion.

She recalled Umar and swore by Allah that when he spoke, he made himself heard; when he walked, he walked briskly; and when he struck, it hurt. Yet he was, she affirmed, truly devoted to worship. — Ibn Sa’d, al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, Dar Sadir edition, 3:290; the account is related by meaning.

The point of the report is not that harshness is worship. It illustrates an early understanding of devotion: faith does not require an affected manner that makes a person seem weak or unnaturally subdued in voice and movement.

A similar report describes how the righteousness of Umar and his son Abdullah became evident when they spoke or acted. They did not cultivate an outward appearance meant to advertise their devotion; it showed in their words and deeds. — Ibn Sa’d, al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, Dar Sadir edition, 3:291; the report is related by meaning.

22. A shepherd on a mountain - and a responsibility he could not ignore

During a journey Umar passed a shepherd tending sheep. Having seen more fertile land earlier, he turned toward the shepherd, called to him, and told him of the better pasture. He added: “Every shepherd is responsible for his flock.” Then he continued on his way. — Ibn Sa’d, Kitab at-Tabaqat al-Kabir, translated by Aisha Bewley, vol. III, p. 225 (short excerpt); Arabic source: Dar Sadir edition, 3:291–292.

The shepherd was not a senior official, and the matter was neither a battle nor something requiring a caliph’s decree. Yet Umar saw a way to benefit the man and his sheep and stopped to help. Such small scenes show how his understanding of strength grew to include responsibility.

23. Some of what his caliphate produced

In his account of Umar’s achievements, Ibn Sa’d lists many measures that shaped the Islamic state.

Among them he mentions: adoption of the title Commander of the Believers, dating official records from the Hijrah of the Prophet, organizing administrative registers of people, stipends and state affairs, known as diwans, appointing judges in the provinces, organizing public stipends, holding governors accountable, founding new cities and administrative centers, the wide conquests in Iraq, Syria, Egypt and elsewhere, gathering people for organized Ramadan night prayer, and Umar himself leading the pilgrimage year after year.

Ibn Sa’d’s list also contains points that require qualification. One wording, for example, presents Umar as the first to collect the Qur’an into written sheets. This needs correction: that collection took place during Abu Bakr’s caliphate, after the battle of al-Yamamah.

So we do not treat every old list of “firsts” with the same certainty. But the broad picture does not depend on any one disputed detail: the man who entered Islam in Makkah did not merely make the Muslims stronger against Quraysh for a day or two. Over time, his ability to decide, organize and act firmly produced effects that reached the life of an entire community.

24. Between two statements

Abdullah ibn Masud used to say: “We have been powerful since `Umar embraced Islam.” At the end of Umar’s life, the Companions would feel that something immense had been lost with his death.

The period from Umar’s conversion until his death is therefore not simply the story of a strong man. It is the story of strength entering faith, then being trained and tested over many years.

On the first day, he wanted everyone in Makkah to know he had accepted Islam. Years later, he feared that his awe might stop a weak person from presenting a need. Said ibn Zayd remembered being restrained for his faith by Umar before Umar accepted Islam. Years later, as caliph, Umar asked himself how he could meet Allah without bearing responsibility for injustice against anyone.

The core personality remained recognizable: firmness, speed of decision, awe, and strength. But Islam redirected the central question: For whom and for what is this strength used?

25. The end of the sixth year

With Umar’s acceptance of Islam, we reach the end of another stage of the Makkan Seerah. Some chronological reports place his conversion in Dhu al-Hijjah of the sixth year of Prophethood, although not every detail of the dating is agreed upon with certainty.

Six years had passed. Islam had appeared, the earliest believers had entered it, Quraysh’s persecution had intensified, migrants had gone to Abyssinia, Hamzah had accepted Islam, and then Umar had accepted Islam.

Yet the confrontation with Quraysh was not moving toward calm. Quite the opposite. At the beginning of the seventh year, Quraysh would take a step more severe and more comprehensive than targeting individual Muslims one by one.

The confrontation would become an attempt to isolate the Prophet and those protecting him socially and economically. The boycott would affect both the Muslim and non-Muslim members of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib who stood by him, with the aim of forcing them to hand him over to Quraysh (Sahih al-Bukhari 1590).

A written pact would be drawn up. The boycott would begin. And here this chapter ends.
