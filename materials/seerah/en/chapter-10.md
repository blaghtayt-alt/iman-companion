Chapter Ten

When Quraysh Went to Abu Talib: The First Attempts to Stop the Call

After the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, stood on al-Safa and warned the clans of Quraysh, the call entered a new stage. The message itself was now public, and Quraysh understood that Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, was not calling to a private form of worship for himself. He was calling people to worship Allah alone and abandon what they worshipped besides Him.

But the shift to public preaching did not mean that every aspect of Muslim life suddenly became public.

The believers still moved cautiously in some circumstances and concealed their meeting places and prayers when necessary. What had become public was the call itself. The names of all Muslims, their meeting places, and every detail of their movements did not become known to Quraysh overnight.

Prayer in the Ravines of Makkah

Ibn Ishaq's Seerah reports that when the Companions of the Messenger of Allah wanted to pray, they would go out to the passes and valleys between Makkah’s mountains so that they could pray away from the eyes of their people.

On one occasion, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, may Allah be pleased with him, was praying with a group of Muslims in one of those ravines when a group of polytheists came upon them.

The polytheists objected to what they were doing and mocked their prayer. The confrontation then escalated until fighting broke out between them.

The report says that Sa'd struck one of them with the jawbone of a camel and wounded his head, and that this was the first blood shed in the conflict between the Muslims and the polytheists.

This is a historical report in Ibn Ishaq’s Seerah, rather than a hadith established from the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, through an authentic, uninterrupted chain of transmission.

Its exact date is also not established decisively. Ibn Ishaq places it near the beginning of the public call, but it could also fit the period when the Muslims were still concealing much of their worship. This itself helps explain that the boundary between "secret" and "public" preaching did not mean that everything moved from total concealment to complete openness in a single day.

The call had become public, but caution remained.

What Changed Quraysh's Attitude?

Ibn Ishaq relates that when the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, began openly proclaiming Islam, Quraysh did not all immediately enter into open war with him. Their hostility intensified when his criticism of idol worship and their deities became explicit.

The Prophet’s call to worship Allah also made clear that worshipping anything besides Him was false.

The meaning of monotheism itself was that what they worshipped besides Allah did not deserve worship and that the idol worship inherited from their forefathers was not the truth.

This touched something especially sensitive for Quraysh: their gods and the religion of their fathers.

Ibn Ishaq's wording here needs some qualification. We have already seen Abu Lahab openly reject the Prophet at al-Safa from the first public call. The point is therefore not that the Prophet had faced no opposition before this. Rather, what becomes visible at this stage is the widening of the hostility and its development into a more organized position among the leaders of Quraysh.

Ibn Ishaq says that when they heard him criticizing their gods, they regarded the matter as grave, rejected him, and united in opposing and showing hostility toward him.

The Muslims at that time were not a large community capable of protecting themselves by force. They were still few, and many concealed their Islam.

The Messenger of Allah, however, had another kind of protection.

Abu Talib Stands in His Defense

Abu Talib, the Prophet's uncle, was one of the leading elders of Banu Hashim and held an important position among his people. He had not entered Islam, but he remained deeply attached to his nephew, protected him, and did not allow Quraysh to reach him as they wished.

Ibn Ishaq described this by saying that Abu Talib showed care for the Messenger of Allah, protected him, and stood between him and his enemies.

The expression used by Ibn Ishaq conveys affection, concern, and active protection.

This carried great weight in a society where protection depended to a large extent on clan and tribal ties. Muhammad belonged to Banu Hashim, and Abu Talib was the senior figure of that household at the time. Quraysh therefore could not treat the Prophet as an isolated man with no one behind him.

According to the social customs of the time, the natural course was to go first to the man responsible for the protection of Banu Hashim.

So negotiations began.

The First Delegation to Abu Talib

Ibn Ishaq’s Seerah reports that a group of Quraysh’s leading men went to Abu Talib. Among those named are ‘Utbah and Shaybah, the sons of Rabi‘ah, Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, Abu Jahl, al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, al-‘As ibn Wa’il, and others. Ibn Ishaq qualifies the list, allowing that only some of those named may have gone.

These names will recur throughout the coming Makkan years because several of these men would become prominent opponents of the call.

Ibn Ishaq recounts the delegation’s complaint to Abu Talib:

They accused his nephew of insulting their gods, criticizing their religion, calling their judgment foolish and declaring their forefathers misguided. They demanded that Abu Talib either restrain him or stand aside and leave them to deal with him.

Their first request was still expressed in the language of negotiation.

In effect, they expected Abu Talib to stop his nephew because he belonged to Banu Hashim and was under his protection. If he would not do so, they wanted him to withdraw that protection and leave his nephew exposed to them.

Abu Talib did not confront them directly.

The Seerah reports that he spoke gently to them, gave them a courteous response, and they left.

Then something happened that would become a recurring pattern in this stage:

The Messenger of Allah continued on his course.

The call did not stop.

The Prophet returned to what he had been commanded to do, making Allah's religion known and calling people to it.

So the first visit did not solve the problem from Quraysh's perspective.

They Returned a Second Time - With a Different Tone

The call continued, as did the Prophet's criticism of polytheism and idol worship.

With time, the tension between Quraysh and the Messenger of Allah grew stronger.

Their leaders returned to Abu Talib again.

This time their words were different.

The account describes their renewed demands:

They acknowledged Abu Talib’s seniority, honor and standing among them, but reminded him that he had not stopped his nephew as they had requested. Swearing by Allah, they declared that they would no longer tolerate what they regarded as abuse of their forefathers, contempt for their judgment and criticism of their gods. Unless Abu Talib stopped his nephew, they threatened to confront both of them until one side was destroyed.

The message was clearer now.

The first time, they had asked him to restrain his nephew or stand aside.

Now they were threatening that the conflict might include Abu Talib himself and those who stood with him.

Abu Talib faced real pressure: on one side were his people, among whom he had lived and whose respect he held; on the other was his nephew, whom he did not want to hand over to them.

According to Ibn Ishaq’s account, Abu Talib sent for the Messenger of Allah, told him of his people’s complaints, and asked him to spare both of them and not place on his uncle a burden he could not bear.

The same account attributes a famous response to the Prophet. Its meaning is:

Addressing his uncle, he swore by Allah that even if they placed the sun in his right hand and the moon in his left to make him abandon his mission, he would not abandon it. He would persist until Allah made it prevail or he died in its service.

However, this famous wording comes through Ibn Ishaq with a chain of transmission that is not strong. Its fame alone does not make it an established authentic hadith.

There is, however, another report from 'Aqil ibn Abi Talib, may Allah be pleased with him, transmitted through a stronger route. In it, Abu Talib told the Prophet that his people were complaining about what they heard from him at the Ka'bah and in their gatherings and asked him to stop troubling them.

The Messenger of Allah looked up at the sky. In al-Tabarani’s account, he swore by Allah that he could not abandon his mission. A parallel version records this reply:

“I am no more capable of stopping that than you are of getting a flame from it (i.e. the sun).”

In other words, abandoning the mission for which Allah had sent him was no more possible for him than taking a flame directly from the sun was possible for them.

Abu Talib then swore by Allah that his nephew had never lied and told the delegation to return with sound judgment.

He remained committed to protecting him.

Abu Talib did not enter Islam, but he did not hand the Messenger of Allah over to Quraysh.

And the Messenger of Allah did not give up any part of what he had been sent with merely to preserve that protection.

Both realities therefore remained together: the call continued, and tribal protection remained in place.

A Stranger Offer Than the First

When Quraysh realized that Abu Talib did not intend to abandon his nephew, they tried another approach.

They brought him a young man of Quraysh named 'Umarah ibn al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, the brother of Khalid ibn al-Walid. Khalid would embrace Islam years later. 'Umarah was known among his people for strength and good appearance.

Ibn Ishaq relates the proposal they made to Abu Talib:

They presented ‘Umarah ibn al-Walid as Quraysh’s strongest and most handsome young man, offering him as an adopted son whose support Abu Talib would receive and whose blood money—the compensation due if he were killed—would belong to him. In exchange, they demanded his nephew so they could kill him, accusing Muhammad of opposing Abu Talib’s religion and that of his ancestors, dividing their people and calling their judgment foolish. They portrayed this as exchanging one man for another.

The offer was shocking in its harsh simplicity:

They would give Abu Talib one of their sons to raise in Muhammad’s place, provided he handed Muhammad over to them to be killed.

Abu Talib’s response made clear how he understood the offer:

He swore by Allah that it was a wretched bargain: they would give him their son to feed and raise, while he would give them his own son to kill. He swore again by Allah that this would never happen.

The issue for him was not whether the young man they offered was of greater or lesser status than Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him.

The issue was that they were asking him to raise their son while handing his own nephew over to them to be killed.

He refused.

Even al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi Sided with Quraysh at That Moment

Among those who addressed Abu Talib at that time was al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi, a man of Banu Nawfal. Years later, his position would be different in another famous episode: he would grant the Messenger of Allah protection when the Prophet returned from Ta'if.

At this stage, however, he was not standing with Abu Talib.

Al-Mut‘im swore by Allah that Abu Talib’s people had treated him fairly and had done all they could to resolve what troubled him. He added that Abu Talib seemed unwilling to accept anything from them.

Abu Talib swore by Allah that they had not treated him fairly. He accused al-Mut‘im of deciding to abandon him and support the others against him, then told him to do as he wished. Ibn Ishaq qualifies the wording, indicating that Abu Talib said this or words to that effect.

The distance between Abu Talib and several branches of Quraysh was now widening.

The Seerah books also attribute poetry to Abu Talib in which he reproached relatives whom he believed had abandoned him, singling out some branches of 'Abd Manaf closely related to Banu Hashim.

The issue was no longer merely that one man of Quraysh had brought a new religion.

It was beginning to affect relations among the clans and tribes themselves.

From Pressuring Abu Talib to Pressuring the Muslims

The first attempts to make Abu Talib abandon the Messenger of Allah had failed.

The Prophet did not stop calling people.

Quraysh therefore began taking another path.

Ibn Ishaq says that Quraysh began inciting one another against those Companions of the Messenger of Allah who belonged to their respective clans. In other words, each clan began dealing with the Muslims under its authority or protection.

The clans turned against the Muslims among them:

torturing them,

pressuring them,

and trying to force them away from their religion - that is, compelling them to abandon Islam.

From this point, different forms of persecution would appear according to each Muslim's position in society.

A person protected by a powerful clan was not in the same position as a slave, a client, or someone without strong tribal protection. Here, a client means someone affiliated with a family or tribe through alliance or manumission.

Yet even as Quraysh began persecuting the Muslims, they still faced one major problem:

What could they do with Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, himself?

He remained under the protection of Banu Hashim.

Abu Talib Gathers Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib

When Abu Talib saw what Quraysh was doing, he did not limit himself to his own personal protection of the Messenger of Allah.

He called upon Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib - two closely related branches descended from 'Abd Manaf - to stand with him in protecting Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him.

Not everyone who responded was Muslim.

To a large degree, the matter rested on kinship and tribal custom: Muhammad was one of their own, Quraysh wanted to reach him, and they would not accept handing over a man from their family to his opponents.

They therefore joined Abu Talib in protecting the Messenger of Allah and standing between him and his enemies - both those among them who had become Muslim and those who remained on the religion of Quraysh.

The clearest exception was:

Abu Lahab.

He too belonged to Banu Hashim and was an uncle of the Prophet, but the bond of kinship that moved others to protect Muhammad did not prevent Abu Lahab from standing on the side of hostility.

The situation in Makkah had now become much clearer than it had been before the call from al-Safa.

The Messenger of Allah openly proclaimed his message and would not retreat from it.

Quraysh first tried to stop him through the head of Banu Hashim.

Abu Talib refused to hand him over.

Quraysh then raised the level of its threats.

Then they offered to replace Muhammad with another young man, and Abu Talib rejected the offer.

When those methods failed, direct pressure began to fall on the Muslims themselves, while Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib gathered around the Prophet to protect him, even though not all of them believed in his message.

The first attempt to close the path of the call through negotiation had failed.

The confrontation in Makkah was entering a harsher stage.
