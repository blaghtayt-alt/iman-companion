Chapter Eight

In Secrecy: The Vigilance of the Early Muslims and the Spreading News

After meeting a number of the earliest men and women to embrace Islam, we come to a question that can seem puzzling at first. Some Companions described themselves as being among the very first Muslims in ways that do not appear to match the names already mentioned.

Sa‘d ibn Abi Waqqas, may Allah be pleased with him, said: “I remained for seven days as one third of the then extant Muslims.” — Sahih al-Bukhari 3727 (excerpt), published English translation on Sunnah.com.

‘Amr ibn ‘Abasah, may Allah be pleased with him, also related words from which he understood himself, at one point, to be one of four Muslims. Similar expressions are reported from other early believers.

How can that be, when we have already encountered Khadijah, ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib, Zayd ibn Harithah, Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, may Allah be pleased with them, and others who entered Islam after them?

The key is the nature of that stage itself. Many Muslims in the earliest years concealed their Islam. A man could know that he himself had believed without knowing that people around him had already believed before him. There was no public list of believers known to everyone. Knowing that a particular person had accepted Islam could be highly sensitive, because disclosure could expose that person to harm from family or tribe.

This does not mean that the people of Makkah had heard nothing about Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, or the new religion. They might hear that Muhammad was saying something new or calling people to worship Allah alone. But hearing of the message was one thing; knowing who had secretly believed in him was another.

For that reason, statements such as “I was the third” or “I was the fourth” do not necessarily function as an exact census of every Muslim in Makkah. They may describe what the speaker knew at that moment. Scholars have pointed to this when reconciling the reports.

A clear example is the report of ‘Amr ibn ‘Abasah. In his first meeting with the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, he asked about the state of the call. When ‘Amr asked who was with him in this, the Prophet told him that a free man and a slave were with him. ‘Amr identified Abu Bakr and Bilal, may Allah be pleased with them, among those with him at that time. Yet others had already accepted Islam as well. This shows how much secrecy surrounded the earliest believers.

Vigilance Was Not Limited to the Beginning

This care in protecting information did not disappear when the earliest Makkan years ended. Later in the Seerah it appears in different forms according to the circumstances.

When the Hijrah begins, information about who will leave, when they will leave, and where they will go will require caution. After the Muslim community is established in Madinah, information about military movements and destinations will likewise become important information that is not disclosed without need.

Ka‘b ibn Malik, may Allah be pleased with him, reported that when the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, intended an expedition, he would often use indirect wording that did not reveal his actual destination, so that useful information would not reach the wrong people. Tabuk was an exception because the journey was distant, the heat severe, and the enemy numerous; people needed to know the destination in order to prepare adequately. — Sahih al-Bukhari 2947–2948.

Before Badr, the Prophet sent Busaysah, may Allah be pleased with him, as a scout to obtain news about Abu Sufyan’s caravan. Those events will be told in detail when the Seerah reaches them.

The Qur’an later also emphasized vigilance and caution even during prayer in the presence of an enemy. In the verse of the prayer of fear, Allah commands: “[…] keeping vigilant and carrying their arms.” Later in the verse, He commands: “[…] but take all precautions.” — Qur’an 4:102 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

One of the clearest later examples occurred shortly before the Conquest of Makkah. The Prophet wanted the movement toward Makkah to remain undisclosed, but Hatib ibn Abi Balta‘ah, may Allah be pleased with him, sent a letter to people in Makkah containing information about the Messenger of Allah’s plans.

Ibn Ishaq reports that the Prophet learned of the letter through revelation. He sent ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib, al-Zubayr and al-Miqdad, may Allah be pleased with them, who caught up with the woman carrying it and recovered the message.

When the Prophet asked Hatib why he had done this, Hatib explained that he had not acted out of disbelief or rejection of Islam. Rather, he feared for his family in Makkah, because unlike many of the emigrants he did not have strong relatives there to protect them. The Prophet accepted his explanation and did not treat him as a hypocrite, while the act itself remained serious.

Concerning this incident, Allah revealed: “O you who believe, do not take My enemies and your enemies as allies, offering them friendship even though they rejected the truth that has come to you […]” — Qur’an 60:1 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

We will return to Hatib’s full story when the Seerah reaches the Conquest of Makkah. What matters here is that the vigilance learned in the earliest Makkan period continued to appear, in different forms, in later stages.

A Young Shepherd

Among those who entered Islam in those early years was ‘Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud, may Allah be pleased with him.

At the time he was a young shepherd tending sheep belonging to ‘Uqbah ibn Abi Mu‘ayt. One day the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, may Allah be pleased with him, passed by having fled from the polytheists.

They asked him for milk. Ibn Mas‘ud explained that the animals had been entrusted to him and that he could not give them their milk. The animals were not his property, so he did not consider himself free to give away their milk without the owner’s permission.

The Prophet then asked whether he had a young ewe that had not yet been mated and therefore would not normally be expected to have milk. Ibn Mas‘ud brought one.

The Prophet rubbed its udder and supplicated, and it filled with milk. Abu Bakr brought a vessel; it was milked, and they all drank. Then the Prophet said to the udder, “Shrink,” and it returned to its former state.

The story did not end with that encounter. The account relates that Ibn Mas‘ud later returned to ask the Prophet to teach him the Qur’an. The Prophet praised him: “[…] you are a learned boy.” This praise refers to the goodness and sound judgment Allah had granted him. Years later that young shepherd became one of the most famous Companions in recitation and knowledge of the Qur’an. He reported that he had learned seventy surahs directly from the Messenger of Allah, and that no one disputed this. — Musnad Ahmad 4412; Sahih Ibn Hibban 6504. Published English excerpts: For the Seeker of Truth, “‘Abdullaah ibn Mas’oud,” section “Story of his acceptance of Islam,” account attributed to Ahmad; the praise is an excerpt from the parallel wording quoted there. Other dialogue is reported indirectly. Explanation of the praise: Qawwam al-Sunnah, Dala’il al-Nubuwwah, report 39, pp. 58–59.

A Man from Outside Makkah Comes Searching

Not all of the earliest Muslims were Makkans.

‘Amr ibn ‘Abasah al-Sulami, may Allah be pleased with him, lived far from Makkah. He said of his pre-Islamic condition that he regarded his people as being upon error because of their worship of idols.

Then he heard that a man had appeared in Makkah bringing remarkable news, so he mounted his riding animal and went to him.

‘Amr arrived while the Messenger of Allah was in hiding because of the hostility of his people. He managed to reach him and asked who he was. The Prophet replied, “I am a Prophet (of Allah).” ‘Amr asked what a prophet was, and the Prophet explained that Allah had sent him. When ‘Amr asked what he had been sent with, the Prophet explained that he had been sent to maintain family ties, break idols, proclaim the oneness of Allah, and associate nothing with Him.

‘Amr then asked who was with him. The Prophet replied, “A free man and a slave.” Abu Bakr and Bilal were among those who had believed.

‘Amr wanted to stay, but the Prophet told him that the circumstances did not permit it then. He instructed him to return to his family and come back when he heard that the Prophet’s cause had prevailed.

So ‘Amr returned to his people.

Years passed while he followed the Prophet’s news. When he heard that the Messenger of Allah had migrated to Madinah, he questioned people arriving from there until he confirmed it, then traveled to Madinah himself.

He entered upon the Prophet and asked whether he recognized him. The Prophet replied, “Yes, you are the same man who met me at Mecca.”

The man who had once come to Makkah during the period of secrecy thus met the Prophet again after he had a home and a community in Madinah.

‘Amr later asked about prayer and ablution and preserved a long hadith from him. When he narrated it in old age, Abu Umamah was surprised at the extent of the reward he mentioned. ‘Amr explained that he had not heard it only once or twice, but many times from the Prophet. — Sahih Muslim 832. The quoted replies are excerpts from the published English translation on Sunnah.com; the remaining dialogue is reported indirectly.

His story itself shows why his description of being among the very first Muslims cannot simply be treated as a comprehensive count of everyone who had accepted Islam in Makkah. He was a visitor from outside the city, arriving at a time when many Muslims concealed their faith.

Abu Dharr Searches for the News Himself

Among the best-known men who came to Makkah searching for the Prophet was Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, may Allah be pleased with him.

His conversion is reported through more than one authentic account. Some details differ between the reports, so they should not all be fused into one story as though they were one identical narration. One of the longest accounts is Abu Dharr’s own report in Sahih Muslim.

Abu Dharr belonged to the tribe of Ghifar. He mentioned that his people did not revere the sacred months in the way other Arabs did. Before meeting the Prophet, he had already turned away from the forms of worship he saw around him, and said that he had prayed to Allah for about three years before meeting the Messenger of Allah, facing whatever direction Allah directed him.

He had a brother named Unays. Unays went to Makkah on an errand and heard about the man who had appeared there. When he returned, he told Abu Dharr that he had met a man claiming that Allah had sent him and that his religion was close to what Abu Dharr had been seeking.

Abu Dharr asked what people said about him. Unays said that they called him a poet, a soothsayer, and a magician.

Unays knew poetry. He said that he compared Muhammad’s speech with poetry and did not find it to be poetry, and he knew the speech of soothsayers and did not find it to be like theirs. He concluded that Muhammad was truthful and those accusing him were lying.

Abu Dharr was not satisfied with second-hand information. He decided to go to Makkah himself.

When he arrived, he did not know the Prophet and asking openly about him was dangerous. He asked a Makkan about the man whom people called “the Sabian,” a label the polytheists used for someone they regarded as having left their religion. The man called people against him, and Abu Dharr was beaten with stones and bones until he fell unconscious.

When he regained consciousness, he went to Zamzam, washed the blood from himself, and drank its water.

In his report Abu Dharr says that he remained in Makkah for a long period with no food except Zamzam water, until his body filled out and he did not feel the pain of hunger.

One night he saw two women circumambulating and calling upon the idols Isaf and Na’ilah, two idols worshipped in Makkah. He sharply rejected what they were doing, and they went away complaining about him.

At that time the Messenger of Allah came with Abu Bakr. After the Prophet prayed, Abu Dharr approached him and greeted him. The Prophet returned his greeting and asked where he was from. Abu Dharr explained that he belonged to the tribe of Ghifar.

The Prophet then asked how long he had been in Makkah. Abu Dharr said about thirty days and nights. When asked who had fed him, he replied that he had eaten nothing except Zamzam water.

The Prophet said of Zamzam, “It is blessed (water) and it also serves as food.”

Abu Bakr then hosted him and gave him raisins from Ta’if, the first food Abu Dharr had eaten there after that period.

The Prophet also told him that he had been shown a land of palm trees, which he thought was Yathrib, the city to which he would later migrate. He instructed Abu Dharr to return to his people and convey the message to them, so that Allah might benefit them through him.

When Abu Dharr returned, Unays asked what he had done. Abu Dharr told him that he had accepted Islam and believed the Messenger of Allah. Unays said that he had no desire to turn away from his brother’s religion, and he too accepted Islam. They then told their mother; she likewise accepted their religion and became Muslim.

Abu Dharr began calling his people, Ghifar, to Islam. Some of them accepted Islam before the Prophet’s migration and others after his arrival in Madinah. People from the tribe of Aslam also entered Islam. The Prophet said, “Ghifar (is a tribe) to whom Allah granted pardon, and Aslam (is the tribe) to whom Allah granted safety.” — Sahih Muslim 2473, 2514. Published English excerpts: Sunnah.com, Muslim 2473a for Zamzam and Muslim 2514a for Ghifar and Aslam. The other dialogue is reported indirectly.

There is another authentic account of Abu Dharr’s conversion narrated by Ibn ‘Abbas. In it, ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib noticed that Abu Dharr was a stranger in Makkah, hosted him for several days, and then carefully led him to the Prophet. After accepting Islam, Abu Dharr publicly declared the two testimonies of faith at the Ka‘bah. The polytheists beat him until al-‘Abbas intervened, reminding Quraysh that their trade route to Syria passed through the territory of Ghifar. Abu Dharr returned the next day and did the same again. — Sahih al-Bukhari 3861; Sahih Muslim 2474.

The two accounts differ in some details and in their sequence of events. Scholars have offered different explanations for these differences. Each narration has its own context; their details do not all belong to a single agreed sequence.

Who Accepted Islam, and Who Made It Public?

Other reports help us understand the period more clearly.

‘Ammar ibn Yasir, may Allah be pleased with him, said that he saw the Messenger of Allah when with him there were only five slaves, two women, and Abu Bakr. — Sahih al-Bukhari 3660.

That does not necessarily mean there were only eight Muslims in all Makkah at that moment. ‘Ammar is describing those he saw openly with the Prophet; others may already have accepted Islam while concealing it.

The distinction becomes even clearer in a report from ‘Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud, who said that the first seven to make their Islam public were the Messenger of Allah, Abu Bakr, ‘Ammar, his mother Sumayyah, Suhayb, Bilal, and al-Miqdad.

He then mentioned that Allah protected the Messenger through his uncle Abu Talib and that Abu Bakr had the protection of his people, while a number of the others were seized and tortured by the polytheists.

Bilal was among those severely tortured while repeating, “One, One,” affirming that Allah alone is God. — Sunan Ibn Majah 150; Musnad Ahmad 3832. The quoted words follow the published English translation of Ibn Majah 150 on Sunnah.com; the surrounding account is narrated indirectly.

The important phrase is “made their Islam public.”

There is a difference between accepting Islam and having people know about it and announcing it publicly. This distinction explains much of the apparent disagreement in reports about the earliest Muslims. A person might have accepted Islam while others did not know it; someone might know of two or three believers while being unaware of many others.

The News Was No Longer Hidden

As time passed, the number entering Islam grew.

The books of Seerah report from Ibn Ishaq that men and women continued entering Islam until news of it spread throughout Makkah and people began talking about it.

The message was no longer something no one had heard of. People were talking about it and hearing news of Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, and those who believed in him, even though aspects of Muslim activity and the identities of some believers were still kept from public view.

Ibn Ishaq also reports, according to what reached him, that in the beginning the Messenger of Allah concealed part of his call for a period, after which another stage came in which he was commanded to proclaim openly what had come from his Lord.

“Then proclaim what you are commanded, and turn away from those who associate partners with Allah.” — Qur’an 15:94 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

“And warn [starting with] the nearest kinsfolk,” — Qur’an 26:214 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

Here a new stage begins.

In the first years the question had often been: Who has believed, and how can that faith remain undisclosed?

Soon Quraysh would have to hear the call from the Messenger of Allah himself, openly.

With that, another kind of confrontation would begin.
