Chapter Eighteen

When the Vast Earth Offers a Way to Preserve Faith

Understanding Hijrah from Makkah to Abyssinia

1. Ja'far, who lived with the emigrants far from Makkah

In the previous chapter, we saw Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, may Allah be pleased with him, standing before the Negus and speaking on behalf of the Muslims.

His selection for that role was not accidental.

Ja'far lived for years with the emigrants in Abyssinia. There, his wife Asma' bint 'Umays, may Allah be pleased with her, gave birth to their sons: 'Abdullah, Muhammad, and 'Awn.

He was also known for generosity and kindness toward those in need.

Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, said:

“And the most generous of all the people to the poor was Ja`far bin Abi Talib.”

He then described how Ja'far would take poor people with him and feed them from whatever food he had at home.

— Sahih al-Bukhari, 3708 (excerpt; published translation on Sunnah.com).

Years later, the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, would say to Ja'far:

“You resemble me in appearance and character.”

That is, you resemble me in both physical form and manners.

— Sahih al-Bukhari, 4251 (excerpt; published translation on Sunnah.com).

Later in the Seerah, Ja‘far’s story will return at the Battle of Mu’tah, where both his hands were severed while he carried the banner. He became known as the one with two wings.

But at this point, Ja'far was still in Abyssinia, leading a group of Muslims living far from the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him.

This raises a question beyond Ja‘far’s own story: why did these Muslims leave Makkah, the Prophet’s homeland and the home of the Ka‘bah? How did leaving it for another land become the right course for some believers?

2. Hijrah is not tied only to the sacredness of a place

Makkah was the most beloved of lands to the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and it contained the Sacred House.

But the honor of a place does not mean that a person must remain there regardless of the oppression he suffers, even if he becomes unable to worship Allah.

Allah says:

O My believing slaves, My earth is spacious, so worship Me alone.

— Surat al-'Ankabut: 56.

The whole earth belongs to Allah, and worshipping Him is not tied to one place. Someone who cannot worship Allah where they live, and is not required to remain there, may seek a place where they can practice their faith.

The migration to Abyssinia embodied this principle. The Muslims did not seek a land more sacred than Makkah, but a place where they could worship Allah more safely. Preserving their faith took priority.

3. Not every environment is the same

The Muslims at that time faced very different environments.

In Makkah, Quraysh fought the call to Islam, beat some Muslims, and tried to force them away from their religion.

In Abyssinia, the people followed another religion, but their king gave the Muslims safety and did not force them to abandon Islam.

Years later, in Madinah, a Muslim community would arise with the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, living among his Companions, and Madinah would become the land of migration and support.

This distinction helps explain Hijrah. The name of a country or the religion of its ruler is not the only consideration; people must also consider whether they can worship Allah and preserve their faith there. Abyssinia was not a Muslim society, yet at that stage it offered the emigrants greater religious security than Makkah.

Umm Salamah, may Allah be pleased with her, said of the Negus:

“[…] we felt safe to practise our religion and we worshipped Allah without being bothered or hearing anything we disliked.” — Musnad Ahmad, 1740, excerpt from Umm Salamah’s account. Translation: Nasiruddin al-Khattab, edited by Huda al-Khattab, Darussalam, 2012, vol. 2, p. 179.

So moving to Abyssinia was not a move from a non-Muslim country to a Muslim country.

It was a move from a place where believers were pressured in their religion to a place where they could live their faith with greater security.

4. When can staying itself become dangerous?

Years after this migration, verses in Surat al-Nisa' would describe people who believed but remained among the polytheists even though they were able to leave.

Allah says:

When the angels take the souls of those who have wronged themselves, they will say, “What was the matter with you?” They will say, “We were oppressed in the land.” They will say, “Was not Allah’s earth spacious enough for you to migrate?” They are those whose refuge will be Hell – what a terrible destination!

— Surat al-Nisa': 97.

Ibn ‘Abbas, may Allah be pleased with them both, reported that some Muslims were among the polytheists, adding to their numbers against the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. One of them might be killed by an arrow or by a blow in battle.

Then this verse was revealed.

— Sahih al-Bukhari: 4596.

The danger concerned more than a person’s inward beliefs. Someone may be Muslim, yet remaining in a particular place can put them in a position they would not choose. In the report just mentioned, remaining among the polytheists placed these Muslims in their ranks when they faced the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.

That is why the Qur'anic question was:

Was not Allah’s earth spacious enough for you to migrate?

5. But not everyone who stayed was able to leave

The Qur'anic passage did not end with that verse.

Allah immediately exempted those who truly could not leave.

He says:

Except the helpless men, women, and children who have no means nor do they know where to go.
They are those whom Allah may pardon, for Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.

— Surat al-Nisa': 98–99.

Here, the exemption concerns people who lack the strength or means to escape harm.

The verse then explains this weakness in two expressions:

who have no means

That is, they do not possess a real way to get out.

And:

nor do they know where to go.

That is, they do not know a route by which they can reach a safe place.

A person may be physically healthy but imprisoned, know the route but be unable to escape those guarding them, or be too ill to endure the journey. A woman or child may have no means of travelling independently. The Qur’an therefore distinguishes between those who can leave and those who cannot.

6. «We were oppressed in the land.» is not just a phrase

There is an important distinction within the verses themselves.

The first group said:

We were oppressed in the land.

But the angels did not accept their excuse because they were actually able to migrate.

The following verse then excused:

Except the helpless men, women, and children who have no means nor do they know where to go.

Not everyone who describes themselves as unable is truly unable. Travel may be difficult, and leaving home, relatives or possessions may be painful, but a difficult decision is not always the same as genuine inability. Equally, someone who truly cannot leave must not be burdened beyond their capacity. The Qur’an itself distinguishes between these situations.

7. The Messenger of Allah did not forget those who could not leave

Some Muslims remained in Makkah because they were unable to migrate.

The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, did not treat them as if they had simply chosen to stay despite being able to leave.

Rather, he prayed to Allah for them.

Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, reported that the Prophet ﷺ made this supplication in qunut, a supplication offered during prayer:

He asked Allah to rescue Salamah ibn Hisham, al-Walid ibn al-Walid, and ‘Ayyash ibn Abi Rabi‘ah, and to rescue the oppressed believers.

— Sahih al-Bukhari, 2932 (meaning of the supplication).

These people were believers.

But they could not leave as others had.

That is why the Prophet prayed:

“O Allah, save the oppressed believers.” — Published excerpt: HadeethEnc, 8297.

Hijrah was not a test in which the strong succeeded while every helpless person was blamed.

Responsibility was connected to ability.

8. Why was Abyssinia a valid solution?

The emigrants to Abyssinia—including ‘Uthman and Ruqayyah, Abu Salamah and Umm Salamah, al-Zubayr, Mus‘ab and Ja‘far, may Allah be pleased with them—found religious security and justice there. They had not gone to a Muslim country, nor had the Negus publicly declared Islam when they arrived. Yet religious security and justice made Abyssinia a suitable refuge.

If a person cannot practice his religion in one place and finds another place where he can, migrating there can become a means of preserving faith.

This explains why Abyssinia was a suitable refuge, even though the Muslim community later led by the Prophet ﷺ would be established in Madinah.

Their immediate need was to preserve their faith and find a place where they could pray and worship Allah beyond the reach of those torturing them to make them abandon Islam.

9. Why did the Prophet not leave with them?

If Abyssinia offered safety, why did the Messenger of Allah ﷺ remain in Makkah rather than migrate there with his Companions?

The circumstances of all Muslims were not the same.

The Messenger of Allah had protection from Banu Hashim, and Abu Talib defended him.

The call to Islam itself was still active in Makkah, and the Prophet was the one receiving revelation and conveying it to the people.

Some of his Companions, however, did not have the same protection.

For that reason, the solution suitable for an oppressed believer was not necessarily the same solution suitable for the Messenger of Allah at that time.

Those who needed to leave did so.

Those who could remain, and had protection allowing them to continue their mission, stayed.

This shows that Hijrah was not one identical decision applied to every person in the same way.

10. Some people may need to migrate while others do not

Quraysh did not harm all Muslims in the same way.

A man protected by his tribe was not like an enslaved person whose master could drag him out onto the burning ground and torture him.

A person able to openly show his Islam was not like someone prevented from prayer and beaten until he abandoned his religion.

So leaving could become necessary for one group while others were still able to remain.

That is what happened when some Muslims migrated to Abyssinia. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ, Abu Bakr and other Companions remained in Makkah, while others left. The ruling on migration depended not only on the place but also on each person’s circumstances and what they could do there.

11. A non-Muslim land that does not fight you

The story of Abyssinia reveals another important distinction.

Makkah at that time was controlled by people fighting the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and pressuring those who believed in him.

Abyssinia was ruled by a king who did not initially follow Islam, yet he protected the Muslims rather than fighting them. After hearing their account of their faith, he granted them safety and refused Quraysh’s request to return them. Societies that differ from Muslims in religion cannot all be treated alike: some persecute them and obstruct their worship, while others allow them to practice their faith. This distinction between Makkah and Abyssinia made a real difference to the emigrants’ lives, and some remained in Abyssinia for years.

12. Later, a Muslim community would be established in Madinah

Years later, the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, would migrate to Madinah.

A Muslim community would then be established there.

Yet not everyone in Abyssinia returned immediately.

Ja'far, may Allah be pleased with him, and a group of Muslims remained there until the seventh year after the Hijrah, then came to the Messenger of Allah around the time of Khaybar.

This detail matters.

The existence of a Muslim community in Madinah did not mean that Ja'far and those with him were condemned for remaining in Abyssinia.

They were living in a land where they could preserve their religion under a just king.

So they remained until the time came for their return.

This does not mean that every person in every age can directly compare his or her situation to Abyssinia. Conditions differ from one person and country to another.

But the story of Abyssinia itself establishes a clear meaning:

A believer may find greater security for faith in a land whose people differ from him in religion than in another land that persecutes him and prevents him from worship.

13. The Negus himself faced another kind of limitation

The Negus’s own circumstances illustrate another aspect of this question. He was the king of Abyssinia, not an emigrant from Makkah. According to the Seerah accounts, he accepted Islam after learning what the Messenger of Allah ﷺ had brought.

An authentic report relates that when the Negus died, the Prophet ﷺ informed his Companions and led them in the funeral prayer for him in his absence. — Sahih al-Bukhari, 1245.

But the Negus ruled a people most of whom had not entered Islam.

As we saw in the previous chapter, some of his senior religious leaders objected even when he spoke about Jesus, peace be upon him, in a way consistent with Islam.

Being king did not mean that he could change everything in his country in a single moment.

Scholars therefore cited his circumstances to illustrate that people are accountable according to their ability.

Allah says:

Allah does not burden any soul greater than it can bear.

— Surat al-Baqarah: 286.

Religious responsibility is inseparable from ability, as the situation of the oppressed believers in Makkah also shows. Someone able to leave is not in the same position as someone unable to do so. The same distinction applies to the freedom to practice one’s faith openly and to the extent of the power a person possesses.

14. Hijrah in Surat al-Nahl

Among the surahs containing meanings strongly connected to this stage is Surat al-Nahl.

Allah says:

Those who migrated for the sake of Allah after being oppressed, We will surely give them good in this world. But the reward of the Hereafter is greater, if only they knew,
those who endured patiently and put their trust in their Lord.

— Surat al-Nahl: 41–42.

It is reported from Qatadah, may Allah have mercy on him, in his explanation of this verse that he connected it to those whom the people of Makkah oppressed, after which some of them went out to Abyssinia.

This is an interpretation from a Successor, a Muslim from the generation after the Companions. It is not a prophetic hadith establishing with certainty that this event was the specific reason for the verse’s revelation.

The verse brings together the experiences seen in this migration: the believers suffered oppression, emigrated for Allah’s sake, endured patiently and placed their trust in their Lord. Hijrah was more than a move from one city to another; it meant leaving something they loved to preserve something greater.

15. The surah also mentions the person who is coerced

The same surah also speaks of a Muslim who is tortured and forced to utter words contrary to his faith.

Allah says:

Whoever disbelieves in Allah after having believed – except one who is compelled while his heart is firm in faith – but those who embrace disbelief willingly, upon them will be the wrath of Allah, and for them there will be a great punishment.

— Surat al-Nahl: 106.

Then Allah says later:

As for those who emigrated after being persecuted, then strove [for the cause of Allah], and were patient – indeed, your Lord is after that All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

— Surat al-Nahl: 110.

The persecution here consisted of pressure and torture intended to make them abandon their faith.

The surah thus addresses experiences the believers faced in Makkah: torture, coercion to utter words contrary to their faith, departure from their homeland and patient endurance of harm. The Qur’an spoke directly to the experiences of the Muslim community.

16. Surat al-Nahl also addresses Quraysh

Surat al-Nahl also addresses Quraysh, reminding them of Allah’s blessings.

Allah says:

If you try to count Allah’s favors, you will not be able to enumerate them. Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

— Surat al-Nahl: 18.

The surah then challenges the worship of beings that cannot create:

Is then He Who creates equal to one who cannot create? Will you not then take heed?

— Surat al-Nahl: 17.

It records what they said about the Qur'an:

When it is said to them, “What has your Lord sent down?” They say, “Ancient fables!”

— Surat al-Nahl: 24.

It answers their attempt to use divine decree as an excuse for their polytheism:

Those who associate partners with Allah say, “If Allah had willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshiped anything besides Him”

— Surat al-Nahl: 35.

Then it returns the issue to its foundation:

Indeed, We sent to every community a messenger, [saying], “Worship Allah and shun false gods.”

— Surat al-Nahl: 36.

So while some believers were leaving Makkah to preserve their religion, the Qur'an continued addressing those who remained there and answering the arguments of the polytheists one after another.

17. Ibrahim, to whom Quraysh traced its heritage

Near the end of Surat al-Nahl comes mention of Ibrahim, peace be upon him.

Quraysh took pride in being connected to the House built by Ibrahim and Isma'il, peace be upon them.

But the Qur'an does not emphasize Ibrahim's lineage alone.

Allah says:

Abraham was a model of excellence, devoted to Allah and inclined to true faith, and he was not one of those who associate partners with Allah.
He was grateful for His favors; He chose him and guided him to a straight path.

— Surat al-Nahl: 120–121.

The devotion described here means turning away from polytheism and worshipping Allah alone.

Then Allah says to His Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him:

Then We revealed to you [O Prophet] to follow the religion of Abraham, inclining to true faith, and he was not one of those who associate partners with Allah.

— Surat al-Nahl: 123.

Quraysh claimed a connection to Ibrahim, yet he was not a polytheist. The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to follow his way of worshipping Allah alone. The discussion thus returns to the central question in Makkah: who truly follows Ibrahim’s religion?

18. «Call to the way of your Lord»

The surah then closes by directing the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, in the path of his call:

Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and goodly exhortation, and reason with them in the best manner.

— Surat al-Nahl: 125.

Then:

And be patient, for your patience comes only from Allah; do not grieve over them, nor be distressed by their evil plots.
Indeed, Allah is with those who fear Him and those who do good.

— Surat al-Nahl: 127–128.

Some Companions had left Makkah, others remained there under oppression, and still others were able to stay and endure. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ continued calling his people to faith.

Qur’anic revelation addressed what the Prophet ﷺ faced from his people: it recounted their words, answered their objections, and directed him to remain patient:

And be patient, for your patience comes only from Allah

19. What does Hijrah mean in the story of Abyssinia?

When we now return to 'Uthman and Ruqayyah, Ja'far and Asma', Umm Salamah (may Allah be pleased with her) and Abu Salamah, and the other emigrants, their story becomes clearer.

The emigrants had not ceased to love Makkah, nor were they seeking greater wealth or moving to a country that had become Muslim. They left because their faith was threatened and they had found a land where they could worship Allah.

The migration to Abyssinia shows why a person may leave a beloved place to preserve something greater. Homeland, family and home are precious, but faith is not to be sacrificed simply to remain in familiar surroundings.

At the same time, Allah does not demand the impossible from those who are genuinely unable. Those who can act bear responsibility according to their ability; those who truly cannot are excused. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ prayed for the oppressed believers to be rescued.

Through these different circumstances—ability and inability, Makkah and Abyssinia—the Muslim community was learning that the earth belongs to Allah. The aim was not simply to stay or to leave, but to worship Him and preserve faith as far as one was able.
