Chapter Nineteen

Names with Lives Behind Them

The Remaining Emigrants to Abyssinia and Some of Their Stories

1. Khabbab ibn al-Aratt: A Name from the Earliest Days

Before continuing with the emigrants to Abyssinia, we turn to Khabbab ibn al-Aratt, may Allah be pleased with him, one of the earliest men to embrace Islam in Makkah.

Khabbab was taken captive and sold during the pre-Islamic period. He eventually reached Makkah, where Umm Anmar al-Khuza’iyyah purchased him. He was also an ally of Banu Zuhrah, linked to them through protection and support rather than descent.

Reports differ over Khabbab’s place among the first converts. Some name him as the sixth person to accept Islam; others place his conversion after a larger number of believers. No conclusive report establishes his precise position, but the accounts place him among the earliest Muslims, when the community was still very small.

Accepting Islam then could cost a person his wealth, security and physical safety. The Muslims had no state, political power or large community to protect them, and Khabbab was among those who bore the consequences.

2. The Scars of Fire Remained on His Back

Khabbab was one of the vulnerable believers who lacked the tribal or social protection needed to restrain an aggressor.

Early biographical works and collections arranged by generation preserve accounts of the severe torture he endured.

One such report says that during the caliphate of Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, Khabbab entered upon him. Umar seated him close to himself and honored him because he knew his early service and suffering.

Khabbab spoke about what had happened to him in Makkah, then uncovered his back, revealing old marks left by fire and torture.

These details appear in biographical works and collections arranged by generation; not every wording has the standing of an authentic hadith.

But the basic fact that Khabbab was tortured severely is well known and firmly established. We previously encountered his authentic report when he came to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, while he was in the shade of the Ka'bah and said:

“Will you not supplicate for our victory (over our opponents)? Will you not invoke Allah for us?” [Excerpt: Sahih al-Bukhari 3612; published translation: HadeethEnc 4246.]

Khabbab was not speaking of minor discomfort. He had endured physical torture intended to force a believer to abandon his faith.

3. A Blacksmith Asking for His Wages

Khabbab was a blacksmith who made iron tools and weapons.

Khabbab did work for al-As ibn Wa’il al-Sahmi, a leading man of Quraysh, who owed him payment. When Khabbab asked for his wages, al-As told him that he would not pay unless Khabbab rejected Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him.

This was more than a theoretical dispute: Khabbab had worked with his own hands, and his wages were being withheld to pressure him into abandoning his faith.

Khabbab replied that he would never deny Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him. Mentioning al-As’s death and resurrection emphasized his permanent refusal; it did not set a time after which he would abandon his faith. — Al-Qastallani, Irshad al-Sari, commentary on Sahih al-Bukhari 2275.

Al-As denied the resurrection. Mocking Khabbab, he told him to wait until he died and was resurrected, claiming that he would then be given wealth and children and would repay his debt. — Indirect account of the report in Sahih al-Bukhari 2091.

Then Allah revealed:

Have you seen the one who rejects Our verses and says, “I will surely be given wealth and children [if I am resurrected]”?
Has he obtained knowledge of the unseen, or has he taken a pledge with the Most Compassionate?
Never! We will write down what he says and will prolong his punishment extensively.
We will inherit what he boasts of, and he will come to Us all alone.

— Qur’an 19:77–80 — Rowwad Translation Center

The basis of this incident from Khabbab is authentically established in Sahih al-Bukhari.

The Qur’an thus preserves an episode from the life of a man whose name might otherwise pass quickly in a Seerah list: a Muslim blacksmith asking for his earnings, a leader of Quraysh making payment conditional on disbelief, and Khabbab refusing.

4. Years Later, His Life Had Changed

Khabbab lived long enough to see Islam move from weakness in Makkah to a time when the Muslims had a state and wealth.

Reports mention that toward the end of his life he would look at the wealth he had acquired and fear that some of his good things had already been given to him in this world.

He later settled in Kufah and, according to the well-known view in the biographical works, died there in the year 37 AH.

Much separated the Khabbab who had no one to stop his tormentors in Makkah from the man who lived to witness changes that the persecuted early believers could scarcely have imagined. Yet here his name remains tied to the beginning, when he stood firm among a small community of Muslims.

5. Why Return to Lists of Emigrants?

We now return to the emigrants to Abyssinia. Their lists may seem long, but passing over names such as Uthman ibn Maz’un, Hisham ibn al-As, Adi ibn Nadlah, Abu Sabrah and Suhayl ibn Bayda’ without learning about their lives reduces the Seerah to a register of unfamiliar names.

Behind each name was someone who left Makkah when migration itself was dangerous. Some returned and witnessed Badr; some were imprisoned in Makkah and prevented from reaching Madinah; some died in Abyssinia; and others lived to take part in events after the death of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him. Their lists belong to the story because their lives belong to it.

6. From Banu Jumah: The Family of Uthman ibn Maz'un

Many emigrants to Abyssinia came from Banu Jumah, a branch of Quraysh. Among the best known was Uthman ibn Maz’un, may Allah be pleased with him. His son al-Sa’ib and his brothers Qudamah and Abd Allah ibn Maz’un accompanied him: three brothers and the son of one of them from a single household.

From Banu Jumah as well was Hatib ibn al-Harith, accompanied by his wife Fatimah bint al-Mujallal and their two sons, Muhammad and al-Harith.

His brother Hattab ibn al-Harith also left with his wife Fukayhah bint Yasar.

Ibn Ishaq also lists Sufyan ibn Mu‘ammar, with his sons Jabir and Junadah and his wife Hasanah, their mother. Sharhabil ibn Hasanah accompanied the family; in this account, he was the boys’ maternal half-brother.

Ibn Ishaq's list includes other names from Banu Jumah as well.

Migration was not always the departure of one man. Brothers, husbands and wives, children and sometimes whole families traveled together.

7. From Banu Sahm: Seven Brothers Left Together

Next come names from Banu Sahm, the same branch of Quraysh to which Amr ibn al-As belonged.

The emigrants from Banu Sahm included Khunays ibn Hudhafah, Abd Allah ibn Hudhafah, Hisham ibn al-As and several sons of al-Harith ibn Qays. The sons of al-Harith mentioned earlier were seven brothers who left one household for Abyssinia.

Sources differ over some of their names: one brother is called Bishr in some accounts and Tamim in others. Ibn Sa’d names Ma‘bad ibn al-Harith, then records Ma‘mar as an alternative name in another report. — Al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, al-Khanji edition, 4/181–183, entries 428–434; the Ma‘bad/Ma‘mar variant appears in entry 434.

The Seerah works preserve the picture of a family whose sons embraced Islam in large numbers and emigrated. Their later paths differed: some are reported to have been martyred at al-Yamamah, some were killed at al-Yarmuk, and some took part in the conquests of al-Sham. Abyssinia began a phase of their lives rather than ending their stories.

8. Hisham ibn al-As: Brother of the Man Quraysh Sent to Bring the Muslims Back

One notable emigrant from Banu Sahm was Hisham ibn al-As ibn Wa’il, may Allah be pleased with him, the brother of Amr ibn al-As. Amr had not yet accepted Islam. He was one of the two envoys Quraysh sent to the Negus to seek the emigrants’ return, while his own brother Hisham had become a Muslim and emigrated.

Their father, al-As ibn Wa'il, was the same man we encountered earlier in Khabbab's story, when he withheld Khabbab's wages and mocked the resurrection.

Hisham's mother was from Banu Makhzum and, according to genealogical works, was a sister of Abu Jahl.

Hisham grew up among relatives who were prominent opponents of the call, yet he accepted Islam early. Such differences recurred in Makkah: a father’s, uncle’s or brother’s position did not necessarily determine another family member’s choice.

9. From Abyssinia to Makkah... Then to Imprisonment

Hisham migrated to Abyssinia in the second migration.

Some biographical works relate that Hisham returned to Makkah after hearing that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, had migrated to Madinah, intending to join him.

Another account says his people prevented him from leaving before the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, migrated to Madinah.

But the journey was interrupted.

Biographical works state that his people detained him in Makkah and prevented him from migrating. Some also name his father among those responsible. Yet Ibn Hajar states that his father, al-As, died before the Hijrah, so the father’s involvement at that point remains uncertain.

Hisham thus endured successive hardships. He left Makkah for Abyssinia, returned hoping to join the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and was then detained and imprisoned. He reached the Prophet only years later.

In the previous chapter we saw that some Muslims remained in Makkah as genuinely helpless believers, and that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, prayed for several of them by name.

A man who remained because he did not wish to migrate was in a different position from one who tried to leave and was forcibly prevented.

10. Hisham Later: From Imprisonment to the Battlefields of al-Sham

Hisham eventually reached the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and witnessed the events that followed.

After the Prophet's death, he lived on and participated in the conquests of al-Sham.

The reports differ about the battle in which he was martyred. Some mention Ajnadayn, while another report from his brother Amr says that the two of them were together at al-Yarmuk, where they spent the night asking Allah to grant them martyrdom, and when morning came Hisham was granted martyrdom.

Biographical works do agree, however, that he fought in al-Sham and that his life ended there as a martyr according to the well-known reports.

Ibn Sa’d transmits from Zayd ibn Aslam that when Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, heard of Hisham’s death, he prayed for Allah’s mercy upon him and praised the great support he had given Islam. — Al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, biography of Hisham ibn al-As; the historical report is presented here in indirect form.

The name in the list of emigrants thus opens onto a whole life: early acceptance of Islam, migration, return and imprisonment, followed by joining the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, years of service and a final end on the battlefields of al-Sham.

11. His Brother Amr Remembers Him

The biographical collections preserve an account of people discussing which brother had greater merit, Hisham or Amr ibn al-As. Amr passed by and later learned what they had been discussing.

Amr told them that he and Hisham had been at al-Yarmuk and had spent the night asking Allah to grant them martyrdom. When morning came, Hisham was granted martyrdom, while Amr was not. He regarded this as evidence of his brother’s greater merit. — Ibn Sa’d, Al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, biography of Hisham ibn al-As, report of Abd Allah ibn Ubayd Allah ibn Umayr; the dialogue is presented here in indirect form.

The same report preserves another lesson.

Amr also noticed that they had kept the young men away from their gathering. He objected and urged them to make room for the young, welcome them, speak to them and help them understand what was being taught. Those who were young in their community today might soon be its elders, he explained. His own generation had once been young too, before becoming elders in turn. — Ibn Sa’d, Al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, biography of Hisham ibn al-As; continuation of the same report, presented in indirect form.

These reports come from biographical collections and should not all be given the authority of an authenticated hadith. They nevertheless convey how members of the Companions’ generation understood responsibility passing to those who came after them.

12. From Banu Adi: A Man Who Died in Abyssinia

The list then includes emigrants from Banu Adi, the branch to which Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, belonged.

They included Mu’ammar ibn Abd Allah, Urwah ibn Abd al-Uzza, Adi ibn Nadlah and his son al-Nu’man, and Amir ibn Rabi’ah with his wife Layla bint Abi Hathmah, may Allah be pleased with her. Amir and Layla have appeared earlier in this account.

The biographical collections describe Adi ibn Nadlah as an early Muslim who migrated to Abyssinia and died there. He neither returned to Makkah nor reached Madinah. His story shows that the emigrants did not know when they would return: some came back after months or years, while others spent the rest of their lives in the land of migration.

13. The Household of Suhayl ibn Amr: Five Emigrants from One Family

Among Banu Amir ibn Lu’ayy, the household of Suhayl ibn Amr stands out. Suhayl was then a prominent opponent of the call, although he later accepted Islam. Five of his close relatives left for Abyssinia: his daughter Umm Kulthum, wife of Abu Sabrah ibn Abi Ruhm; his son Abd Allah; and his three brothers Sulayt, al-Sakran and Hatib ibn Amr.

The head of the family’s opposition did not prevent his children and brothers from choosing another path.

14. Al-Sakran and Sawdah

Al-Sakran ibn Amr, may Allah be pleased with him, emigrated with his wife Sawdah bint Zam’ah, may Allah be pleased with her.

Sawdah (may Allah be pleased with her) would later become a wife of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, after the death of Khadijah, may Allah be pleased with her.

Reports differ over where al-Sakran died. Some place his death in Abyssinia; others say he returned to Makkah and died there before the migration to Madinah.

What matters at this stage is that Sawdah (may Allah be pleased with her) was among the women who experienced migration to Abyssinia before she became one of the Mothers of the Believers [the wives of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him].

Umm Salamah, Umm Habibah and Sawdah, may Allah be pleased with them, were thus three future wives of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, who had experienced migration to Abyssinia. They had known the hardships of the call and migration before entering his household.

15. What About Ubayd Allah ibn Jahsh?

Connected with Umm Habibah, may Allah be pleased with her, is a well-known report in the Seerah books about her first husband, Ubayd Allah ibn Jahsh, stating that he became Christian in Abyssinia and died there.

This report is widely circulated in works of Seerah and biography.

However, some researchers have questioned how firmly its details are established. Among the points they cite is the hadith of Abu Sufyan with Heraclius, in which Heraclius asked him about the followers of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him:

“Does anybody amongst those who embrace his religion become displeased and renounce the religion afterwards?”

Abu Sufyan answered:

“No”.

This answer alone does not necessarily invalidate every report of Ubayd Allah’s conversion to Christianity; neither are the details of the story established as an undisputed authentic account.

It is well known in the Seerah works that Ubayd Allah ibn Jahsh became Christian in Abyssinia, but the strength of the detailed reports about this is a matter of discussion.

16. Banu al-Harith ibn Fihr: Names Many People Do Not Know

Ibn Ishaq lists eight emigrants from Banu al-Harith ibn Fihr: Abu Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah, whose name was Amir ibn Abd Allah; Suhayl ibn Bayda’; Amr ibn Abi Sarh; Iyad ibn Zuhayr; Amr ibn al-Harith ibn Zuhayr; Uthman ibn Abd Ghanm; Sa’d ibn Abd Qays; and al-Harith ibn Abd Qays.

A reader may recognize Abu Ubaydah, may Allah be pleased with him, and Suhayl ibn Bayda’ while knowing little of the others. Yet all belonged to the early community that left Makkah to preserve its faith. These lists help us see how much larger the first generation was than the few famous names most often recalled.

17. How Many Were They?

After listing the male emigrants, Ibn Ishaq gives a total of eighty-three men who reached Abyssinia, provided that Ammar ibn Yasir, may Allah be pleased with him, is included. He expressed uncertainty about counting Ammar among them.

That total excludes the young children who traveled with their parents or were born in Abyssinia, and the lists also name women who emigrated. Differences over names and methods of counting prevent certainty about the totals. What is clear is that a community of dozens of Muslims from different clans and families had formed in Abyssinia, beyond the departure of a few individuals.

18. A Poet Among the Emigrants Sends His Voice Back to Makkah

Abd Allah ibn al-Harith ibn Qays, may Allah be pleased with him, was one of the emigrant brothers from Banu Sahm and a poet. Seerah works preserve verses he composed after the Muslims found safety in Abyssinia under the Negus’s protection.

He said:

O rider, carry my message far and wide to whoever seeks Allah’s message and His religion.

He addresses, in particular, the believers suffering in Makkah:

Every servant of Allah oppressed in the heart of Makkah, subjugated and put through trials.

Oppression here means injustice and pressure; the trials refer to harm inflicted on a person to force him to abandon his religion.

The message he asks the rider to convey is:

We have found Allah’s lands spacious, offering escape from humiliation, disgrace and degradation.

Disgrace here means the loss of dignity brought about by humiliation.

These words were not reflections composed centuries after the migration. Their author had left Makkah, reached Abyssinia and then addressed the believers still suffering persecution, reminding them of the spaciousness of Allah’s lands.

19. The Names Are No Longer Just Names

Names can blur together when the emigrants are read as a list. Learning their stories reveals the lives behind them.

Khabbab was a vulnerable blacksmith whose body bore the marks of torture.

Hisham ibn al-As came from the household of al-As ibn Wa’il and Amr ibn al-As, before Amr accepted Islam. Hisham emigrated, was imprisoned and struggled to join the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him.

Adi ibn Nadlah left Makkah and died in Abyssinia.

Al-Sakran and Sawdah emigrated as husband and wife. His life ended early, and Sawdah, may Allah be pleased with her, later became one of the Mothers of the Believers.

Five close relatives of Suhayl ibn Amr took the road of migration while the head of their family still opposed the call.

Eight men from Banu al-Harith belonged to that early community, although most of their names are unfamiliar to many readers today.

In his poetry, Abd Allah ibn al-Harith expressed what the spaciousness of Allah’s lands meant to the emigrants. It was an experience they had lived before it became a subject for later generations to discuss.

20. Even Makkah Could Be Left When Faith Was the Price

Al-Suhayli drew a lesson from their migration: the Muslims left Makkah despite its virtue and greatness, and they went to a land ruled by a Christian king rather than a Muslim state. They were nevertheless counted among the emigrants and early believers of distinction because they left to preserve their religion and found a place where they could worship their Lord without obstruction.

The previous chapter explained this meaning in greater detail; the emigrants’ lives now make it concrete. Migration meant homes left behind, husbands and wives traveling together, children born abroad, people dying before they could return, young men returning only to be imprisoned, and others living on to fight on distant battlefields years later.

The emigrants’ names are therefore an integral part of the Seerah. They belonged to the generation that supported the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, when that support could begin with a declaration of faith and steadfastness through all that followed.
