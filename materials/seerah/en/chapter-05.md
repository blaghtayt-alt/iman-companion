Chapter Five

Arise and Warn: When the Call Began

The previous chapter ended with Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, returning from the Cave of Hira carrying the first verses of the Qur’an. Waraqah ibn Nawfal then recognized that the one who had come to Muhammad was Gabriel, peace be upon him, the angel who brings Allah’s revelation to His prophets. After this beginning came what the reports call the “pause in revelation” — a period during which revelation stopped for a time after it had begun. No sound report fixes its exact length with certainty.

The beginning had taken place, but the message had not yet become a public call among the people. Then came the day when that period of waiting ended and a new stage began.

Jabir ibn ‘Abdullah, may Allah be pleased with him, reported that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was walking when he heard a voice from the sky. He looked up and saw the same angel who had come to him at Hira, seated on a chair between heaven and earth. The sight overwhelmed him with fear, so he hurried back to his family. He said, “Cover me!”

Then a new address was revealed. This time it did not begin with the word “Read,” but with a description of his state at that moment:

“O you covered [in your cloak], arise and warn, and proclaim the greatness of your Lord, and purify your garments, and shun idol worshiping.”

Qur’an 74:1–5 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

“Al-Muddaththir” means the one wrapped in his clothes. The movement in the words is clear: you who covered yourself after the fear, now it is time to rise. The stage was no longer only about receiving revelation privately; with it began the responsibility of delivering the message to other people.

The first command was: “arise and warn.” A warning is to alert someone to a danger before it reaches them. In the message of the prophets, this means warning people about the consequence of associating partners with Allah and disobeying Him, and calling them to worship Allah before they meet Him. The message was therefore not merely new information; it was news that required a person to change direction.

Then came the command to “proclaim the greatness of your Lord.” In other words, magnify your Lord and make Him greater in your heart than everything people glorify — idols, leaders, or tribes. This carried particular weight in Makkah, where many idols stood around the Ka‘bah and tribal identity had enormous power in daily life.

Allah also commanded him to “purify your garments.” Qur’anic commentators mentioned meanings connected to outward cleanliness as well as purity of conduct and character. The general lesson is that the bearer of a message does not call others to purity while neglecting himself; the one who calls others must first prepare himself before confronting society.

Then came the command to “shun idol worshiping.” The Arabic word al-rujz was explained as idols and, more broadly, what is filthy and false; to shun it means to leave it and keep away from it. In Makkah, this meant a clear separation from idol worship, which had become part of public life around the Ka‘bah.

In the next two opening verses came further training:

“And do not do a favor for a greater return, and be patient for the sake of your Lord.”

Qur’an 74:6–7 — Rowwad Translation Center, IslamHouse/QuranEnc.

The point is that giving or doing good should not become a way of seeking a greater return from people, and the bearer of the message should not regard what he offers as a favor for which others owe him repayment. He was then commanded to be patient for his Lord. The reason patience was mentioned from the beginning would soon become clear: a call that warns people and challenges deeply rooted beliefs would not be accepted easily by everyone.

These first commands after the resumption of revelation were therefore directed to Muhammad himself, peace and blessings be upon him: know your Lord, rise, warn, magnify your Lord, purify yourself, leave falsehood, do not make your work a search for repayment, and be patient. Revelation was training the bearer of the message before he was to train others.

From then on, revelation continued after the pause, and Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, began calling people to what he had been sent with.

But “the beginning of the call” does not mean that on the first day he gathered all of Makkah in one place. The call began among those who knew him closely and already knew his truthfulness and trustworthiness. The first person to believe in him was his wife Khadijah, may Allah be pleased with her. She had believed him from the moment he returned from Hira, reassured him, and stood beside him when there was not yet a Muslim community to protect him or a worldly power behind him.

Islam then entered his household and the circle closest to him. Among the earliest Muslims was ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib, may Allah be pleased with him. He was still a young boy — not yet a grown man — and was living in the Prophet’s home. Another of the earliest believers was Zayd ibn Harithah, may Allah be pleased with him, whom the Prophet had freed before the mission and who remained very close to him.

Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, may Allah be pleased with him, was also among the earliest to believe. He was well known among Quraysh: a merchant of good character with broad knowledge of Quraysh’s family lines and history. People knew him and liked to sit with him. His faith therefore did not remain a private matter; he began speaking to friends and acquaintances whom he trusted.

The books of Seerah report that among those who responded through him in this early period were ‘Uthman ibn ‘Affan, al-Zubayr ibn al-‘Awwam, ‘Abd al-Rahman ibn ‘Awf, Sa‘d ibn Abi Waqqas, and Talhah ibn ‘Ubaydillah, may Allah be pleased with them. Years later they would become some of the best-known Companions of the Prophet, but at that moment they were simply individuals entering a new faith while most of Makkah remained as it had been.

Reports differ over the order in which some of the earliest Muslims accepted Islam after Khadijah, may Allah be pleased with her. The call began within a very small circle: at home, among friends, and among people who knew Muhammad closely.

This period is sometimes called the “secret call.” That does not mean Islam was a hidden secret known to no one. It means that the call had not yet become an open, public confrontation with all of Quraysh. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, called people individually, and a small community of believers gradually formed around him.

The books of Seerah commonly report that this non-public phase lasted about three years. Later came the command to proclaim the message openly and warn in public. Before the story reaches that day, however, this small community first had to learn: Who was their Lord? What was the Qur’an being revealed to them? How were they to pray? And how would they remain firm once the people of Makkah learned about them?

In the next chapter we will come closer to those first believers themselves: men, women, and young people who entered Islam when it had no wealth, state, or worldly power behind it, and who became the first nucleus of the community raised around the Messenger of Allah.
