Allah’s greatness and its effect on the heart and our deeds

Ibn Mas‘ūd (may Allah be pleased with him) reported: A Jewish rabbi came to the Messenger of Allah (may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him) and said: "O Muhammad, we learned that Allah will place the heavens on one finger, and the earths on one finger, and the trees on one finger, and the water and dust on one finger, and all other creatures on one finger. Then, He will say: 'I am the King.'" Thereupon, the Prophet (may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him) laughed until his premolar teeth became visible, in confirmation of the rabbi’s words. Then, the Messenger of Allah (may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him) recited: {They did not revere Allah His true reverence. On the Day of Resurrection, the whole earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand. Glorified and exalted is He above all what they associate with Him!}

Al-Bukhari; Muslim — The Book of Faith

The rabbi was a Jewish scholar. His words “we learned” refer to what they found in their scripture. The Prophet accepted the account as true and recited the verse that reveals Allah’s greatness and declares Him exalted above all partners associated with Him. The description of the heavens, the earths and everything they contain is not merely meant to make us picture an astonishing scene. It leads the heart to know the One for whom none of this creation presents any difficulty, to revere Him and to submit to Him alone.

The encounter also shows that truth is not rejected simply because the person speaking differs from us in belief. The Prophet affirmed what was true in the rabbi’s words, just as he confirmed what Abu Hurayrah reported about the devil and the benefit of reciting Ayat al-Kursi before sleep: “He (who came to you at night) told you the truth although he is a liar; that was a shaytan.” What matters is whether the report is established, not whether we like or dislike the person. The need to verify it remains.
Al-Bukhari; Summarized Islamic Fiqh

The attributes revealed about Allah are affirmed in a way befitting Him: without likening Him to His creation, and without denying what He has affirmed. His Fingers must not be likened to those of created beings. We must neither invent an account of how they are nor strip the words of their meaning by treating them merely as a figure of speech for how easily He governs creation. His effortless governance and perfect power are implications and benefits of the report; they do not cancel what it affirms. Likewise, the Prophet said: “Indeed, the hearts of all the children of Adam are between two Fingers of the Most Compassionate, like one heart; He directs it as He wills.” Just as the vastness of the heavens presents no difficulty for Allah, the multitude of hearts does not crowd or impede His governance.
Muslim

Ibn Qutaybah rejected interpreting the two Fingers as two blessings. In the report in which the Prophet prays for his heart to remain steadfast, that interpretation would not answer the fear of the heart changing: if it were already protected by two blessings, why would he pray for its steadfastness? The point is to preserve what the report says in a way befitting Allah, without likening Him to creation.
Ibn Qutaybah, Interpretation of Conflicting Hadiths

Nor does the expression “between” necessarily imply contact or indwelling. Clouds are between the sky and the earth without touching either, and Badr is between Makkah and Madinah while being distant from both, as Ibn Uthaymin explained.
Ibn Uthaymin, Al-Qawaid al-Muthla

The heart’s response is governed by the words of those firmly grounded in knowledge: “We believe in it. It is all from our Lord[1].” (3:7) Understanding the meaning does not mean being able to comprehend how the attribute is.

Revering Allah is among the noblest acts of the heart. When it takes root, a person honors revelation and religious law, recognizes the rank of the messengers, and humbles himself before Allah alone with awe and surrender. The name “the Most Great” brings together two connected meanings: the perfection of the Lord’s strength, might, power, knowledge, glory and grandeur; and His right to be revered by our hearts, tongues and actions.

The Prophet reported these words from Allah: “Glory is My Izār and Pride is My Cloak. I will punish whoever contends with Me over either of them.” Izār is the Arabic word for a lower garment. Another narration warns of being cast into the Fire. These warnings call the servant to humility: knowing Allah’s greatness must not become a reason to exalt oneself.
Al-Adab al-Mufrad; Muslim; Abu Dawud

Reverence includes striving to know Allah, love Him and fear Him; obeying, remembering and thanking Him; submitting to His commands and judgment; and honoring the times, places, people and deeds He has honored. Reverence is the heart of worship. It is not enough for the tongue to speak of it while the heart treats His commands lightly.

One of its greatest sources of nourishment is reflection on the signs in creation. Noah rebuked his people: “What is the matter with you that you do not fear the Majesty of Allah[1],” (71:13) “when He has created you in stages[1]?” (71:14) He connected the rebuke to evidence close to them: their own creation. A person passes through stages in the mother’s womb, then nursing, childhood, discernment and youth, without bringing these stages about for himself. The One who alone governs this deserves to be worshiped alone. Remembering our beginnings also shows that we can be brought back to life after death.

The field of reflection then widens to the seven heavens, and to the moon as a light and the sun as a lamp: “Do you not see how Allah has created seven heavens, one above another,” (71:15) “and has placed the moon therein as a light and the sun as a burning lamp?” (71:16) Greatness in creation is joined to mercy in its benefits, bringing reverence, love, fear and hope together in the heart.

The account extends to our origin from the earth, our return to it and our emergence from it again: “Allah has produced you from the earth like a plant[1], then He will return you into it and then will bring you out again.” (71:17, 71:18) It also describes the earth being made ready for habitation, tilling, planting and travel: “Allah has made the earth widespread for you so that you may tread its spacious paths[1].” (71:19, 71:20) These signs are not remote from human needs. They surround us in our creation, provision, death and final destiny.

The alternation of night and day continually renews this reflection. Allah says: “Indeed, in the alternation of the night and day, and in what Allah has created in the heavens and earth, there are signs[1] for people who fear Him.” (10:6) He also says: “Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and earth and the alternation of the night and day are signs for people of understanding” (3:190).

A person considers the sky’s height, breadth and stars, and the earth’s mountains, seas, rivers, valleys, trees and benefits. From the greatness of what is close at hand, he moves to what is greater still. The earth that amazes him is small compared with the heavens, and the heavens and earth lie within the vastness of the Kursi: “His Kursī[5] [i.e., footstool] extends over the heavens and earth” (2:255). Then comes the Throne, and the reported comparison: “The Kursi in comparison to the Throne is only like an iron ring thrown in a desert on earth.”
Ibn Abi Shaybah, The Throne

The account from the Companion Ibn Mas‘ud describes the vastness of these created things and the distances between them. He mentions a journey of five hundred years between the lowest heaven and the next, and the same between each successive heaven; five hundred years between the seventh heaven and the Kursi; and the same between the Kursi and the water. He then brings together Allah’s exaltedness and His all-encompassing knowledge: “The Throne is above the water and Allah is above the Throne, and none of your deeds are concealed from Him.”
The Book of Monotheism

Reflection does not end with admiration for a created thing. It points to the greatness of its Creator, as in the report: “Reflect deeply upon the Blessings of Allah, but do not reflect upon the essence of Allah.”
Al-Tabarani, Al-Mu‘jam al-Awsat
This report has been graded hasan through supporting narrations; its individual chain has been criticised.

When reflection produces sincere reverence, a person treats the religion as sacred: “whoever honors the rituals of Allah, it is from the piety of the hearts.” (22:32) “whoever honours the sacred things of Allâh, then that is better for him with his Lord” (22:30).

Remembrance is not repetition detached from this meaning. In bowing and prostration, the Prophet would say: “Glory be to the Possessor of greatness, dominion, grandeur, and majesty.” He said: “So, while bowing, glorify the Lord”. He would also say “Glory be to my Great Lord” and “Glory be to my Lord, the Most High”.
Abu Dawud; Al-Nasai; Muslim

The two phrases that are light on the tongue, heavy in the scales and beloved to the Most Compassionate bring praise and reverence together: “Glory and praise be to Allah; glory be to Allah, the Magnificent.” In this way, hearts are brought to life and their reverence for Allah grows stronger.
Muslim; Al-Bukhari

The servant must beware of what tears down this inner life. Sins weaken reverence, and weakened reverence then leads to greater audacity. Ibn al-Qayyim noted that one punishment for sins is that they weaken reverence for the Lord in the heart.
Ibn al-Qayyim, The Disease and the Cure

A person cannot excuse his audacity by claiming to have good hope in Allah. Hope accompanied by reverence honors what Allah has made sacred. Treating His commands and prohibitions lightly contradicts that reverence.
Ibn al-Qayyim, The Disease and the Cure

The scene of the Hereafter reveals the end of attachment to wealth and authority. The one given his record in his left hand says: “My wealth has been of no avail to me. My authority has vanished[1].” (69:28, 69:29) The reason for his punishment is then stated: “For he did not believe in Allah, the Most Great” (69:33).

To revere Allah as He deserves is to know Him in a way that bears fruit in worship. It is reverence in private that shows itself in how we act and the limits we observe. It goes beyond a passing sense of wonder at a scene in the universe, after which we forget what we owe our Lord.

Quran translations: Rowwad Translation Center; al-Hilali and Khan (22:30); The Book of Faith (39:67).
