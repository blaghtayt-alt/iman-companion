# The Trial We Await

Someone may long for relief from distress, more generous provision, or the fulfilment of a wish they have long hoped for, imagining that attaining it will end their trial. Yet what we eagerly await may bring a test we are unprepared for: the trial of ease. Hardship may pass, only for another test to begin—in gratitude, steadfastness and fulfilling our obligations. What we receive after a long wait becomes a trust whose duties we will be called to account for.

> “If they had remained steadfast on the right way, We would have given them abundant rain,
>
> as a test for them[1]. But whoever turns away from the remembrance of his Lord[2], He will make him suffer an arduous punishment.

[Al-Jinn 72:16–17. Translation: Rowwad Translation Center, v1.0.20, QuranEnc.com.]

[1] As to whether they will be grateful for Allah's provisions or not.
 [2] i.e., the Qur’an and obedience to Him.

Reference: al-Sa‘di’s Quran commentary, al-Jinn 72:16–17.

The two verses link giving with testing: the water they are given is a blessing through which they are tested. This changes how the heart views what it hopes for. Receiving a blessing calls for preparation to meet the obligations it brings, and care not to let it distract us from remembering our Lord. Someone who recognises prosperity as a test remains alert to how it affects them, rather than merely rejoicing that their pain has ended. They endured patiently when life was hard; now they are tested when their circumstances improve and what they love is placed before them.

Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf (may Allah be pleased with him) said: "We were tested along with the Messenger of Allah(s.a.w) by adversity, so we were patient, then we were tested after him with prosperity, but we were not patient." Recorded by al-Tirmidhi (2464), who graded it hasan (good). [English quotation: Sunnah.com.]

Note: This is a statement by the Companion Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, not words spoken by the Prophet. The Arabic parent explicitly attributes the grade hasan (good) to al-Tirmidhi; Darussalam separately grades the report da‘if (weak). These different assessments are attributed to their sources, not presented as a consensus or a new grading by the translator. The English quotation is reproduced unchanged from Sunnah.com, whose page does not identify the translator. The publisher’s abbreviation “s.a.w” refers to blessings and peace upon the Messenger.

His words remind us that steadfastness in hardship does not mean we can forgo self-discipline in times of ease. Each state has its own test, and someone may falter before what they desire after standing firm against what hurts them.

A believer may hope for a better life, seek a livelihood and pursue good, seek an end to injustice against them, and help remove injustice against others as far as they can. They may choose to forgo a right of their own, but they have no authority to waive the rights of others. These efforts should be accompanied by an awareness that better circumstances bring fresh responsibilities. We need to prepare for blessings as we prepare to face hardship, and remain true to our religion in both.

> Joseph said, “My Lord, I would rather be in prison than that to which they are calling me. Unless you protect me from their guile, I may succumb to them and fall into ignorance.”

[Joseph 12:33. Translation: Rowwad Translation Center, v1.0.20, QuranEnc.com.]

Reference: al-Sa‘di’s Quran commentary, Joseph 12:33.

Joseph (peace be upon him) chose the hardship of prison over yielding to the sin he was being urged to commit. For him, preserving his religion mattered more than a pleasure that would bring a bad outcome. Yet even as he stood firm, he sought his Lord’s help against their plotting and acknowledged his need for that help. This can awaken a heart that measures goodness by comfort alone: hardship through which a person preserves their religion may be better for them than ease that draws them towards what angers Allah. Facing temptation therefore requires us to stay alert and seek Allah’s help, just as we must when facing harm.

This test may emerge when pressure fails to make someone compromise, but they are then given access to worldly gains that appeal to them. Temptation comes with promises and benefits, and they are urged to retreat little by little, until they feel too embarrassed to denounce wrongdoing or fear losing what they have gained. They may grow accustomed to a concession they once saw as temporary. In time, their judgment of it changes, and they defend it as though it were right. At that point, they need to examine what is happening to their convictions while they celebrate their improved circumstances, and watch where the desire to hold on to their gains may lead them.

Preparing for this test includes putting our inner motives right. A person may speak of supporting the truth and ending injustice while a desire for money, fame or status is mixed into their intentions. We deal with people on the basis of what they show outwardly; their inner motives are for Allah to judge. But each of us is required to examine our own intention, so that selfish desires we have not recognised do not get the better of us as we pursue what we want. Even after gaining wealth, status or influence, we still need this self-examination, and to remember the rights and trusts that have come into our hands.

When relief comes, then, receive it with gratitude and fulfil what is due to Allah in it. Let what you have gained help you do good. As your ability grows, remember that your responsibility grows with it, and continue to fear falling short of what the blessing requires of you. Part of being fully prepared for what you hope for is thinking about what you will do with it if it is granted to you. The relief you await may be the beginning of your test in gratitude and steadfastness.
