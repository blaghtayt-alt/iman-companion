A Little Is Enough

You may begin a retreat for worship in the mosque carrying a small bag with two garments. You sleep in a space barely large enough for your body, eat simpler food than usual, and wait your turn to use facilities shared with a group of people. At first, some of this feels difficult. You miss your bed, the quiet of your room and food prepared to your taste. Then the days pass, and you discover that you have managed to live. You missed things you love, but life did not stop without them. Amid this little, you still had Qur’an to read, prayers to stand for, and supplications in which to express your need of your Lord. Perhaps your heart even found an attentiveness that you had missed when every comfort was within reach.

This knowledge, which you have tasted for yourself, deserves to come home with you. Many things you considered indispensable needs were familiar habits that had made life without them hard to imagine. Retreat reveals something of their hold over you: you can postpone a desire, accept different food, sleep somewhere unfamiliar, and life goes on. Experience teaches you that your need for some things is smaller than the space they have occupied within you.

Your retreat may also be comfortable, legally valid, and a time in which you perform your worship. The validity of worship under its legal rulings is one matter; what you gain from it in training your heart is another. What we need to take away is freedom from the captivity of habit, so that we become more capable of obedience when it conflicts with our comfort. If you have not made a retreat, a journey, a hardship or a few simple days may have taught you the same lesson: I thought I could not manage, and then I did. Must every desire return to its old position, as though you had learned nothing?

Enough to bring you back to your Lord

In the report of Abu al-Darda, may Allah be pleased with him, two angels call out at sunrise, making themselves heard by the inhabitants of the earth except humans and jinn: “O people! Come towards your Lord, for a little that suffices is better than much that distracts.” Reported by Ahmad; Ibn Hibban also transmits the report. Notice how having enough accompanies this call: “Come towards your Lord”. You recognize what is enough when you know where you want to go, and you recognize what distracts you when you remember what you were created for.

Your day may be full from beginning to end, yet you go to bed feeling that something important has not happened. You completed tasks, followed the news and spoke to many people, but you did not pause to attend to your heart or consider your relationship with Allah. The days repeat, and the demands upon you multiply, until the first question disappears: what purpose does all this activity serve?

Allah created us to worship Him: to know Him through what He has taught us of His names, attributes and signs; to love Him, hope in Him and fear Him; and to journey toward Him by what He has prescribed. This servitude includes prayer, fasting and obligatory charity, reflection on His signs, honesty and justice, earning a lawful livelihood, and fulfilling the rights of family and other people. Do not confine religion to the minutes spent on obligatory acts, then go through the rest of the day as though your actions and choices had no connection with serving Him. Your work, spending and good companionship can be acts of obedience when your intention, means and fulfilment of rights are sound.

To live and do all this, you need food, drink, shelter and clothing. You also need the peace, chastity and companionship of marriage. Allah made these things means that sustain human life and placed within us desires that seek them. The disorder begins when a means takes the place of the purpose, and a whole life is devoted to what should have helped that life. You eat to gain strength, then spend your strength chasing what you will eat. You work to support your home, then have nothing left to bring home but exhaustion. You seek people’s help in upholding the truth, then change the truth to keep them around you.

Desire does not become dangerous simply because it exists within you, but when you grant it the authority to judge everything. A person may grow so attached to a forbidden relationship that they resent the religion that prevents it, and seek to distort or cancel its ruling in order to keep what they desire. What Allah placed in the human soul is thus made a reason to object to Him. Love and companionship have their beautiful place in marriage, together with chastity, the preservation of lineage and the continuation of the family. When desire is released from its limits, trust, rights and mutual compassion are exposed to harm. The same applies to food and money: needing them does not make every means of acquiring them permissible.

Here we need revelation to put our aims in order and to assess by it what individuals and societies have come to admire. A habit, idea or way of judging life may appeal to us, and we may follow it for a long time before seeing the human rights and religious commitments it neglects. Familiarity does not make everything a sound standard. When you remember that you are Allah’s servant, the question becomes more immediate and honest: does this help me fulfil what I live for, or take me away from it?

How does having more scatter our attention?

Take something close at hand that hardly leaves your fingers: your phone and social media. They have made communication easier, brought distant people closer, and opened opportunities for learning, work and keeping in touch. Then our use of them went far beyond what we needed. You go in to do something, move from page to page and clip to clip, then realize that a long time has passed without any result you can name. You came to connect with people, and now you cannot sit with yourself for a few minutes without a screen.

Not everything that passes before your eyes disappears when you close the phone. Some remains in your mind and returns when you stand to pray, open the Qur’an or try to reflect. Your body may stand before Allah while your heart moves through what it last saw and heard. The harm has spread beyond browsing time into the time you hoped would be devoted to your Lord. Having enough is a mercy here: take the benefit of the tool while preserving your heart’s capacity for stillness and your prayer’s share of attention.

Then consider shopping. You enjoy something new for a little while, and almost as soon as it becomes familiar, something newer appears and your longing moves to it. The device, clothes and furniture change, but the feeling that something else is missing remains. Your need for the thing may not be what moves you; it may be your desire for people to see you own it. Allah says: Competition for worldly gains distracts you [from Allah], (Al-Takathur 102:1). When the reckoning begins with what others have, it can hardly end with what you have, because comparison can always find someone with more.

This way of thinking expands until a rising level of consumption becomes life’s purpose, and every increase in income becomes an invitation to an equal increase in expenses. More money comes in, but the soul feels no more at ease, because it has moved the point at which it says, ‘I have enough.’ Even the person persuading you that you must buy more may be caught on the other side of the same cycle, accumulating profits without knowing where to stop. The problem concerns the demands we cultivate in our hearts as well as what we hold in our hands.

Calling this ‘well-being’ is not enough to make it an aim that rules over every other aim. Relieving hardship and providing people with food, housing and medical care are clear goods. Turning someone into a consumer who is never satisfied, and measuring their success by a stream of new possessions, is another matter that we need to question. Not every improvement is a necessity, and not everything available to buy deserves another portion of our lives as its price.

When this race becomes oppressive, a person may seek escape in more amusement, then find stillness even harder to bear. Lawful recreation has a beneficial place: it refreshes us, helps us return to serious work and brings companionship to the family. Look at what follows it. Do you return better able to fulfil your duties, or need another diversion to avoid facing an emptiness or a question? Escape may even cross the limits of the permissible into intoxicants, drugs and other forms of corruption. The longer we put off facing what is happening in the heart, the greater the cost of escaping it.

As your heart loosens this attachment, it is not being invited into emptiness. A wider door stands before it: greater knowledge of Allah and what He loves, greater delight in remembering Him, and actions that draw you closer to Him. More food can leave you overfull, but understanding a verse you used to pass over, or making a sincere supplication after heedlessness, may open new life within your heart. A report states, in a translation of its Arabic meaning: ‘Two eager seekers are never satisfied: one eager for knowledge is never satisfied, and one eager for this world is never satisfied.’ Reported by al-Hakim. In which pursuit do you want your longing to grow?

Seeking the world here goes beyond a mouthful that satisfies hunger to wealth and standing whose possessor accepts no limit. The believer’s destination is Paradise and Allah’s pleasure. The bliss of the Hereafter remains, untouched by this world’s boredom or exhaustion. Giving up some accumulation therefore need not diminish you. Having enough may make room in your day for what expands your heart and renews your hope. In place of what you leave of the excess, you can put beneficial knowledge, kindness that brings someone joy, or time alone in which your heart softens toward its Lord.

When the Qur’an examines our attachment to blessings

Luxury is repeatedly mentioned in the Qur’an in warnings against turning away, ingratitude and disobedience. What matters is more than the mere presence of money or a spacious house. It is what the blessing does to its possessor: does it bear witness to Allah’s bounty and call them to gratitude, or do they become so absorbed in it that they find unwelcome any truth that conflicts with it?

But the chiefs of his people who disbelieved and denied the meeting of the Hereafter, and whom We had made affluent in the life of this world, said, “He is no more than a man like yourselves; he eats what you eat and drinks what you drink. (Al-Mu’minun 23:33).

The blessings were present, and denial was present alongside them. What they had been given did not lead them to know the Giver or revere His command. This is the pause that brings you back to yourself: do you make comfort a condition of responding? When you hear the truth, do you first investigate what it will cost you in habits, then judge it by how well it agrees with them? Attachment may begin small, then preserving your way of life comes to take priority over correcting what you know is wrong.

Allah also says: But the wrongdoers pursued their luxury[59] which they were given therein, and they were wicked. (Hud 11:116). They follow the comforts they have been given until the blessing becomes a path that leads them, instead of remaining in their hands to use as Allah commands. From this you can appreciate the value of sometimes going against a permissible habit so that you retain the ability to choose. Accept simpler food, postpone a purchase and live a day without answering every desire. Your soul learns that it can bear the absence of some familiar things.

In Hud’s address to his people, peace be upon him, we read: Do you build a landmark[17] on every high place for no purpose? And do you construct for yourselves palaces, hoping that you will live forever? (Al-Shu‘ara 26:128–129). Ibn Kathir explains that the rebuke concerned building without need, for amusement and a display of power, wasting time and exhausting bodies on what brings no benefit. Consider what appearances consume: money, health, thought and years of life. A thing may impress the people who see it while doing little good for the person who has exhausted himself over it.

You have a limited span of life, a measure of bodily strength, a capacity to endure, and a certain amount of money. What you spend of these on one thing is no longer available for another. Useful work with a good intention may bring together worldly benefit and reward in the Hereafter. The loss lies in draining these gifts for an increase you do not need, then complaining that there is no time for the Qur’an, learning, reflection and fulfilling rights. Perhaps some of what you call unavoidable busyness was a choice you repeated until you forgot that you could reconsider it.

The Qur’an also warns about luxury when it becomes an example others follow and a source of power: Whenever We decide to destroy a town, We command its affluent ones [to obey Allah] but they defiantly disobey, so the punishment becomes inevitable, and We destroy it completely. (Al-Isra 17:16). One explanation of ‘We command’ is that they were commanded to obey but disobeyed; the verse must not be understood as Allah religiously commanding sinful conduct. Books of commentary also mention interpretations concerning increase in number and the granting of authority, according to the reading and interpretation. The point is that when the wealthy and influential lead corruption, they draw others after them. Its danger increases when wealth and power combine and tempt a person to feel that no one else’s rights can limit their ability. They need reverence for Allah to restrain them from oppression.

Ibn al-Jawzi explains that the affluent are singled out because they are leaders whom people follow. You see a phone in a famous person’s hand and begin to feel that you need one like it. Its price may mean little in that person’s wealth, while you enter into instalments to resemble their image. What have you gained? If what you already have does the job, what need did the extra purchase meet? You may even be able to pay the full price and still need to question your heart: why do I want it? Being able to buy does not make every purchase a wise choice.

Allah says of the people of the left: Indeed, they used to be in luxuries before, and persisted in the gravest sin[5]. (Al-Waqi‘ah 56:45–46). In explaining the passage, al-Razi draws attention to the ugliness of ingratitude in the presence of blessings. Blessings call for gratitude, yet they persisted in sin. A person may become so occupied with their comforts that they pass Allah’s signs without taking heed. When did you last sit and reflect on a blessing you received, a change in your circumstances, or weakness that appeared after strength? When did you consider what must change within you as you watched circumstances change? The soul needs time in which the tongue grows quiet and observation becomes clear.

Other verses reveal the connection between luxury and defending falsehood: We never sent a warner to a town except that its affluent ones said, “We disbelieve in what you have been sent with.” (Saba 34:34). Allah also says: Likewise, We never sent any warner to a town before you except that its affluent ones said, “We found our forefathers on a path, and we are following their footsteps.” (Al-Zukhruf 43:23). An inherited custom may become an argument used to reject the truth and protect what is familiar. The problem then goes beyond lacking information: it is attachment to what a person fears changing.

To avoid being deceived by the glitter of things, read Allah’s words: Were it not that all people might become a single community [of disbelievers][13], We would have given those who disbelieve in the Most Compassionate One houses of sliver roofs and staircases to ascend, and their houses having [silver] doors and couches on which they recline, and [golden] ornaments. But all of these are mere enjoyments of the life of this world, and the Hereafter with your Lord is [only] for those who fear Allah. (Al-Zukhruf 43:33–35).

Ibn Kathir explains that the concern is that people might unite in disbelief if they saw disbelievers given such adornments and thought them a sign of divine love. Silver roofs, couches and gold, however dazzling, are enjoyments of this world. The end of the verses brings you back to reverence for Allah and the Hereafter. Do not read Allah’s pleasure with someone in the size of their house, or judge a wealthy person to be under His displeasure merely because they live comfortably. Consider how the blessing was earned, how it is received with gratitude and used, and how much obedience remains in the heart alongside it. You need this standard whenever images offer you another.

What do we really need?

Scholars distinguish between necessities, needs that relieve hardship, and enhancements. Applied here to daily living, these are what life cannot function properly without, what removes difficulty, and what adds beauty, comfort and adornment. These levels exist within a single area: food is not all on one level, and neither are clothing and housing. One meal sustains your body; another is easier for you and more to your taste; most of the price of a third pays for the name or exclusivity of the place. Do not make every food expense a necessity simply because it concerns food.

Al-Razi brings contentment closer to understanding by considering a person’s basic need for shelter, clothing and food. He then shows how a person seeks an ornamented house, finer clothes and better riding animals, imposing new hardships upon himself. His words, translated from Arabic, are: ‘Seeking wealth produces poverty.’ In other words, expanding demands may increase the sense of need, even as possessions multiply. He gives examples of extreme simplicity, mentioning even the most basic shelter and patched clothing. The point is to distinguish necessity from excess, not to require families to live in caves or make do with one garment throughout a lifetime.

It is unfair to tell someone unable to meet their basic needs that their whole problem is a lack of contentment. Some people struggle for food, rent and medicine, and they have a right to help and justice. What addresses you here concerns the demands you are able to reconsider. Which things have become a condition of living in your mind, although alternatives are possible? What standard did you choose, then make falling below it a defeat, although your family could live with reasonable comfort at a lower level?

Look at the life of the Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, and his Companions to recover a sense of the value of what you have. Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, related that they would see three crescents over two months without a fire being lit in the Messenger of Allah’s homes. They lived on dates and water, with milk that came from some of their neighbours. Al-Bukhari reports this. Sahih Muslim relates how the Prophet, Abu Bakr and Umar left their homes because of hunger, and an Ansari then honoured them with hospitality. The Prophet slept on a mat that left marks on his side, and Abu Hurayra spoke of hunger so severe that he fell unconscious.

Read these accounts to become grateful and to understand that the value of life is not measured by the comforts available in it. Among the Companions were people of wealth and trade. An increase in wealth did not oblige them to make increasing the adornments of life their entire concern. Do not belittle a familiar blessing because someone else has more. The home that shelters you, the food you find and your ability to worship contain goodness that has become so familiar that you look through it only at what you lack.

The loss caused by putting demands in the wrong order is especially clear in marriage. A young man and woman need chastity, companionship and a home. Then the costs of the celebration, bridal gift, furniture and standard of housing accumulate before them, until the basic need is delayed for additions surrounding it. Both want what Allah has made lawful, but society places conditions at the door, not all of which life depends upon. Accumulating more becomes a barrier to having enough: we insist on the most complete picture of a beginning, and prevent the beginning itself.

A couple can begin in a small home suited to their circumstances, with a bedroom, living room and facilities sufficient for them, then move to something larger as need and means allow. When they move, another couple can find a suitable beginning in the small home. Small homes for young people in the German city of Dresden are among the examples mentioned in connection with this idea. What matters is the possibility of designing beginnings suited to their occupants’ stage of life. Families can help by easing expectations, and those able to build, finance and plan can provide suitable alternatives. A good intention does not dispense with thinking through solutions people can actually use.

Making things easier must not become a reason to disregard either spouse’s rights or ignore what they actually need; needs differ. But the question deserves to remain: why do we make something we may need years from now a condition without which life cannot begin? We may boast that our tools have become easier than those of earlier generations, then use them to complicate our simplest needs. Advances in cars, phones and construction do not guarantee wisdom in ordering our aims. We learn wisdom from revelation and the Prophet’s guidance while benefiting from every useful means.

What greed does to wealth and standing

The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, said: “Two wolves free among sheep are no more destructive to them than a man's desire for wealth and honor is to his religion.” Reported by al-Tirmidhi, who called it a good and sound report. ‘Honor’ here means rank and standing among people. Picture two hungry wolves entering a flock, then remember that the report warns you about the damage greed does to religion. The loss is more than buying something and regretting its price: it may reach your honesty, prayer, fairness and the rights of those around you.

The image of the two wolves conveys something beyond meeting a passing need: widespread destruction. So it is when greed takes over. It does not stop at what sustains your life, and may destroy things more precious than what it gathers along the way. You need not love banknotes themselves to fall into it. You may love the comfort, travel, appearance or influence money brings, and your demands are what call for more of it. Treating greed therefore begins by examining what you want to buy with it.

A story is related in this connection about Asim ibn Adi and his brother buying one hundred shares of Khaybar, linked to the warning about wealth and standing. The chain of transmission of this story is disputed. What the heart needs is already clear from the established report itself: a sale or purchase may be permissible as a contract, while the person involved still needs to examine the greed growing within. We do not judge merely by the amount of wealth, nor let a transaction’s validity replace questions about its purpose and the rights it competes with.

Another report expresses the danger of the heart’s attachment in the Prophet’s words, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him: “Perish the slave of Dinar, Dirham, Qatifa (thick soft cloth), and Khamisa (a 
garment), for if he is given, he is pleased; otherwise he is dissatisfied.” Reported by al-Bukhari. Look at pleasure and displeasure: what is directing them? Do you resent prayer when you worry about a new garment, or compromise over what is lawful and forbidden on the way to a profit? Things that should have served you have begun to command, and you obey. Each time you postpone a permissible desire for Allah, you remind yourself that what you crave need not always decide.

It is related that Umar, may Allah be pleased with him, asked Jabir when he bought meat he had wanted to eat, in a rendering of the reported Arabic words: ‘Do you buy whenever you desire something?’ The question does not prohibit meat or buying good things; it asks us to pause before an automatic response. You can have the money and choose not to buy now. You leave some permissible things to train yourself, without claiming they are forbidden to others, and without your restraint becoming stinginess toward an obligatory right or miserliness over money you love to accumulate.

One fruit of having enough is that there are fewer things others can use to pressure you. A story is told about King Faisal during the oil cutoff, conveying a readiness to return to dates and water in the face of threats. Its authenticity is not asserted here. The question it raises is understandable without depending on its being established: how much freedom do you lose when your ability to endure depends on retaining every comfort? Someone able to live with less than they are used to is better able to remain firm without selling that firmness out of fear for every extra comfort.

Greed can enter actions whose outward appearance seems praiseworthy. A person may give money so that people speak of his generosity, or expose himself to danger in pursuit of renown and fame. Al-Mutanabbi described the cost of seeking eminence in a couplet that may be rendered: ‘Were it not for hardship, everyone would be a master; generosity impoverishes, and boldness kills.’ A person may give much, yet carry away none of the reward he hopes for, because his heart was seeking an image among people. When he is sincere toward Allah and seeks His reward, generosity and courage in their proper place become worship. Do not stop doing good out of fear about your intention; correct the intention and keep the good.

The desire to accumulate may extend to friends, followers and supporters. Someone begins by calling to the truth, then finds a smaller audience hard to bear and compromises the foundation of his message. Or he makes agreement with people who differ in belief an aim for which he sacrifices sound belief itself. He has then lost the purpose to preserve a means from which he hopes for strength, and even that imagined strength may never hold. Cooperation has its proper situations and rulings. What calls for examination here is letting numbers become a reason to bargain away the foundation of truth.

Allah says: O Prophet, Allah is sufficient for you[40] and for the believers who follow you. (Al-Anfal 8:64). Allah is sufficient for His Prophet and for those who follow him, as Ibn Kathir explains. Do not make the number of followers alone proof of success, or small numbers proof that the way has been lost. A small group may have sincerity and steadfastness that benefit others, while a large gathering may contain confusion that dissipates the fruit of gathering. A sound small group may grow, and a large group whose foundation is unsettled may scatter. Having enough extends to this area too: keep the purpose in view when the sight of great numbers tempts you.

The price you pay from your life

In his explanation of the two wolves report, Ibn Rajab draws attention to a loss that could be enough on its own to condemn greed: a person wastes a life in which he could have earned high ranks, becoming absorbed in gathering money that he later leaves to someone else, while the reckoning for it remains upon him. He describes life as having no price, meaning that its worth cannot be measured in money. You may repair a financial loss with other money, but an hour that has left your life will not return as it was.

It therefore helps to ask about the price of your choices in a wider way. You have enough money for a new phone or a course that teaches you something useful. You choose the phone, and the learning opportunity is among the things that choice costs you. This is the idea of ‘opportunity cost’: the value of the alternative you leave when you choose. It does not mean that every purchase is wrong. It reveals what disappears from view when all you see is the written price.

When you choose to attend a match, for example, what you spend goes beyond the ticket price to the time spent travelling, waiting, watching and returning. That time might have contained a family visit, work or learning you had been postponing. The point is not to prohibit lawful rest or count every hour of recreation as wasted; useful rest may help you with what follows. The point is to choose with an awareness of what you give and what you leave, and not let repetition become a habit, then say that your day left no room for anything.

Look in this way at the scattered minutes spent browsing. The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, said: “Whoever says: Subhānallāh al-‘azhīm wa bihamdih (Glory be to Allah the Most Great and praise is due to Him), a date palm will be planted for him in Paradise.” Reported by al-Tirmidhi. How often was a minute in your hands, only to go into following something you did not need, when it could have become remembrance of Allah that you carried with you? A report deemed good by some scholars promises a reward like that of a pilgrimage and a lesser pilgrimage for sitting in remembrance after the congregational dawn prayer until sunrise, then praying two units after the sun has risen higher and the period in which prayer is prohibited has ended. Knowing the reward makes time you once thought small look larger to you.

Do not turn this knowledge into an exhausting calculation or a reason to accuse yourself over every sleep and task. The body has its right, providing a livelihood has its right, and reward is connected to acceptance, sincerity and action that is religiously prescribed. But this knowledge awakens longing for what you were missing without noticing. Instead of saying, ‘I have no time for the Qur’an,’ try reclaiming time when you know where it went. Instead of waiting for complete leisure that never comes, make a place for remembrance in your day and give reflection a sitting in which notifications do not interrupt every thought.

Perhaps you did not need another life in order to begin; you needed to lighten some of what crowded this one. When you remember that provision is decreed, you do not abandon the means of earning it or neglect your duties. Rather, some of the anxiety that pushes you beyond every limit grows quieter. Seek your livelihood lawfully, work well, and recognize that blessed effort does not require surrendering what remains of your heart, relationships and obedience.

Enough for the body, help for the heart

Food is one of the nearest ways to learn the limit of enough. The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, said: “The human does not fill any container that is worse than his stomach. It is sufficient for the son of Adam to eat what will support his back. If this is not possible, then a third for food, a third for drink, and third for his breath.” Reported by al-Tirmidhi, who called it good and sound. This guidance takes account of the body’s need while restraining the self from continuing into fullness beyond that need.

You may gather everything you desire at the meal that breaks your fast, then stand to pray feeling heavy, seeking attentiveness for your heart while your body pulls you toward sleep. Before looking for a distant cause of your listlessness, reconsider what you put before prayer. Take the food that sustains and helps you, and leave the excess whose effect on you is familiar. Every craving need not receive an answer, every dish on offer need not be tried, and every state of fullness need not have more food added to it.

In Sahih al-Bukhari, it is related that Ibn Umar, may Allah be pleased with him and his father, gave food to a poor man who ate a great deal, and recalled the Prophet’s words, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him: “A believer eats in one intestine (is satisfied with a little food), and a kafir (unbeliever) eats in seven intestines (eats much food)”. The lesson here awakens contentment and discourages giving free rein to greed. Do not turn it into a judgment on someone’s faith from the size of their meal. Bodies and work have different needs, and a hungry person may have a reason you cannot see. Let the report train you; do not use it to belittle people.

Books of conduct contain strong expressions condemning overindulgence in food and warning of the laziness toward prayer and absorption in desires it brings. Umar is reported to have warned of a person’s ruin when he prefers his desire to his religion; al-Fudayl ibn Iyad is reported to have said that much food and much speech harden the heart; and Luqman is reported to have advised against eating when already full. They share one lesson: learn to stop at enough. Do not let pursuing flavours become your day’s main project, or make the number of restaurants you try and the consumption you display a measure of your worth. Turning the number of meals into a fixed measure of reverence for Allah does not put people’s lives right.

The matter extends beyond food. Those who write about spiritual discipline speak of reducing excess speech, food, socializing and sleep. Useless talk may fill the mind, company in which the heart finds no quiet may take away reflection, and sleep that continues until duties are missed may take away the day’s blessing. The point is to reconsider the excess according to your circumstances, while preserving useful speech, relationships, bodily rest and the body’s rights. When some of these areas grow quieter, you hear what the noise of habit used to hide.

Contentment also affects other people. Writers on conduct transmit Ibn Hubayra’s reminder that sustenance is yours and other people’s, and that exceeding your need may come at their expense. This is especially apparent in crises, when fear drives some people to accumulate large supplies and leaves less for those who have not yet arrived. Take what you need and consider what your hand is doing to others. Believing that your provision will not pass you by does not prevent moderate preparedness, but it helps keep your fears from becoming harm to people.

Simplicity you choose when you have the means

Clothing, walking and riding offer another field for training ourselves. Fudalah ibn Ubayd, may Allah be pleased with him, related: “The Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) has forbidden us to indulge much in luxury.” He also related: “The Prophet (ﷺ) used to command us to go barefoot at times.” Reported by Abu Dawud. These words came in the context of questions about his appearance, hair and bare feet while he governed. They teach us not to become so absorbed in maintaining comfort and appearance that we cannot bear anything else. Keep the words ‘at times’ in view: they preserve balance in understanding and practice.

Commentators on the poem about conduct express this through moderation: a person should not live wholly in indulgence or wholly in austerity, but walk and ride, wear shoes and sometimes go barefoot. You know what suits your ability, location and people’s customs, without causing harm or seeking attention through contrivance. The car may be outside your home, and you choose to walk a distance you can manage. You may be able to afford more expensive transport and use a simpler means that does the job. Your capacity to live expands, and you become less dependent on one way of doing things.

A car may break down, fuel may be unavailable or money may become tight, and then you need what you could have practised while comfortably placed. Money alone does not guarantee that every means will remain available: you may have its price and still be unable to find it. If some walking and alternative means have become familiar, hardship is easier to bear than if your first encounter with it comes in necessity. The reports of Umar’s warning against indulgence and his call to the toughness that preserves readiness belong in this connection.

A person’s position does not make attending to his own needs a disgrace. The Prophet, the finest of people, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, walked. It is told of Abu Hurayra, may Allah be pleased with him, that he carried a bundle of firewood while holding office and jokingly asked, in words rendered from Arabic, ‘Make way for the governor.’ Such an image makes imagined requirements of status seem less weighty to the heart. What do you lose by carrying what you need or riding with other people? It may bring you closer to them, teach you about their lives and remove a demand for deference that you had not noticed within yourself.

In Hudhayfa’s report, may Allah be pleased with him, the Prophet said, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, in a translation of the Arabic meaning: ‘Follow Ammar’s guidance.’ Al-Tirmidhi reported it and deemed it good. Writers on conduct mention this guidance in a context of austerity and leaving unchecked indulgence in comforts. Simplicity can thus help us be earnest in obedience and bear responsibility, with the moderation that preserves the body’s rights. From the example you learn strength in action and sincerity of heart, and loosening attachment to this world becomes a path toward the good you can offer within it.

The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, said: “Whoever gives up wearing elegant expensive clothes out of humbleness with Allah, while he can afford it, Allah will call him on the Day of Judgment before all people and let him choose whichever of the garments of faith he would like to wear.” Reported by al-Tirmidhi. What is meant is leaving luxury in clothing out of humility while able to afford it. You can buy from a simpler place, or wear a serviceable garment you already have instead of replacing what does not need replacing, with an intention that draws you closer to Allah. The ability to obtain something finer gives you a choice; it does not oblige you to spend everything you can afford on your appearance.

But pay attention to the other condition: “out of humbleness with Allah”. One person may admire his fine garment, while another admires his modest garment because he hears people describe him as detached from worldly things. Al-Hasan is reported to have said, in a rendering of his Arabic words: ‘Some people have put their humility into their clothing and their pride into their breasts.’ A simple garment may hide a strong desire for fame, and its wearer may look down on those who live comfortably while thinking himself more righteous.

Do not replace one attachment with another. You seek greater sincerity of heart toward Allah: to wear what suits you, preserve cleanliness and fulfil rights, and recognize that neither a brand name nor an appearance of austerity decides virtue. If you leave something, let doing so increase your gratitude, gentleness and humility. Do not wait for people to discover what you have given up, or turn your choice into a measure by which to judge their intentions.

Beyond the question: is it forbidden?

When you hear an exhortation to leave some permissible things, your first response may be: is it forbidden? Asking about the ruling is right; no one may prohibit what Allah has made lawful. But knowing the space of the permissible then opens a question of choice: what is more beneficial now? What purifies my heart? What competes with a more binding right? Allah’s mercy in the breadth of what is permissible does not require you to answer every desire, just as training the self does not authorize you to invent rulings for people that Allah has not prescribed.

You may practise leaving something permissible to strengthen your will and preserve your ability to choose when desire calls you to what is unlawful. You enjoy good things and give thanks, and leave some good things when you see benefit for your heart in doing so. This does not become hostility toward blessings; you are learning to use them sincerely. The better you understand this distinction, the more your view expands beyond discussing the whole exhortation through a single question, while retaining precision about the religious ruling.

This applies to retreat, food, clothing and everything discussed above. Religious law determines the boundaries of an act, and then you ask yourself what fulfils its purpose, puts the intention right and raises you in goodness. An act may be valid while a fruit of it escapes you; you may leave a permissible thing seeking Allah’s reward, and leaving it becomes worship. Do not underestimate this space: much of the hearts’ journey toward Allah takes place within it.

Returning with a lighter heart

If these meanings move you, carry them gently into life. Do not return from a lesson and suddenly change your family’s way of living, demanding that they bear what you have not prepared them for, then treat their reluctance as a fault in their faith. They have rights, needs and habits that require time to change. Begin with yourself, discuss what can be lightened, and choose what meets their purpose at lower cost without humiliation or hardship. The contentment that puts your heart right should make your companionship more merciful.

You are not being called to leave your work or neglect the livelihood of your dependants. Reconsider the way of life that calls for more from you without end. Recognize where you need to work hard to earn and where you need to stop inventing a new demand. Your provision may increase while your expenses remain at a reasonable level, opening more room for charity, help and learning, and easing some fears. Money you refrain from spending on adornment need not all be accumulated for its own sake; its good may lie in reaching someone else’s need.

Choose a clear beginning you can sustain: a purchase you postpone until you understand your need, a period in which you reduce browsing, a meal in which you stop at enough, and attention you give your family before going to another occupation. You need not begin with everything at once. Look at the habit that most takes away your power to choose, begin by going against it to an extent you can bear, then thank Allah for the strength and peace He opens to you.

When you see something tempting in another person’s life, do not look only at what is missing from your own picture. Remember what Allah has provided to meet your needs and the good you can do today. Seeking more may have hidden the joy of an existing blessing: a bed to return to, a home where you find those you love, food at hand, and time in which you can repent. As long as the door of return is open, there is still something in your life worth preserving.

Here you return to the small retreat bag. Its value lay in more than the small amount it carried: it showed you something about your heart. You can do without some familiar things and still find solace, hope and work to do. Take this understanding with you to the market, home, phone and workplace, so that when comforts return, you remain better able to use them and less subject to them.

Whenever the desire for more awakens in you, remember the call: “Come towards your Lord”. Remember that a little which is enough may preserve time in which to read a verse, a heart attentive in prayer, a hand with which to help someone in need, and patience with which to treat your family well. A little of this world is enough when you know its place along the way, and when a greater longing for what is with Allah remains in your heart. O Allah, grant us grateful hearts and contented souls. Help us use Your blessings in what pleases You and preserve from our lives what draws us closer to You.

Encouraging the frequent remembrance of Allah Almighty and it includes exalting Allah along with praising Him.

Paragraph 34 — Explanation translated from Arabic source context
The three categories are used here as a practical illustration. Al-Shatibi’s discussion covers religious and worldly interests across worship, customs and dealings, and includes good moral qualities among enhancements. It is not limited to levels of spending on food, clothing and housing.

Paragraph 72 — Benefits translated from the Arabic explanation; own rendering of meaning
Humility in clothing is virtuous, without raising oneself above others or being proud toward them. The promised reward is for leaving fine clothing out of humility, rather than stinginess or displaying an image of renunciation. This restraint should express detachment from worldly adornment rather than preoccupation with it. Someone who sincerely resolves that, if able, they would leave such clothing out of humility is rewarded for the intention. Allah undertakes to adorn the one who leaves adornment for His sake.

The garments of faith are the garments of Paradise given to the people of faith, as al-Tirmidhi explains.
