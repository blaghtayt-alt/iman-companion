## 101:1

> The Striking Calamity!

“The Striking Calamity!” The surah opens with a single Arabic word: al-qāriʿah [Arabic: الْقَارِعَةُ]. There is no explanation or introduction. The word itself strikes the ear, just as one object strikes another and makes a loud sound; it is also used for an overwhelming calamity. This forceful opening draws the heart away from its distractions. There is news that, when it arrives, will overshadow all the news of this world, because our very destiny is at stake. The word stands without an explicit verb to frame it, as though it has been cast into the listener’s hearing to compel the question: what is this striking calamity? The Quran does not wait for its hearer to believe in resurrection before announcing it. It declares the truth with force, because people’s rejection of the news does not prevent it from coming to pass.

## 101:2

> What is the Striking Calamity?

“What is the Striking Calamity?” This question conveys the greatness of the event; it is not a question asked out of ignorance. After the word has struck the ear, the question strikes the mind: what is this event that bears such a name? Repeating the name strengthens its impact rather than lessening it. In our lives, some calamities are so serious that merely hearing about them makes us put aside whatever we are doing. But this surah calls us to reconsider which calamities matter most. What deserves to occupy your heart more than the news of a day that determines your eternal destiny?

## 101:3

> How do you know what the Striking Calamity is?

“How do you know what the Striking Calamity is?” The meaning moves beyond recognizing the event’s greatness to acknowledging that the reality of that day exceeds what human imagination can grasp on its own. What you know of it, you know because Allah has told you. The unseen realities of the Hereafter cannot therefore be measured by the standards of worldly experience. Revelation opens a window through which you can see what your eyes have not seen. Faith here means receiving this news with certainty, until its effect upon your heart is as real as if you had witnessed the signs leading up to it.

## 101:4

> On that Day people will be like scattered moths[1],

After the sound comes a visible scene: “On that Day people will be like scattered moths[1]”. People who now move with plans, a sense of direction and an air of importance will become scattered and light, moving in every direction like moths drawn erratically towards a light. The image brings together weakness, confusion and a heedless rush. One lesson to reflect on is that a person who follows every desire and doubtful idea that comes along, without a sound standard of judgment, already resembles these moths in their conduct. Such a person needs the steadying weight of revelation before the day arrives when all people will be caught in this turmoil.

## 101:5

> and the mountains will be like carded wool[2].

“and the mountains will be like carded wool[2].” A mountain is among the heaviest and most firmly established things a person can see. [The Arabic word al-ʿihn (الْعِهْنِ) means colored wool; al-manfūsh (الْمَنْفُوشِ) describes wool whose fibers have been loosened until it is light and easily scattered.] If mountains themselves become like this, where can a person look for material stability? The surah removes reliance on the heaviest visible creation, then points to another kind of weight that will not collapse: the weight of deeds in the balance. Everything you thought a fortress in this world can become light and insubstantial. What will determine your place is the value of what you offered to Allah.

## 101:6

> Then the one whose scales of good deeds are heavy,

Amid a scene in which everything has become light, the heart hears the words it is searching for: “Then the one whose scales of good deeds are heavy”. On that day, it is deeds, not mountains, that provide firmness. [The plural mawāzīn (مَوَازِينُ) means balances or scales.] It recalls the many words, intentions and choices that will be brought forward: no part of a life was without significance. Allah’s measure may overturn the world’s judgments completely. Someone highly esteemed by people may carry no weight with Allah, while the deeds of a servant whom no one notices may outweigh mountains. So do not ask only how people see you. Ask what weight this word, this decision and this heart have with Allah.

## 101:7

> will have a pleasant life[3].

For the one whose balance is heavy, the promise is: “will have a pleasant life[3].” This is a life that brings satisfaction and peace, after the terror in which people were scattered and mountains flew apart. Such security is the fruit of weight acquired beforehand through faith and action. Whoever bore a weighty message in this world and held firmly to revelation, refusing to be swept away by desires and doubts, will find firmness on the day when everything becomes light. [The Arabic word rāḍiyah (رَاضِيَةٍ) evokes this contented life.] It depicts a blessing untouched by resentment or fear of loss, because its measure is no longer exposed to the changes of people and the world.

## 101:8

> But the one whose scales of good deeds are light,

Then comes the other group: “But the one whose scales of good deeds are light”. Their calamity is not the loss of money that can be replaced, or an illness for which a treatment might be sought. It is that their deeds come to an end while the balance remains light. The image is deeply moving: deeds are placed on the scales one after another until none remain, yet nothing comes that tips the balance. This is regret that can no longer be remedied. Every day before the weighing is therefore an opportunity to add something of real worth. Do not dismiss a small good deed or postpone repentance, because you do not know which deed will carry the greatest weight with Allah.

## 101:9

> his abode will be the abyss[4].

“his abode will be the abyss[4].” Several explanations of this expression have been mentioned. [The Arabic word ummuhu (أُمُّهُ) literally means “his mother”.] One explanation takes it to mean the home and refuge that will receive him as a mother receives her child—but this refuge is the abyss. Another understands it as his being thrown headfirst into the Fire. A third finds in the expression a sense of lament. [The Arabic idiom ثكلته أمه means “may his mother be bereaved of him”; it expresses grief at a person’s fate.] These explanations share the image of a fate that has become inseparable from the person: the Fire takes charge of him after his balance has proved light. Anyone who seeks a refuge in this world at any cost should ask: what refuge am I preparing for myself after death?

## 101:10

> And how do you know what it is?

“And how do you know what it is?” This repeats the question of awe with which the surah began. Just as the striking calamity exceeds your imagination, so does the abyss. Do not diminish the meaning of the Fire simply because you have heard its name many times. Repetition may make a word familiar, while the reality it names admits of no such familiarity. The question awakens the imagination once more before the final answer, so that the ending does not pass over the heart like a well-known name that no longer stirs fear.

## 101:11

> It is a Blazing Fire.

The answer is: “It is a Blazing Fire.” Every fire gives off heat. [The Arabic description ḥāmiyah (حَامِيَةٌ) emphasizes intense heat.] When Allah singles out this Fire with that description, it conveys a heat beyond comparison with the fire of this world. This is not a fire that settles and then dies down. It is the final consequence of the weightless way of life that a person chose in response to revelation. The surah begins with a sound that strikes the heart and ends with fiercely burning fire. Between them stands one practical question: what will make your balance heavy? A person who truly believes in this scene does not need to change the Quran’s message to suit those who deny it. It is enough to present the truth with certainty and compassion, beginning by weighing one’s own deeds before they are weighed in judgment.
