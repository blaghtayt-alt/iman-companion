## 1:1

> In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.

The surah begins in Allah’s name. [The Arabic particle bāʾ in بسم conveys accompaniment and seeking help.] It is as though the servant begins the whole journey asking not to be left to their own devices, seeking Allah’s help from the very first moment. Bringing together the names “the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful” at the opening introduces the heart to its Lord through His mercy. The servant thus approaches the Quran and life itself seeking Allah’s help and mercy in both the great matters and the smallest details of life.

## 1:2

> All praise be to Allah, the Lord of the worlds,

Praise is broader than gratitude for a particular blessing: it is offered with love and submission to Allah, for who He is, His names and attributes, the beauty of His creation, and His blessings. The words “the Lord of the worlds” convey more than ownership alone. [The Arabic word Rabb also conveys nurturing, governing, and continually caring for creation.] All the worlds grow, move, and receive their provision under His lordship, and each is a sign pointing to Him. Praise then becomes a conscious response to seeing the effects of His lordship in everything.

## 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful,

Mercy is mentioned before the Day of Reckoning so that the servant knows that the Lord who nurtures them and governs their affairs is “the Most Compassionate” and “the Most Merciful”. [One explanation of the two Arabic names is that al-Raḥmān denotes vast, immense mercy, while al-Raḥīm denotes mercy that reaches the servant in the smallest details of their circumstances.] The servant should therefore not think that they need to turn to Allah for help only in major matters: they need Him in every detail of this life and the Hereafter.

## 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment.

Mercy does not cancel the reckoning. There is a day when deeds are brought forward for judgment and their recompense becomes clear. [The Arabic word dīn, translated here as “Judgment”, brings together reckoning and recompense with the way of life by which people are judged. There is no reckoning without a standard that has already been made known.] On that day, every passing form of ownership and power over which people contend in this world falls away, and it becomes clear that all dominion belongs to Allah. Wrongs are then redressed and rights restored, resolving the difficulty that arises when this world is viewed in isolation, without the complete picture of the Hereafter.

## 1:5

> You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.

“You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.” [Placing the Arabic pronoun iyyāka, meaning “You”, before each verb expresses exclusivity: worship is for Allah alone, and help is sought from Him alone.] Belief in Allah’s oneness is therefore more than acknowledging Him as God and Creator. It means giving neither personal desire, social pressure, nor the approval of any created being precedence over His command. Yet the servant also admits their inability: they want to worship, but cannot do so by their own strength and power, and so ask for help from the One they worship. [The plural forms “we” connect the journey to Allah with a community that worships and seeks His help, rather than with an isolated self closed in upon itself.]

## 1:6

> Guide us to the straight path,

The greatest request repeated in every unit of prayer is for guidance, even though the person asking is already standing before Allah. They are not asking only to begin the journey, but to remain steadfast, grow in guidance, and be safe from straying until the end. “Guide us to the straight path” presents guidance as continual accompaniment along the whole path, from the beginnings of faith and worship until meeting Allah. Someone who has already received a measure of guidance therefore never outgrows the need for it. Rather, their fear of losing it and their longing for its fullness grow stronger.

## 1:7

> the path of those whom You have blessed; not of those who incurred Your Wrath, or of those who went astray.

The path is not an abstract idea: people have gone before us and walked it. The servant asks to be joined to those whom Allah has blessed. At the same time, they fear two ways of going astray: knowing the truth but acting against it and so deserving anger, or proceeding without knowledge, following desire, and losing the way. Jews are mentioned as an example of the first and Christians as an example of the second. The prayer thus remains a request for both beneficial knowledge and sincere action, together with a daily fear of losing either and straying from the path.
