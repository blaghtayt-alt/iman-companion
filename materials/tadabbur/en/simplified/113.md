## 113:1

> Say, “I seek refuge with the Lord of the daybreak,

Scholars differ over whether this surah was revealed in Makkah or Madinah; a view that it was revealed more than once has also been reported. Its meanings are needed in every condition: weakness and strength, morning and evening, ease and hardship. The opening command, ‘Say’, makes seeking refuge something to proclaim, teach and repeat, rather than a lesson learned once and then forgotten. Its placement after Al-Ikhlas also has a clear practical significance. 

Whoever is certain that Allah is One in His essence, attributes and actions, and that all needs are brought to Him, naturally turns to Him alone when afraid. [The name الصمد in Al-Ikhlas conveys, among its meanings, the One to whom all turn with their needs.] The words ‘I seek refuge’ express more than a passing request for protection. [The Arabic أعوذ conveys taking refuge; its root has also been explained in terms of clinging and remaining close.] It is as though the servant were pleading: My Lord, do not leave me to myself for even a moment. 

Then comes ‘the Lord of the daybreak’. [الفلق most commonly means daybreak. Its root conveys splitting open what was closed, allowing light or life to emerge.] Night gives way to day, a plant breaks through the ground, and a child emerges into the world. Before naming the evils, the surah opens with an image of hope: the One who brings light out of the world's darkness can dispel the darkness of your troubles. [The word الرب, Lord, also conveys care and governance.] This is fitting when asking for protection: the Lord directs affairs gently and gradually, just as night does not become day all at once, but gives way to light little by little.

## 113:2

> from the harm of all what He has created;

After identifying the One with whom refuge is sought, the surah gives a comprehensive request for protection: ‘from the harm of all what He has created’. Among the creatures around us are things that benefit us and things that harm us; we may even find evil within ourselves from which we need protection. This does not mean that Allah's acts or commands are evil. Rather, created things can contain dangers and harms that we do not fully understand and do not always know how to avert. 

Here an important meaning becomes clear: the Creator knows where a thing's harm lies and how to turn it away, whereas a person may try to remedy a danger they do not understand and make it worse. We may also want something we think is good when Allah knows it is harmful to us. The surah therefore begins with holding fast to the Creator before naming particular forms of evil. The servant acknowledges their weakness before a web of dangers that they cannot entirely understand or control.

## 113:3

> from the harm of darkening [night] when it spreads around,

The most widely mentioned interpretation of ‘from the harm of darkening [night] when it spreads around’ is night as its darkness deepens and enters every opening. An interpretation connected with the moon in a state associated with darkness has also been reported. [In the Arabic phrase غاسق إذا وقب, the verb وقب conveys entering: the image is of darkness penetrating places until no opening for light remains.] Hidden dangers become more frequent at such a time: an animal, a thief or someone intent on harm may move where human perception is weakened. 

It is part of Allah's mercy that life contains things that break the illusion of self-sufficiency. However strong people become, they cannot see in the dark as they please or ward off every danger alone. They take the precautions within their power, yet still need to seek refuge with Allah. [The words إذا وقب also intensify the image: darkness has entered and deepened, rather than merely appeared.] Yet the Lord of daybreak, who dispels the night, can relieve whatever has darkened His servant's life, however closed the way forward may seem.

## 113:4

> from the harm of the sorceresses[1] who blow on knots,

The words ‘from the harm of the sorceresses who blow on knots’ turn to an evil that is more hidden and intricate. [The Arabic النفاثات has been interpreted as women practising sorcery, or as souls that blow on knots. The published translation expresses the first interpretation.] The image is of blowing upon knots with the intention of causing harm. Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, relates the established account of sorcery affecting the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him (Sahih al-Bukhari 5765; Sahih Muslim 2189a). 

Yet sorcery, though real, has no independent power to harm anyone. Allah says: ‘But they could not harm anyone except by Allah’s permission’. One reflection here is the contrast between the confusion of perception or obstruction of action that sorcery may cause and the sound understanding and good action to which revelation leads. Abandoning the Quran opens the way to attachment to sources of darkness, whereas remaining close to Allah's words is a means of protection and of seeing clearly. 

The knots also suggest a connection with the surah's name. [العقد means knots, while الفلق conveys splitting open.] However tangled a difficulty becomes, the Lord of daybreak can open it up and unravel it. Reciting the surahs of refuge with conviction is therefore more than moving the tongue: it is seeking protection with the One who alone has power over harm and benefit.

## 113:5

> and from the harm of the envier when he envies."

The detailed list ends with an unseen evil that may come from those closest to us: ‘and from the harm of the envier when he envies’. Envy may remain within a person's heart, or it may suddenly become a desire to see another person's blessing disappear or to harm them. No one can monitor that moment or always anticipate it; even the envious person may not fully recognize what has stirred within them. The answer is therefore not to live in anxious suspicions and isolation, concealing every blessing, although discretion is appropriate in some circumstances. 

It is to keep seeking refuge with Allah. Envy also differs from the praiseworthy wish to have a blessing like someone else's: wanting something similar without wishing that they lose it is one thing; wanting their blessing to disappear is another. The surah's ending brings together the features of the evils already mentioned: darkness in which we cannot see what happens, sorcery worked through hidden knots, and envy whose emergence we cannot predict. 

Faced with dangers that practical precautions cannot fully control, the surah's whole meaning remains before us: take complete refuge with the Lord of daybreak and hold fast to Him, certain that He can split open every darkness and that protection comes from Him alone.
