Verse 1

All praise be to Allah, the Originator of the heavens and earth, Who made the angels messengers with two, three, or four wings. He increases in creation whatever He wills. Indeed, Allah is Most Capable of all things.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

The surah begins with praise of Allah. It teaches the heart to look at creation in relation to its Creator before becoming absorbed in its details: all good comes from Him, every blessing is by His grace, and He deserves praise in what He gives, what He withholds, and how He governs. The believer repeats this meaning in the opening supplication of prayer: “All good is within Your hands and evil does not stem from You.” [Muslim, 771]. No deficiency or purposelessness is therefore attributed to Allah, even when the wisdom in some of what He decrees is hidden from us.

The description “the Originator of the heavens and earth” then traces all existence back to the One who first brought it into being without a prior model. The root of the Arabic word translated “Originator” also carries the meaning of splitting, as in “When the sky breaks apart.” [82:1]. From the same root comes fitrah: the original nature with which a human being begins life. The Prophet ﷺ said: “There is none born but is created to his true nature (Islam).” [al-Bukhari, 1358; Muslim, 2658]. This fitrah has been explained in different ways: is it the original readiness to accept the truth, or the created nature in accord with worshipping Allah alone, which misguided upbringing then diverts? In either explanation, a human being does not begin independently of the Lord, but as a creature within whom Allah has placed signs that point to Him.

To understand originating creation, consider the difference between a building that a person constructs from existing stone, iron, and sand, and a tiny seed from which Allah brings forth a living plant, or a drop of reproductive fluid from which human organs and senses develop. The builder uses materials and properties that the builder did not create. However far researchers advance in knowledge, they work with things and laws that Allah has bestowed on creation. Assembling existing things does not become bringing them into existence from nothing. In the report of Busr ibn Jahhash, the Prophet ﷺ spat into his palm and then related from his Lord: “Do you think you can escape from My punishment, O son of Adam, when I have created you from something like this?” [Ibn Majah, 2707]. This brings the arrogant person back to the weakness of their own beginnings and reminds them that the One who originated them has power over them.

The verse moves from the vastness of creation to its diversity: “Who made the angels messengers with two, three, or four wings.” The angels are Allah’s servants and His messengers in the tasks He assigns them. Although they share their original nature and obedience, their wings and the measure of what they have been given differ. Sharing a kind or a function does not require identical attributes. A familiar example helps explain how people can have one thing in common yet differ in other ways, without drawing a comparison between angels and human beings. Two doctors practise the same profession, yet their abilities and the ways they help others can differ. The same is true of two teachers. Allah has said of human messengers: “Those messengers – We favored some of them over others.” [2:253].

Then “He increases in creation whatever He wills.” directs our attention beyond the number just mentioned. One explanation is that Allah increases the angels’ wings. Another understands the wording more broadly, to include whatever He wills of strength, a beautiful voice, eloquence, knowledge, and all other qualities of creation. Do not confine Allah’s power to a condition in which you have grown accustomed to seeing someone. He may increase that person’s understanding or set their affairs right in ways you did not expect. One example of Allah’s power to set a person’s affairs right is the report: “Mahdi is one of us, the people of the Household. Allah
will rectify him
in a single night.” [Ibn Majah, 4085; hasan].

The verse ends with “Indeed, Allah is Most Capable of all things.” Knowledge of the Creator thus becomes trust and hope, rather than cold information about the universe. The Quran nourishes this knowledge by setting creation, life, and diversity before the heart. Just as medicine that wards off illness cannot replace the food that sustains life, concentrating only on answering doubts cannot replace constant companionship with the verses that acquaint the servant with the Lord and revive love and reverence for Him.

Notes

In this invocation, evil in created things is distinguished from imperfection in Allah’s acts. Allah is the Creator of both good and evil.


Verse 2

Whatever mercy Allah opens up for people, none can withhold it; and whatever He withholds, none can release it thereafter, for He is the All-Mighty, All-Wise.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

After acquainting us with the One who originated creation and increases it as He wills, the verse acquaints us with the One who opens its doors of mercy: “Whatever mercy Allah opens up for people, none can withhold it”. It does not say that mercy consists only of wealth or good health. Mercy may be knowledge that dispels confusion, an act of obedience that Allah makes easy, a child, provision, or a tranquillity that money cannot buy. The Arabic verb for opening evokes a door that was shut and then opens. A person may think every way forward has ended, only for good to arrive from a direction they had never considered.

Allah’s mercy therefore cannot be measured by the place where His servant happens to be or by the outward means surrounding that servant. Abraham, peace be upon him, was cast into the fire, but by Allah’s command it became cool and safe for him. When he was cast into it, he said: “Allah is Sufficient for us, and He is the best Protector[84].” [al-Bukhari, 4563]. Those who lit the fire could not hold back the mercy that Allah had opened for him.

In Joseph’s story, peace be upon him, circumstances followed one another which, viewed separately, might each have seemed a road to ruin: separation from his father, being sold, entering the household of the Egyptian dignitary, and then prison. Yet Allah led him through these circumstances to a position of authority and a reunion with his family, until he said: “My Lord is gracious to whom He wills.” [12:100]. Mercy was not absent when the outcome remained hidden. Allah’s gentle care was at work in circumstances whose eventual outcome Joseph and his family could not fully foresee.

The Prophet ﷺ was with his companion in the cave when their pursuers drew close, but their proximity did not prevent Allah’s protection. Jonah, peace be upon him, was in layers of darkness from which he could not escape by his own devices, yet Allah heard his call and rescued him. These accounts do not promise believers the same form of deliverance in every trial. They teach that worldly means do not independently control what is withheld or given. Someone poor may live in peace, while another person owns a great deal without tasting rest in the heart. Allah is the One of whom the Quran says: “He is the One Who makes people laugh and weep[16]” [53:43]. A person who merely counts possessions and overlooks the mercy within them has not understood the worth of a blessing.

This is affirmed by what the Prophet ﷺ said to Ibn Abbas: “And know that if the Ummah were to gather together to benefit you with anything, they would not benefit you except with what Allah had already prescribed for you.” [al-Tirmidhi, 2516]. The servant continues to use the means available, but does not treat people’s hearts or their abilities as the storehouses of mercy.

Then comes the other side: “and whatever He withholds, none can release it thereafter”. If Allah withholds something, no one can release it independently of Him. Withholding what a person wants may itself be mercy, because receiving it might corrupt that person’s faith or increase their misery. Belief in this verse must not become an insistence that mercy can only take the form someone has chosen for themselves.

The hadith says: “How astonishing the believer's affair is! Indeed, all of his affairs are good”. The Prophet ﷺ then described the believer’s gratitude in prosperity and patience in adversity [Muslim, 2999]. Good is not confined to a change in circumstances that fulfils a person’s wishes. It may lie in the worship and submission that Allah enables within those circumstances. This is why “the All-Mighty, All-Wise” come together at the end of the verse: no one can overcome His giving or withholding, and neither occurs without wisdom.

This truth is not a lesson to be stated once and left behind. In the remembrance after rising from bowing, the worshipper says: “no one can withhold what Thou givest or give what Thou withholdest” [Muslim, 477]. The worshipper repeats this while praising the Lord, until the heart learns to knock on the doors of practical means without forgetting the One who holds their opening in His hands.

Notes

[84] This is one of the greatest Dhikr (remembrance) during times of hardship. Ibn ‘Abbās (رضي الله عنهما) reported: “Hasbunā Allāh wa ni‘ma al-Wakīl (Allah suffices us, He is the best of Protectors), was said by Abraham (عليه السلام) when he was thrown in the fire and was said by Muhammad (ﷺ) when it was said: “People have regrouped for you, so fear them”, their belief grew firmer and they said: “Allah suffices us, He is the best of Protectors”.” [Al-Bukhārī: 4563]

Verse 3

O people, remember Allah’s favor upon you. Is there any creator other than Allah who gives you provision from heaven and earth? None has the right to be worshiped except Him. How can you then be deluded?

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

The verse addresses everyone: “remember Allah’s favor upon you.” A person may live surrounded by blessings, yet look only at the last hand through which a blessing reached them and forget the One who created it and brought it to them. A loaf of bread bought from a baker is not the baker’s work alone. Before it came crops, water and soil, a farmer, a miller and someone to transport it. Each uses materials and abilities whose origin they did not create.

Likewise, a patient who seeks a skilled doctor is making use of a lawful means. But the doctor did not create the patient’s body, the medicine, or its capacity to benefit the patient. The heart must not become so attached to the doctor that it forgets to ask its Lord and acknowledge its need of Him. Remembering here means more than a word on the tongue: when enjoying food, children, work and good health, we bring to mind that their origin and availability are from Allah, then thank Him for them.

The Quran asks a question that false objects of worship cannot answer: “Is there any creator other than Allah who gives you provision from heaven and earth?” Anyone claiming such authority should show what they have created of the sky, water, earth or living beings. When the king disputed with Abraham, peace be upon him, about his Lord, Abraham did not leave him with a claim he could use to deceive people. The Quran recounts: «Abraham said, “It is Allah Who brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west.” Thus the disbeliever was dumbfounded» [2:258]. The inability of someone contesting Allah’s lordship became plain when he was asked to exercise power over something he did not possess.

Pharaoh was similar, despite all the means of power he had amassed. When the signs came upon his people, they went back to Moses, asking him to call upon his Lord: “call upon your Lord for us, by virtue of the covenant He has made with you[18]” [43:49]. How can someone who cannot ward off affliction from himself and his people become a god for others?

What came before leads to this affirmation: “None has the right to be worshiped except Him.” The One who alone creates and provides is the One entitled to worship. A creature dependent on Him cannot be made His equal. The final question then awakens our innate disposition: “How can you then be deluded?” How can people be turned away from this clear truth? Habit, fear or imitation of others may bring about this reversal. A person sees the bounty of their Lord every day, yet directs their gratitude and their heart’s attachment elsewhere.

One way the Sunnah trains us to resist this heedlessness is by repeating the declaration of Allah’s oneness one hundred times in a day: “There is no god but Allah. He is One, and He has no partner with Him; to Him belong the sovereignty and praise, and He is competent over all things” [al-Bukhari, 3293; Muslim, 2691]. The problem is not that the tongue does not know the words. The heart needs to recall their meaning amid the many means and names competing for its attention. It acknowledges the Giver of blessings, makes use of the means He created, and does not give those means His place.

43:49 — [18] See 7:134.


Verse 4

If they reject you, there were messengers rejected before you; it is to Allah that all matters will be returned.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

People’s rejection of the truth does not mean that its proof has collapsed, or that the bearer of the message must change it to win their acceptance. Before the Prophet ﷺ, truthful messengers had faced the same rejection. Allah’s words, “If they reject you, there were messengers rejected before you”, place what he encounters within a familiar path, rather than leaving him to measure his message by his people’s satisfaction with it.

People ask questions for different reasons. We should not assume that everyone who asks is being stubborn. A person may recognise what is right, then find the consequences of following it burdensome: abandoning a habit, losing status, or opposing influential people. They go on asking for proof when the problem lies in their readiness to accept it. Neither the number of people who reject a teaching nor the volume of their objections is evidence that it is false.

Allah then returns every outcome to Himself: “it is to Allah that all matters will be returned.” A situation that today seems settled in favour of someone with power or wealth is not the end of the story. There is a return to the One who knows the truthfulness of the messengers and what their deniers concealed.

In this way, a caller to Allah learns to bring together conveying the message, compassion and steadfastness: explaining what they have to convey, bearing patiently with people, and recognising that their response is not under the caller’s control. They do not trade away the truth for immediate relief from rejection. The One who sent the Messenger will judge at the journey’s end, not the crowd confronting him along the way.


Verse 5

O people, indeed Allah’s promise is true, so do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver [Satan] deceive you concerning Allah.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

The affirmation “indeed Allah’s promise is true” prevents the heart from reducing reality to what it sees at present. Allah’s promises include resurrection, reckoning and recompense, and what He has told us about the outcome of faith and denial. A delay in the fulfilment of a promise does not make it an illusion, just as falsehood does not become true because it lasts for a time.

Yet worldly life is immediately perceptible and its pleasures are close at hand. It may so dominate our attention that the lasting future seems less important than the passing moment. Hence the warning: “so do not let the life of this world deceive you”. Do not take the enjoyment, health and power it offers as proof that you are safe from reckoning, or exchange what endures for what will soon perish.

Then comes a warning against a more dangerous deception: “nor let the Chief Deceiver [Satan] deceive you concerning Allah.” One of the best-known explanations of the Arabic word al-gharūr [the Chief Deceiver [Satan]] here is Satan. The warning also encompasses every deception that makes a person rely on a false idea of their Lord: telling themselves that His mercy means He will not call them to account, that a long respite is a sign of His approval, or that continuing in sin is easy as long as the door of repentance is mentioned.

Deception thus moves from making disobedience attractive to distorting our knowledge of Allah. Talk of mercy itself becomes an excuse to persist in sin instead of a reason to return to Him. The Quran relates how the jinn recognised this error, acknowledging that “the fool among us [i.e., Satan] used to say outrageous things about Allah[2]” [72:4]. Someone who turns away from reminders may come to feel secure in this invented image, until they become the companion of one who turns them away from the right path while they think they are guided. This is described in the verses about turning away from the remembrance of the Most Compassionate [43:36–38].

The remedy is to set hope right, not to close its door. Believers trust their Lord’s promise and turn to obeying Him and repenting, while fearing the consequences of disobedience. They do not demand that mercy cancel justice. When the realities of Paradise, Hell and resurrection take firm root in the heart, worldly enjoyment returns to its proper size: a person benefits from it without letting it deceive them about their final destination.

Notes

72:4 — [2] i.e., Satan tempted humans to believe that Allah has a wife and children, and that the angels are Allah's daughters.


Verse 6

Indeed, Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy. He only calls his followers to be among the inmates of the Blazing Fire.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

The verse moves from exposing deception to the response it requires: “Indeed, Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy.” Knowing of the enmity is not enough if a person answers their enemy’s call every time. That knowledge must show itself in resisting his whispering, returning to Allah’s command, and closing the entrances through which temptation repeatedly comes.

Satan does not always present evil under its own name. He addresses each person with what he expects will influence them: making them fear for their wealth, status or relationships, or making obedience seem so burdensome that they postpone it. In Sabrah ibn Abi Fakih’s report, Satan sits in the path of a person accepting Islam and reminds them of their forefathers’ religion. Then he sits in the path of emigration, making them fear leaving their land, and in the path of jihad, making them fear for what they leave behind [al-Nasa’i, 3134]. The objection changes, but the aim is the same: to turn the servant away from obeying what has become clear of their Lord’s command.

He may succeed by persuading a person to take one small step, then draw them into another, until in time they reach a state they once thought impossible for themselves. Protection therefore cannot rest merely on confidence in one’s willpower. An example is the Prophet’s ﷺ prohibition of a man being secluded with a non-mahram woman: “Behold! A man is not alone with a woman but the third of them is Ash-Shaitan.” [al-Tirmidhi, 2165]. Religious guidance addresses the entrance to temptation before it takes hold, rather than waiting for a person to boast that they will not weaken.

Yet Satan cannot force the servant to disobey. He will tell his followers: “I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you responded to me[11].” [14:22]. Mentioning the enemy does not cancel responsibility for our choices; it calls for greater vigilance.

Allah then reveals where this invitation leads: “He only calls his followers to be among the inmates of the Blazing Fire.” At first, the invitation may look like an attractive message promising pleasure, success or social acceptance. But the Quran shows what its attractive presentation conceals: where will those who follow this path end up? His followers are those who obey him, united in that obedience despite their different identities and times. A person may think their decision is entirely individual, while in reality they are sharing a path whose travellers make it look attractive to one another.

Someone who sees the Fire at the end of the invitation will not stop at its beautiful wrapping. Someone who takes Satan as an enemy does not need to try every entrance he uses before believing the warning.

Notes

14:22 —  [11] By obeying me instead of Him during life on earth.


Verse 7

Those who disbelieve will have a severe punishment, but those who believe and do righteous deeds will have forgiveness and a great reward.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

After explaining the destination towards which Satan wants to drive his followers, the verse distinguishes the two outcomes: “Those who disbelieve will have a severe punishment”, whereas “those who believe and do righteous deeds will have forgiveness and a great reward.” It is not enough to reject Satan with one’s tongue while abandoning the path of faith and righteous action. Salvation lies in a response that bears out one’s knowledge of the Lord.

Yet the verse does not describe believers as people who never make mistakes. It promises them forgiveness alongside reward. This opens the door of hope to someone who acts but falls short, who struggles against themselves and then slips. Do not make sin a reason to surrender to your enemy, or equate a lapse followed by repentance with disbelief in which its adherent persists. Believers return to Allah instead of turning their mistake into a path they defend. They then hope that Allah will cover their shortcomings and reward their good.

Mentioning forgiveness before reward reminds believers that their deeds never remove their need for pardon. Salvation is not a price they can demand of their Lord: their deeds are accepted through His bounty, and their shortcomings forgiven through His mercy. The verse thus brings together warning against the enemy and refusing to despair after a fall, as well as striving in obedience without becoming complacent about it.


Verse 8

Is one whose evil deed is made appealing to him so he deems it good [like one who is rightly guided]? It is Allah who causes to stray whom He wills and guides whom He wills. So do not let yourself collapse in grief for them. Allah is All-Knowing of what they do.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

The gravest form of deviation is not simply doing something wrong while knowing it is ugly. It is having one’s standard change until the wrong appears good: “Is one whose evil deed is made appealing to him so he deems it good [like one who is rightly guided]?” The deed has not become good; the doer’s perception has changed. They may then grow more energetic and enthusiastic while moving further away, because they no longer sense any need to return.

One example is making a decision that suits one’s desires, then seeking knowledge to justify it rather than test it. A person searches for an isolated opinion or a fragment torn from its context. They turn away whenever something contradicts their choice, but cling to whatever reassures them as though they had reached certainty. Following desire takes on the appearance of seeking evidence.

This shows the danger of turning away after clarification. Allah says: “Allah will not cause a people to go astray after He has guided them until He makes clear to them what they should avoid[85].” [9:115]. Of someone who opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear, He says: “We will leave him to what he has chosen[83]” [4:115]. Allah says: “It is Allah who causes to stray whom He wills and guides whom He wills.” These words must not be understood as injustice towards His servants, or as making clarification worthless. The whole matter is in His hands. He knows what the heart chooses and what it deserves, and He wrongs no one.

The address then turns to the Prophet’s ﷺ heart: “So do not let yourself collapse in grief for them.” Someone who knows the worth of guidance feels pain at seeing people turn away from it. Yet they cannot force guidance upon them or turn their grief into new life for those people’s hearts. Compassion requires conveying the message and patience, rather than the caller destroying themselves with sorrow after doing what they can.

The caller also needs their own certainty to be renewed through revelation. Of the Quran’s gradual revelation, Allah says: “so that We may strengthen your heart thereby” [25:32]. If the Messenger ﷺ receives strength from his Lord, how can those conveying his message do without it?

The verse closes with: “Allah is All-Knowing of what they do.” He knows the obstruction they plan and the motives behind their words. The caller need not drain their heart by pursuing every stratagem. Their task is to explain and act well, and to examine themselves so that their own desires do not make their deeds look good to them too. They then entrust what lies beyond their power to the One who knows both the outward and the hidden.

Notes

4:115 — [83] i.e., make him responsible for his choice.


9:115 — [85] Allah does not hold people accountable for what they did before its prohibition was made clear to them, such as seeking forgiveness for polytheist relatives before this was forbidden. Al-Bukhāri cites this verse in the chapter on fighting the Khārijites after proof has been established against them. Abdullah ibn ‘Umar (رضي الله عنهما) reported regarding al-Harūriyyah (the Khārijites) that the Prophet (ﷺ) said: “They will go out of Islam as an arrow darts out of the game’s body.” [Al-Bukhāri: 6932]


Verse 9

It is Allah Who sends the winds which stir up clouds, then We drive them to a dead land and revive therewith the earth after its death. Such will be the Resurrection.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

A person sees dry ground with no sign of life. Then the winds move, stir up clouds, and water is brought to it; suddenly it grows after having been dead. The Quran presents this recurring scene to say: “Such will be the Resurrection.” The One who revived land you thought empty of life is able to bring people to life after their death. Rain does not cease to be a sign because it is familiar. What has hidden our wonder is that our eyes have grown accustomed to the result and no longer recall the power that brought it about.

The Arabic expression rendered “stir up clouds” uses the present tense amid past-tense verbs, drawing attention to the winds in motion as they raise the clouds, as though the scene were unfolding before you now. It then attributes the driving and reviving to Allah: “then We drive them to a dead land and revive therewith the earth after its death.” This may be among the mercy whose opening was mentioned at the surah’s beginning: water is brought to a place that cannot bring it to itself.

The scene also offers a lesson about the life of hearts, while resurrection remains the direct meaning of the verse’s ending. A heart may dry out through long heedlessness, then Allah brings it an admonition, a verse or a righteous person, and it comes to life. To picture this, someone heedless may pass by a person reciting the Quran at night and a verse falls into their heart. Or they hear words they had not intended to hear, and a door of repentance opens. The bearer of those words did not create the response, just as the cloud does not independently give life.

The image of clouds stirred by the wind, then carrying water elsewhere, reminds those who bear knowledge to first equip themselves with what benefits before passing it on. They should not rush to have an effect while they themselves are not yet filled with guidance. Elsewhere Allah describes “heavy clouds” driven to dead land [7:57]. Good reaches those in need after being carried by what Allah has made a means for it. Likewise, calling to Allah involves more than movement and talk. It means bearing the meanings of revelation and striving to convey them, while acknowledging our need for Allah to make them beneficial.

People do not all receive guidance in the same state, just as lands differ in benefiting from rain. One must neither despair of a heart that seems dead nor become confident in one’s own power to revive it. We strive to awaken what remains of the innate disposition and present the truth clearly, knowing that life and guidance come from Allah. In the same way, the Resurrection with which the verse ends belongs only to the One who revived the earth.


Verse 10

Whoever seeks honor, then all honor belongs to Allah alone. To Him ascend good words, and righteous deeds raise them[1]. But those who plan evil deeds will have a severe punishment, and the plans of such people will perish.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; first session, verses 1–10.

A person may recognise the truth but hesitate to follow it for fear of losing their standing among people, or leaving a group in which they find protection and pride. The verse takes that fear back to the root of the matter: “Whoever seeks honor, then all honor belongs to Allah alone.” A creature’s power or prestige is temporary; the honour that does not pass away belongs to Allah. Do not seek it by disobeying Him, then expect someone who does not even possess control over themselves to preserve it for you.

After showing the source of honour, the verse explains what ascends to Allah: “To Him ascend good words, and righteous deeds raise them[1].” One interpretation is that righteous deeds raise good words: action confirms speech, so declarations of Allah’s oneness, remembrance and good words do not remain detached from a person’s life. This does not mean that someone who falls short in some deeds should abandon remembrance, or that every good word they speak is invalid. Rather, the good of their tongue should be completed by the good of their actions. They must beware of calling people to something they deliberately contradict.

Allah says: “Do you enjoin righteousness upon others while you forget yourselves” [2:44]. Shuʿayb, peace be upon him, said: “I do not want to go against what I am forbidding you.” [11:88]. Another interpretation is that good words, foremost among them the declaration of Allah’s oneness, raise deeds. A deed is not accepted merely for its outward form when the foundation of faith is missing. These two interpretations bring together speech’s need for action that confirms it, and action’s need for sound belief and a good intention.

An application of this meaning is that supplication does not justify abandoning the effort a servant has been commanded to make. Words calling others to good need conduct that testifies to their sincerity. A person should not ask for victory with their tongue while undermining its means through discord or injustice.

On the other side, the people of falsehood are not idle: they are “those who plan evil deeds”, arranging its means and making its appearance attractive. At times this may seem more effective than the word of truth. Yet the Quran does not measure an action’s worth by its outward activity. Some work ascends to Allah, while their schemes “will perish”: they fail and come to ruin, without yielding the goal their authors imagined. To picture this ruin, a landowner may work hard on the land and then find no useful harvest. Not every effort makes a profitable trade.

Later in the surah, those who recite the Quran, pray and spend are explicitly contrasted with such loss: “hoping for a deal that will suffer no loss” [35:29]. Anyone seeking honour should therefore examine how they seek it. Do they bear good words confirmed by deeds, or seek an immediate position through a scheme whose harm will return to them?

Notes

[1] To Allah, i.e., righteous deeds are confirmation of what is uttered by the tongue.


Verse 11

It is Allah Who created you from dust[2], then from a drop of semen, and then He made you into pairs[3]. No female conceives nor gives birth without His knowledge. None is given long life or cut short of his life except that it is in a Record[4]. That is surely easy for Allah.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

The verse takes people back to the beginning of their journey, before they take pride in their strength or long experience: “It is Allah Who created you from dust[2], then from a drop of semen, and then He made you into pairs[3].” Humanity’s origin goes back to dust; people then reproduce from a drop of semen, and Allah makes them male and female. From this origin follow countless forms of bodies, temperaments and lifespans. A person did not choose their beginning or control their own development as they passed from stage to stage. Yet they may forget all this once some means of power become available to them.

The verse then opens a door onto what is hidden within creation: “No female conceives nor gives birth without His knowledge.” No new life lies outside Allah’s knowledge. No birth, loss or movement of a fetus in a distant place is hidden from Him. Human discoveries about some causes and stages of pregnancy do not give people comprehensive knowledge of every pregnancy or make them independent of their Creator.

That knowledge then extends to life after birth: “None is given long life or cut short of his life except that it is in a Record[4].” Lifespans, the time that passes from them, and the differences between people’s spans are not events lost without record. They are known and written. One reminder of the limits of our stay is the hadith: “The lifespan of my nation is between sixty to seventy, and the least of them are those who surpass that.” [al-Tirmidhi, 3550]. This does not fix every individual’s time of death. It reminds us that even what we call a long life has a nearby end, and that those who live longer or shorter lives never pass beyond Allah’s knowledge. He says: “No soul can die except with Allah’s permission at a destined time[65].” [3:145].

The interweaving of pregnancy, birth, provision and appointed lifespans may seem immensely complex to us. The verse concludes it with: “That is surely easy for Allah.”

One account that brings the ease of Allah’s ordering of affairs close to the heart concerns a man from the Children of Israel who borrowed a thousand dinars. When the lender asked for a witness, he replied that Allah was sufficient as a witness. When asked for a guarantor, he replied that Allah was sufficient as a guarantor. The lender accepted and lent him the money until an agreed date. The debtor travelled, but when repayment fell due he could find no boat to cross the sea. He hollowed out a piece of wood, put the money and a letter for the lender inside, sealed it tightly, asked Allah to deliver it on his behalf, and cast it into the sea. The lender went out looking for a boat, hoping that the debtor’s messenger might arrive aboard it. He found the wood and took it as firewood for his family; when he sawed it open, he found the money and letter. The debtor did not stop with what he had done. He kept looking for a boat and then arrived with another thousand dinars to repay the loan. The lender told him that Allah had already delivered what he had sent in the wood [al-Bukhari, 2291].

The story shows things neither man could arrange alone: the sea carrying the wood, the creditor going out and finding it, choosing to take it and opening it, until the debt reached its owner. The borrower made a sincere effort to repay the debt with what he had. He then saw how Allah had arranged things in a way he could never have managed himself. The One who directs these means is able to direct all creation in ways hidden from us. Do not let your inability to imagine how something happens make you dismiss His power.

The Prophet ﷺ therefore instructed those who supplicate to ask firmly and with great yearning, saying: “for there is nothing so great in the eye of Allah which He cannot grant.” [Muslim, 2679]. A request’s greatness in your own eyes does not make it great for the One whose knowledge encompasses births, lifespans and all the means at work. Ask Him, act within what He has permitted, and entrust the completion of affairs to His knowledge and wisdom.

Verse 12

The two bodies of water are not alike: one is fresh and palatable, pleasant to drink, and the other is salty and bitter. Yet from each you eat fresh meat and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships traverse their way through both, so that you may seek His bounty and so that you may give thanks.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

The verse sets before us two kinds of water that differ in taste and drinkability: one is very fresh and easy to drink, the other intensely salty. Despite this difference, both yield provision; people extract ornaments from them, and ships cut through the water to carry people towards their needs. Diversity does not indicate weakness in the Creator or disorder in His creation. It reveals His power to give what we see as one substance different properties and benefits.

Not every benefit depends on something taking the form we prefer. Salt water that we do not drink is not worthless. Its difference from fresh water does not prevent us from acknowledging its usefulness.

One interpretation of the contrast compares the believer to fresh water and the disbeliever to salt water. On this reading, another lesson emerges: benefiting from someone in worldly matters must not be confused with judging their belief to be true. A nonbeliever may treat people well or establish something that benefits them. Their benefit should neither be denied because of their different religion nor treated, by itself, as confirmation of that religion. The Prophet ﷺ explained that Allah does not wrong a believer regarding a good deed, and that a disbeliever is given worldly sustenance for good done for Allah, but for someone who reaches the Hereafter in disbelief, no good deed remains to be rewarded for [Muslim, 2808]. Recompense is connected to faith; not everything granted in this world is a testimony of approval.

Benefit may even come to the truth from a direction that never intended it. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Verily Allah helps this faith even by a sinful person.” [al-Bukhari, 3062; Muslim, 111]. Consider Pharaoh gathering the magicians and the people to establish his falsehood: that very gathering became the setting for Moses’ sign to appear and for the magicians to believe. Allah used what Pharaoh intended as obstruction to reveal the truth. Do not confine the means of good to your expectations, or equate a sinful person with a righteous one because some good came through the former.

Another lesson is that people’s temperaments differ. Some are gentle, approachable and easy to deal with. The hadith praises these qualities in describing those protected from the Fire: “Every person who is near (to people), amicable, and easy (to deal with).” [al-Tirmidhi, 2488]. Others have a firmness that can help when resolve is needed, provided it is governed by justice. People need not all become copies of one another, and no one should make their own temperament the measure of truth.

Another reading of the comparison rebukes turning away: even salt water yields provision and benefits, so how can a heart close itself to all good? This resembles the mention of stones from which rivers gush, contrasted with hard hearts [2:74]. The point is not to deny every worldly benefit from a nonbeliever, but to warn against hardness that prevents benefiting from guidance.

The image “And you see the ships traverse their way through both” returns us to a tangible blessing. Ships cut through water because of properties Allah has placed in it and in their materials. People build bridges and homes and make vessels by relying on properties they know and take into account. Without the degree of regularity Allah has given materials, the construction and use we know would not be possible. Knowledge of a cause is a doorway to thanking its Creator, rather than a veil hiding Him.

The verse’s ending therefore joins effort and gratitude: “so that you may seek His bounty and so that you may give thanks.” You travel, work and discover, knowing that the knowledge and industry you build rest on a prior bounty that you did not originate.


Verse 13

He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and subjected the sun and the moon, each running its course for an appointed term. Such is Allah, your Lord. To Him belongs the dominion, whereas those whom you invoke besides Him do not own so much as the membrane of a date stone.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

Signs of Allah’s subjection of creation unfold before our eyes every day, though habit may conceal them: night enters day and day enters night, the share of each changes, and the sun and moon run towards an appointed term. This ordering has precision and gradual change, rather than a chaos in which life could not exist. The One who upholds this order is not powerless over the small creature who disputes His lordship.

Reflecting on gradual change also offers believers a lesson in how people are nurtured. Not every reform comes into being complete in a moment. Its foundations may accumulate through generations, and one person completes a building another began. If someone makes living to see the final result a condition for working, they abandon their share of good. If they imagine all history begins with them, they fail to recognise those who came before.

The Prophet’s ﷺ comparison of himself and the earlier prophets shows how one person’s work can complete what another has built: a man built a beautiful house, leaving only the place of one brick. People admired it; the Prophet ﷺ was that brick and the last of the prophets [al-Bukhari, 3535; Muslim, 2286]. It is therefore mistaken to wait for a lone hero to reform the community while everyone else watches. A leader is themselves formed within a society and builds upon earlier work, just as a change towards corruption may begin small and then take hold.

A path being true does not mean that every day on it is free of loss. When Heraclius asked Abu Sufyan about the fighting between his people and the Prophet ﷺ, he described its alternating fortunes. Heraclius explained that messengers are tested and then the final outcome is theirs [al-Bukhari, 2804]. As events follow one another, belief must not become dependent on the result of each event. A person must not stand with the truth only when it wins, then abandon it when tested.

Nor does the sun’s regular course mean that it acts independently of its Creator’s command. The Prophet ﷺ told of its prostration and seeking permission [al-Bukhari, 3199; Muslim, 159]. This is a report about the unseen; people cannot determine how it occurs from the movement they observe. In the account of a prophet on an expedition, who approached the town around the afternoon prayer, he said to the sun: “You are under Allah's Order and I am under 
Allah's Order”. Then he asked his Lord to hold it back for them, and it was held until Allah granted victory [al-Bukhari, 3124]. The One who set the regular order in motion can grant a prophet a special sign. What people are accustomed to does not set a limit on His power.

The Quran also recalls the sun’s course by the young men’s cave: “You would have seen the sun when it rose, it would incline away from their Cave to the right; and when it set, it would turn away from them to the left[11]” [18:17]. What appears to people as a distant heavenly body is included in Allah’s ordering of affairs for His servants.

After this vast scene comes the decisive comparison: “Such is Allah, your Lord. To Him belongs the dominion, whereas those whom you invoke besides Him do not own so much as the membrane of a date stone.” The Arabic word qiṭmīr [the membrane of a date stone] refers to the thin covering of the stone. This is not a small difference between two nearly equal powers. It is the difference between a Lord who subjects and possesses the heavenly bodies, and supposed objects of worship that independently own not even this tiny thing. How can the heart leave the Owner of the sun and moon to ask a creature for what only its Creator can do?

18:17 — [11] As such they would get all the air and ventilation they needed to sustain.


Verse 14

If you call upon them, they cannot hear your call, and even if they were to hear, they could not respond to you. On the Day of Resurrection they will disown your associating them as partners with Allah[5]. And none can inform you like the All-Aware.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

After denying ownership to the supposed objects of worship, the verse shows their inability in the very matter of supplication: “If you call upon them, they cannot hear your call, and even if they were to hear, they could not respond to you.” Inanimate objects do not hear, and a creature does not possess unrestricted hearing that encompasses everyone calling upon it. Even supposing that it hears does not establish its ability to answer. How many people hear a nearby cry for help yet cannot remove the harm?

The concern here is supplication as worship and seeking what only Allah can do. It is not asking someone present for help within their capacity in the ordinary means of living. Do not make love for a righteous person or reverence for a prophet proof that they possess the power their Lord alone holds.

The verse then reveals something beyond the disappointment of an immediate request: “On the Day of Resurrection they will disown your associating them as partners with Allah[5].” They will disavow what their worshippers attributed to them. When Allah asks Jesus, peace be upon him, “did you tell people, ‘Take me and my mother as gods besides Allah?’”, his answer is: “Glory be to You! It is not for me to say what I have no right.” [5:116]. A prophet does not support those who elevate him beyond his proper place. He declares his servitude and exalts his Lord above having a partner.

In the Hereafter, someone may see a hope collapse after setting their heart on it and spending a lifetime in pursuit of it. It is related that Umar, may Allah be pleased with him, saw a monk and wept, recalling “overburdened and weary[1],” and “as they burn in a scorching Fire.” [88:3–4; Ibn Kathir, Musnad al-Faruq]. The lesson of abundant toil cannot be separated from whether the path on which it was spent is sound. When we worship, then, we should look as closely at what guides us as we do at how hard we have worked.

The verse concludes: “And none can inform you like the All-Aware.” A human may be called an expert after experiences in which they err, get things right and learn. Allah’s knowledge is never preceded by ignorance and needs no experiment. When He tells you of these supposed deities’ inability and their stance on the Day of Resurrection, do not put an inherited image or an unfounded hope before His report. The One who knows the hidden and the final outcome is showing you now what benefits you, before mistaken hope turns into regret.

[5] Or your worship of them.

88:3 — [1] They will be burdened with the heavy shackles in the Hereafter, or they performed deeds in their worldly life which were bereft of faith or not done for the sake of Allah.


Verse 15

O people, it is you who are in need of Allah, whereas Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

The address puts the reality of all people in its proper place: “it is you who are in need of Allah”. Need is not a condition that appears during calamity and disappears when circumstances improve. A person needs Allah for their very existence, their body’s continued life, their heart’s movement, and their ability to sleep, wake and get up for work. They may possess money without being able to buy a breath when their chest tightens, sleep when it eludes them, or guidance when the paths become confusing.

Part of this need is that the means people rely on are not independent of Him. They are created, just as people are, and work through what Allah has allowed within them.

Remembrance therefore trains the heart to acknowledge its need during ordinary times, not only crises. At bedtime, a person says: “In Your Name, O my Lord, I place my side (on the bed), and by You I will lift it.” [al-Bukhari, 6320; Muslim, 2714]. In the morning: “O Allah, by You we have reached the morning, and by You we have reached the evening; by You we live, and by You we die” [al-Tirmidhi, 3391]. In the opening supplication of prayer: “My (power as well as existence) is due to Thee (Thine grace) and I turn to Thee (for supplication).” [Muslim, 771].

When hearing the invitations to prayer and success, the response is: “There is no might and no power except with Allah.” [Muslim, 385]. Even moving towards obedience needs help; it does not rest on a strength the servant claims for themselves. Even the food bought with their money and earned through their work recalls this need when they say after eating: “All praise is due to Allah who fed me this and granted it as provision to me, without any effort from me nor power” [al-Tirmidhi, 3458]. Their strength, earnings and purchased food are all gifts from Allah. This does not deny that they moved and made an effort.

Once this is settled in the heart, declaring one’s need is no longer an artificial humility contradicting what one inwardly believes. It describes reality. The beginning of Surah al-ʿAlaq recalls creation and teaching, then warns: “Indeed, man transgresses
when he deems himself to be self-sufficient.” [96:6–7]. The person’s affliction is not that they have actually become independent of Allah, but that they see themselves that way.

In contrast: “whereas Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.” Our obedience does not strengthen Him, nor does our disobedience weaken Him. He does not need our worship in order to accept and reward it.

To recognise how small the deeds that impress us are, recall what the Prophet ﷺ told of al-Bayt al-Maʿmur: seventy thousand angels enter it each day and never return to it [al-Bukhari, 3207; Muslim, 162]. He also told of the multitude of angels prostrating in the heavens: “There is no spot, the size of four fingers in them, except that there is an angel placing his forehead in it, prostrating to Allah.” [al-Tirmidhi, 2312]. Allah does not need a great number of worshippers. All of them need Him to enable them and keep them in existence.

The danger of conceit can be pictured through someone who offers a small gift to a king, then waits for people to esteem them greatly because of it. On seeing what others offer, they recognise their poor judgment. What, then, of someone who treats their Creator as indebted to them for obedience they could only perform with His help? The purpose is to free obedience from the thought of doing Allah a favour, not to diminish its value. Even a little grows in worth through His acceptance and bounty. He has no need of it, while the servant needs Him.

A request beyond one person’s ability looks small when put to someone who possesses its means. Likewise, we must not measure the breadth of Allah’s power by the narrowness of our own; to Allah belongs the highest comparison. His self-sufficiency is joined to praise: He is praised for His perfection and generosity. He accepts from His needy servant and appreciates their good, although He receives no benefit from them.

Knowing our need of Him does not mean humiliation before everyone. It brings an honour that frees us from servitude to those who are needy like ourselves, and gathers the heart upon the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.


Verse 16

If He wills, He can eliminate you and bring in a new creation,

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

After explaining people’s need and Allah’s self-sufficiency, the verse shows a consequence of that self-sufficiency: “If He wills, He can eliminate you and bring in a new creation,” [35:16]. Our existence is not necessary for His dominion, nor is our community’s survival a right we can impose on Him. The One who brought us into existence can remove us and create others. This answers those who become so impressed with themselves that they think good cannot continue without them, regard their deeds as a favour to their Lord, or feel safe about their outcome merely because they belong to a believing community.

The Quran makes clear that no one can resist Allah’s power, even those whom people wrongly exalt: Say, “Who has the power to prevent Allah, if He chose to destroy the Messiah, son of Mary, his mother, and all those who are on earth?” [5:17].

There is also a warning to society against treating the presence of righteous people as a guarantee that excuses it from resisting corruption. When Zainab bint Jahsh, may Allah be pleased with her, asked: “would we be perished in spite of the fact that there would be good people amongst us?”, the Prophet ﷺ replied: “Of course, but only when the evil predominates.” [al-Bukhari, 3346; Muslim, 2880]. It is not enough to feel reassured by the righteousness of some individuals while allowing evil to spread without doing what one can to put things right.

This includes Ibn Umar’s report about immorality appearing among a people until they practise it openly, followed by plague and diseases unknown to their forebears [Ibn Majah, 4019]. The spread of sin is not detached from a society’s life and the effects of its deeds.

The possibility of being replaced does not invite us to sit idle on the pretext that others will do the work. It calls us to seize the honour of being employed in His obedience while we are here, knowing that the favour is His when He enables us to act; we are not doing Him a favour.


Verse 17

for that is not difficult for Allah.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, verses 11–18.

“for that is not difficult for Allah.” Removing creation and bringing others in their place is neither impossible nor difficult for Him. Replacing a nation or bringing a generation to an end may seem enormous to someone who controls only a small part of the means. But the One who originated the heavens and earth and brought people forth from dust and a drop of fluid cannot be incapable of bringing a new creation.

People see generations succeeding one another, yet one of them may act as if their own existence keeps the world from coming to an end. The verse removes this illusion, and another: that prolonged oppression means Allah’s power has been overcome, or that the absence of immediate punishment proves it impossible. His power need not be displayed to every impatient person in the form they stipulate. His wisdom determines the appointed time.

Within this warning there is also a door of hope. As long as Allah has kept me alive, the door to action has not closed before me. I should neither use the respite to become more heedless nor belittle what I can put right on the pretext that I am only one person. Instead, I seek the help of the One for whom nothing is impossible and fulfil my duty before the opportunity He has graciously given me ends.


Verse 18

No bearer of burdens can bear the burden of another. If a heavily-laden soul calls for help to bear its burden, nothing of it will be carried, even if he is a close relative. You can only warn those who fear their Lord unseen[6] and establish prayer. Whoever purifies himself, he only does so for his own benefit. To Allah is the final return.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; second session, with a supporting passage from the fifth session.

People may share the effects of an event in this world, but in the reckoning of the Hereafter no one’s deeds are lost among another’s: “No bearer of burdens can bear the burden of another.” The burden is heavy, as though every soul comes carrying its own deeds, whose reality neither lineage nor group affiliation can conceal.

The picture becomes clearer in: “If a heavily-laden soul calls for help to bear its burden, nothing of it will be carried, even if he is a close relative.” In this life, someone may see their mother or child carrying belongings and take part of the load, or find someone willing to carry it all out of love or compassion. There, however, a person cannot transfer their sin to another’s record, and an appeal to kinship cannot remove any responsibility. The problem is not merely that a relative is unwilling to help: the reckoning does not work through exchanging burdens to evade justice.

Understanding this also means distinguishing between a widespread calamity and an equal recompense for everyone it affects. The Prophet ﷺ told of an army heading for the Kaaba that would be swallowed by the earth from first to last. Asked about people among them who did not belong to them, he explained that they would be resurrected according to their intentions [al-Bukhari, 2118]. One event brings them together in this life, while Allah’s knowledge distinguishes their situations in the Hereafter. This prevents both reliance on a righteous affiliation and certainty that everyone struck by a disaster is corrupt.

The verse then moves from responsibility to the qualities of those who benefit from reminders: “You can only warn those who fear their Lord unseen[6] and establish prayer.” This does not restrict the Messenger’s call to these people or mean leaving everyone else aside. It explains that warning bears fruit in a heart ready to receive it: one that fears its Lord without seeing the recompense, remains mindful of Him away from people’s eyes, and shows this in prayer. Hearing that goes no further than the ear differs from fear that moves a person to act.

The benefit returns to the one who acts: “Whoever purifies himself, he only does so for his own benefit.” Cleansing oneself of polytheism and sin, and growing through obedience, benefits the person; it adds nothing to their Lord’s self-sufficiency. Someone who teaches and calls them can show the way but cannot undertake purification on their behalf. The Prophet ﷺ was told: “You are not responsible for their guidance [O Prophet]” [2:272], and: “although you are not to be blamed if he does not purify himself[4].” [80:7]. The caller should not take upon themselves what is beyond their control, nor should the person called make someone else responsible for their own turning away.

All will return to Allah, so the verse ends: “To Allah is the final return.” With Him the reality of each person’s burden and what they put right within themselves will appear, without reliance on kinship or being deceived by appearances before others.

Verse 19

The blind and the seeing are not equal[7],

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

After explaining a person’s responsibility for purifying themselves, familiar comparisons help us understand spiritual insight and show that people’s paths are not equal: “The blind and the seeing are not equal[7],” [35:19]. Someone who sees the road and its dangers is unlike someone who cannot see them. Likewise, someone who sees the truth through revelation differs from someone who turns away from it. The earlier discussion of the two seas showed that different things can share a benefit. That does not erase the difference between faith and disbelief, or mean every choice leads to the same outcome.

The relationship between our innate disposition and revelation can be pictured through an eye and light. A healthy eye cannot see without light, just as abundant light cannot bring sight to one who lacks the ability to see. Saying that one has a good heart or a strong intellect cannot replace seeking the guidance that shows one where to go. A person may know a great deal about the means of worldly life, yet be confused about its greatest questions: Who created me? Why do I live? Where am I going?

Reflecting on the verse makes these distinctions clear again. The embellishment of falsehood can make someone so accustomed to their path that they defend their blindness and regard holding to the light as weakness or backwardness. A wealth of information alone is not insight. Insight means putting information in its proper place within Allah’s guidance, recognising through it what benefits or harms us, and choosing accordingly.

Verse 20

nor are the depths of darkness and the light[8],

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

A healthy eye is not enough when a person walks in darkness. The second comparison therefore follows the blind and the seeing: “nor are the depths of darkness and the light[8],” [35:20]. Revelation reveals the path, explains the value of what a person encounters, and returns them to the reality of their existence and destination. In Arabic, darkness appears in the plural while light is singular, fitting the many paths of misguidance in contrast with the one truth that comes from Allah. Desire, a doubt, deeply rooted traditions or fear of people may veil a person. These veils can accumulate into “layers of darkness, one above another.” [24:40]. By contrast, the light of our innate disposition meets the light of revelation, increasing the heart’s knowledge of its Lord and ability to distinguish the path.

Insight does not mean seeing every unseen reality with one’s eyes before believing. Revelation teaches a person what their senses cannot reach. It is wrong to treat everything one does not witness as nonexistent. It was obstinacy to say: “we will never believe you until we see Allah openly” [2:55]. Human benefit is not confined to what one can touch in the present moment. We remember, reflect and accept truthful reports, then work for what is to come.

Removing revelation from life thus extinguishes the light our will needs, even if we retain skill in manufacturing, management or calculation. These skills benefit people in their own fields, but cannot by themselves replace guidance in knowing our purpose and what deserves the expenditure of our lives. The distinction between darkness and light is not verbal ornament. It appears when a person makes a decision: What guidance will I follow, and what outcome do I hope for?

Verse 21

nor the shade and the scorching wind[9].

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

The next image is felt directly by the body: “nor the shade and the scorching wind[9].” [35:21]. Shade brings relief from intense heat, unlike scorching, harmful heat. The comparison moves from the ability to see the path to its effect on the traveller and their destination. One aspect compares faith and the mercy to which it leads with shade, and disbelief and its outcome with scorching heat. Outward worldly splendour must not become the judge of a person’s final destination. Someone may possess what impresses people yet have lost what they need most. A believer may be deprived of some comforts without that deprivation proving them worthless before their Lord.

The Prophet ﷺ said: “Were this world worth a wing of a mosquito in the sight of Allah, He would not have given the disbeliever a drink of water thereof.” [al-Tirmidhi, 2320]. Sharing in provision does not remove the distinctions revelation explains, nor do differences in wealth alone determine people’s worth. Correcting this measure, Allah says: “for a believing slave woman is better than a free polytheist, even though she may attract you.” [2:221]. Beauty or status may dazzle the eye, but Allah teaches it that faith has a value it must not overlook.

The adornment on earth does not last forever: Allah says, “And We will surely turn all that is on it into a barren land[7].” [18:8]. A believer benefits from what is lawful and does not deny its beauty, but does not sell the light that guides them for its glitter. They may find in faith a tranquillity that helps them through trials. This does not mean they never grieve or feel pain. Rather, they have a Lord in whom they hope, a purpose that gives their patience meaning, and an outcome not confined to the fierce heat they are passing through today.

18:8 — [7] It shows the transient nature of life in this world, so one should invest in it for what comes after death.


Verse 22

Nor are the living and the dead equal. Allah makes to hear whom He wills, but you cannot make to hear those who are in the graves[10].

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

The comparisons reach their culmination in life and death: “Nor are the living and the dead equal.” Faith gives life to the heart. Through it, a person knows their Lord, moves towards obedience, benefits from what they hear and carries good to others. Turning away disables this life even while the body works and pursues its interests. The comparison concerns the life of responding to guidance; it does not deny what a person can accomplish in worldly affairs.

Allah then ascribes the power to give life to the heart to Himself alone: “Allah makes to hear whom He wills, but you cannot make to hear those who are in the graves[10].” The point is not that the Messenger’s voice cannot reach people’s ears, but that he cannot create in them the hearing that accepts and benefits. Two people may hear the same verse: it opens a door to repentance for one, while the other shuts themselves against it.

The Prophet’s comparison with rain explains these different responses. Good land accepts the water and grows abundant grass and vegetation. Another kind holds the water, benefiting people through drinking, watering and cultivation. A third is barren ground that neither holds water nor grows grass. This pictures the guidance and knowledge Allah sent with His Prophet ﷺ: those who benefit from it and teach it, and those who pay it no heed [al-Bukhari, 79; Muslim, 2282]. The goodness in the rain is the same, but the recipients’ conditions differ. One patch of land’s failure to benefit does not justify saying water gives no life.

This teaches the caller patience and wise use of effort. They should not neglect a receptive person who wants to learn because they are constantly occupied with someone who wants only an argument, or suppose that success means everyone must respond. Like someone sowing seed, they should recognise where to plant and tend it, rather than exhaust it on unsuitable ground and blame it for not growing.

This is not a final judgment that particular individuals will never be guided. Allah gives life to hearts and may change them. It distinguishes the message a servant conveys from guidance beyond their control. The opening of Surah ʿAbasa corrects the order of attention between the one who feels self-sufficient and the one who comes striving and in fear [80:1–10]. Concern for people should be wise, rather than exhausting the caller while leaving the seeker of guidance with nothing.

Verse 23

You are but a warner.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

“You are but a warner.” This draws a clear boundary between the Messenger’s duty and what is beyond his power. He explains the truth and warns of the outcome of opposing it; he does not create faith in people’s hearts. It frees the caller from a burden they cannot bear. They should not attribute every response to their own skill or treat every rejection as a verdict that their message is false. When the nations were shown to the Prophet ﷺ, he told of a prophet with a small group and a prophet with no one accompanying him [al-Bukhari, 5705; Muslim, 220]. The truth of a message does not wait for numbers to certify it.

Yet this is no excuse for an inadequate explanation or harshness towards the people addressed. Warning is a responsible task: it should reach people clearly and in its proper place.

Someone who sees a person walking towards danger and warns them is not being pessimistic or expressing hatred. They are showing mercy before the person reaches destruction. Likewise, reminding people of death and recompense is not a desire to spoil their lives, but a call to use the life still in their hands. The bearer of the message should combine truthful warning with good communication, then recognise their human limits: call, remain patient and hope, without collapsing because something beyond their control did not happen as they wished.


Verse 24

We have sent you with the truth as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner. There has never been any community except that a warner came to it[11].

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

“We have sent you with the truth as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner.” The message combines explaining the path to salvation with warning of what destroys a person. The Messenger gives glad tidings of Allah’s forgiveness, mercy and bliss, and warns against disbelief, sin and their outcome. He leaves the heart neither in fear that cuts off hope nor in a false security that cancels responsibility. Balance does not mean diluting some of the truth to please the listener. It means hearing promise and warning as they came, and knowing the way to salvation rather than only picturing punishment.

Allah then explains that establishing the proof did not begin with this community alone: “There has never been any community except that a warner came to it[11].” Sending warners has been His way among nations, although we have not received all their names and detailed accounts. Allah did not leave creation without purpose and then surprise them with a reckoning for matters never explained.

This strengthens the Messenger ﷺ when his people reject him. He belongs to a succession of messengers, facing what they faced and fulfilling what they fulfilled. It strengthens his followers too: a limited response must not excuse abandoning warning, nor should the multitude of opponents make them forget the glad tidings Allah sent as mercy for those seeking guidance.

Verse 25

And if they deny you - then already have those before them denied. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs and written ordinances and with the enlightening Scripture.

Quran translation: Noor International Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

If every rejection arose from insufficient evidence, it would end as soon as clear proofs arrived. Yet the Quran speaks of nations whose messengers brought clear evidence and books, and who still rejected them. It therefore tells the Prophet ﷺ: “And if they deny you - then already have those before them denied.” It then lists what those messengers brought: “with clear proofs and written ordinances and with the enlightening Scripture.” Clear proofs disclose the truth; scriptures are written books carrying what was revealed to them; and an illuminating book sheds light on the guidance they need. Multiple means of explanation do not remove a person’s ability to reject what has become clear. In the Arabic, repeating the preposition bi- [with] before these descriptions draws attention to the strength and variety of the proof established against them. Their rejection should not be attributed to a deficiency in the message itself.

A person seeking reform therefore needs to understand what they are addressing. Someone may have a genuine doubt needing an informed answer. Another may present a question that conceals fear for an interest, pride, or unaddressed pain, and need both explanation and help removing that obstacle. Like someone repeating a treatment without diagnosing its target, one may multiply proofs without coming closer to the cause of rejection. Giving a tenth answer will not help someone who is not asking to learn. Equally, it is wrong to accuse a sincere questioner of obstinacy instead of answering them.

The Quran explains that some who demand a sign would not believe even if it came [6:109–110]. A change in the heart’s stance does not follow mechanically from multiplying miracles. The illuminating Book exposes these motives as well as presenting evidence. It teaches us neither to reduce a person to a theoretical question nor to dismiss their need for proof. The Messenger conveys what he was sent with; each heart still has its own stance towards the light brought to it.


Verse 26

Then I seized those who disbelieved. How severe was My response!

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

The proof came, then rejection, then seizure: “Then I seized those who disbelieved. How severe was My response!” This order reminds us that punishment did not come without explanation, and its delay did not reflect inability to inflict it. A believer may long for oppression to end because of intense suffering, but Allah directs matters beyond their grasp. He opens the door to repentance, distinguishes the truthful from the false, brings believers from the descendants of those who resist, and allows acts of patient and truthful servitude to emerge during trials. The servant need not see all these purposes as events unfold.

Khabbab’s complaint to the Prophet ﷺ shows how heavy this waiting can be. Amid persecution, the Companions asked him for help and supplication. He reminded them of earlier people’s steadfastness and gave glad tidings that the matter would reach completion, then said: “but you (people) are hasty” [al-Bukhari, 3612]. This did not belittle their pain. It strengthened them against treating the long road as a disproof of the promise.

The phrase “My response” refers to more than an objection that leaves reality unchanged. It is Allah’s condemnation expressed through punishment, until once-powerful nations became a lesson for those after them. Looking at the remains of those who rejected the truth is therefore not merely admiring their surviving stonework. When the Prophet ﷺ passed al-Hijr, he forbade entering the dwellings of those punished except with reflection, humility and weeping, lest the visitor suffer what they suffered [al-Bukhari, 433; Muslim, 2980]. The passage concerns the dwellings of a people whose punishment revelation reports; it does not issue one verdict on every ancient site. Looking should prompt the questions: What did their strength achieve when Allah’s command came? Do I merely know the outcome, or examine the path that could lead to something like it?


Verse 27

Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, then We bring forth thereby fruits of various colors? And in the mountains there are various tracts of white and red, of various colors, and raven black;

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

The Quran brings the heart back from the traces of punishment to the visible signs of creation: “Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, then We bring forth thereby fruits of various colors?” Water that looks the same to us is the means through which Allah produces fruits differing in colour, taste, scent and benefit. His power is not compelled to produce one form from one origin. The mountains provide another example: visible streaks in the rock, white and red in varying shades, and others intensely black. Soft fruit and solid mountains alike show the action of a powerful Creator who brings forth variety without being defeated by differences of form. The Quran reinforces this: “They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We cause some of them to excel others in taste.” [13:4]. It also counts differences in languages and colours among Allah’s signs [30:22].

Reflection goes beyond admiring the view to recognising the power that brought it forth. If the same water falls and fruits differ, do not be surprised that the same revelation reaches different hearts and their responses vary. One person listens and examines themselves, another merely admires the phrasing, and a third persists in turning away.

This both consoles and warns the bearer of the message. They cannot make people identical copies, but must carry to them the water that gives life, and themselves look at the signs with a desire to be changed by them.


Verse 28

just as humans, animals and livestock are of various colors. Only the people of knowledge among His slaves fear Allah. Indeed, Allah is All-Mighty, All-Forgiving.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

The scene of variety extends to people, animals and livestock: “just as humans, animals and livestock are of various colors.” The differences seen in fruit and mountains also appear in living beings. The Quran then identifies the fruit reflection should produce: “Only the people of knowledge among His slaves fear Allah.” Praiseworthy knowledge is not a large store of memorised names or skill in debate. It is knowledge of Allah that brings reverence and submission. In the verse’s wording, the people of knowledge are those who fear, and Allah is the One they fear. They better recognise His greatness and perceive what His signs signify. Ibn Abbas is reported to have described them as knowing that Allah has power over all things. Reflection on creation’s variety thus meets revelation, which teaches us about its Creator.

One way of connecting the Arabic wording completes the description of colours at the expression corresponding to “just as”, then begins a fresh statement about fear of Allah. Another interpretation relates the comparison to differences among the servants too, as the preceding things differ. In either case, the educational lesson is that differences in people’s forms are not the basis of merit. What matters is what knowledge brings forth within them.

The verse should not be confined to an academic title. Someone studying the details of a cell or the intricacies of the human body may grow in recognition of creation’s precision, if they read it in the light of guidance. Someone memorising matters of religious law must still ask: What effect have these had on my humility and uprightness? Surah al-ʿAlaq begins with “Read in the name of your Lord” [96:1] and ends with “prostrate and draw near [to Allah].” [96:19]. Between them it warns about “when he deems himself to be self-sufficient.” [96:7]. Knowledge may lead to prostration, or be received with arrogance that creates an illusion of self-sufficiency.

The same verse may reach two hearts and have different effects. Allah says of a revealed surah: “As for those who believe, it does increase them in faith and they rejoice.” In contrast: “But as for those in whose hearts is sickness, it only increases them in wickedness[91] to their wickedness” [9:124–125].

When a Prophetic report concerns something outside their experience, people differ in how they approach its authenticity and meaning. Consider the report that speaks of disease and cure in connection with a fly, or the report about washing a vessel after a dog has licked it [al-Bukhari, 3320; Muslim, 279]. One person seeks to establish the report’s authenticity and understand it, while another treats its strangeness to them as a reason to reject it before investigating.

The earlier comparison of rain and land helps explain the difference: the goodness of the water does not change, but the land’s readiness to receive it varies. The next verse then mentions recitation, prayer and spending, showing a practical path to fruitful knowledge. The heart receives, the body acts uprightly and the hand gives.

The ending “Indeed, Allah is All-Mighty, All-Forgiving.” joins awe of the All-Mighty, who cannot be overcome, with hope in the All-Forgiving, who forgives shortcomings. Knowledge should become neither hardness without mercy nor mercy turned into a sense of safety that extinguishes fear.

Verse 29

Those who recite the Book of Allah, establish prayer, and spend out of what We have provided for them, secretly and openly, hoping for a deal that will suffer no loss,

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

After knowledge that bears the fruit of fear, the verse presents a practical picture of its people: they recite Allah’s Book, establish prayer and spend from His provision. Knowledge does not stop at admiring a meaning; it reorders a person’s relationship with their Lord and what they possess. The Arabic verb translated “recite” evokes renewed recitation, followed by the wording “establish prayer, and spend”, describing actions through which their response has been realised. The different Arabic verb forms do not make prayer or spending activities that come to an end. They draw attention to a continuing source that nourishes obedience. In Arabic, the word for recitation is also connected with following, as in: “and by the moon as it follows it[1],” [91:2]. A believer reads the verse, then follows its guidance in heart and action, rather than merely passing the words over their tongue.

The need for this provision does not end after a first repentance or the beginning of religious commitment. The path to Allah is surrounded by hardships that test sincerity. The Prophet ﷺ said: “The Paradise is surrounded by hardships and the Hell-Fire is surrounded by temptations” [Muslim, 2822]. Losing contact with revelation can be pictured through someone on a dark, thorny road, carrying a lamp and provisions. If these are lost halfway, the thorns do not disappear: the traveller loses what made them visible and gave strength to overcome them. Likewise, a person may outwardly keep working for the religion yet exhaust their heart by leaving what renews its hope. Pain alone does not distinguish the path of truth from falsehood. Allah says: “If you are suffering harm, they too are suffering harm just like you; but you hope from Allah what they do not hope.” [4:104]. The hope the Quran revives gives hardship its meaning.

Surah al-Nur follows the comparison of light with houses where Allah’s name is remembered, and men whom trade does not distract from remembrance, prayer and zakah [24:35–37]. Light bears fruit in worship and giving, as it does here. Readers should examine their recitation’s effect: Does it help them establish prayer and free their hands from stinginess? “out of what We have provided for them” recalls a gift already received, so spending it is no favour to their Lord. “secretly and openly” keeps obedience from becoming merely a display for others. They give where giving benefits, seeking Allah’s Face in both situations.

Then comes the glad tiding: “hoping for a deal that will suffer no loss,” [35:29]. This trade does not lose its value through a stagnant market or the trader’s death. An account describes the Quran meeting its companion on the Day of Resurrection. The Quran tells its companion that every trader stands behind their trade, while it, the Quran, stands that Day behind every trade [Abu Ubayd, Fada’il al-Quran]. On the day of need, the person sees the effect of the companionship that kept them awake at night and directed their actions.

One fruit in this life is renewed faith rather than meanings growing worn in the heart. The report says: “Verily, faith wears out within one of you as a shirt becomes worn out; so, ask Allah to renew faith in your hearts.” [al-Hakim, al-Mustadrak, 5]. Repeating a surah is not returning without benefit. A reader may understand something previously unnoticed, or encounter a situation that brings home the need for what they had read without feeling its relevance. The reward of recitation is renewed too.

This explains why even many beneficial books should not replace a person’s connection with the Quran. A useful book helps them understand it, but cannot be recited in prayer in its place or take revelation’s role. A believer protects time to recite, listen and reflect, because sound work needs a heart that has not become cut off from its source of guidance. This does not diminish the importance of the rest of their work.

Verse 30

so that He will reward them in full and give them more out of His bounty[14]. He is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

They hope for trade that suffers no loss because they deal with a Lord whose generosity exceeds their calculations: “so that He will reward them in full and give them more out of His bounty[14].” Reward in full means nothing is taken away from the recompense for the deed. The increase is a gift beyond what its recipient’s hope had encompassed. This is not bargaining between equal parties, and the servant should not regard their deed as imposing on their Lord an amount they independently deserve.

The two names at the verse’s end explain this hope: “He is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative.” He forgives the shortcomings mingled with deeds, and appreciates a little by multiplying its reward. The linguistic sense of appreciation can be brought closer through fertile ground that receives a little seed and water yet produces much good, or an animal that benefits its owner beyond what its feed might lead them to expect. To Allah belongs the highest comparison. These examples illustrate abundant giving, without likening His attributes to His creation.

The Prophet ﷺ explained that Allah accepts charity from good earnings and nurtures it for its giver until it becomes like a mountain [al-Bukhari, 1410; Muslim, 1014]. What left the hand small may become great in the scales. A believer should not belittle a sincere deed or measure its reward by its size in their own eyes.

A striking picture of increase comes in the report of Moses, peace be upon him, asking about the lowest-ranking inhabitant of Paradise. That person is asked whether they would be pleased to have the equivalent of a worldly king’s dominion. They agree, then receive as much again and again until declaring themselves pleased with their Lord. They are then told: “It is for you and, ten times like it, and for you is what your self desires and your eye enjoys.” [Muslim, 189]. Their hopes were reached, then bounty came beyond them. What, then, of what Allah has prepared for those of higher rank? The reward of ten good deeds for a letter of the Quran illustrates the same meaning [al-Tirmidhi, 2910]. The servant recites for a limited time, while Allah multiplies their reward beyond what human dealings can offer.

The scene of the righteous in Surah al-Insan also explains bounty. They fed the needy, an orphan and a captive, saying: “We feed you only for the sake of Allah; we seek from you neither reward nor gratitude.” [76:9]. The Quran then describes vessels being passed around them, drinks served to them and young attendants serving them. The honour rises to: “and their Lord will give them a pure drink.” [76:21]. They gave food seeking His Face, and He honoured them beyond the measure of what they offered. This is not a numerical calculation by which we assign a particular pleasure in Paradise to each deed. It teaches us about a Lord who pays in full and gives more, joining forgiveness with appreciation for those who turn to Him. Knowing this makes hope for acceptance and bounty greater than self-admiration, and helps a person give without waiting for people’s thanks.

Verse 31

The Book that We have revealed to you [O Prophet] is the truth, confirming that which came before it. Indeed, Allah is All-Aware and All-Seeing of His slaves.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, verses 19–32.

After describing recitation of the Book, its fruit and reward, the verse affirms what they recite: “The Book that We have revealed to you [O Prophet] is the truth” [35:31]. It is not temporary guidance that changes with tastes, or a human opinion placed on a level with every conception of Allah and our destination. It is revelation from the One who knows His servants, and they turn to it when they disagree. They study worldly matters and benefit from human knowledge, yet it is to revelation that they turn to know their Lord and the purpose for which He created them. In these matters they do not give human knowledge the same authority as revelation. Describing it as “confirming that which came before it” connects it with the origin of earlier messages. Allah, who revealed it, also sent the messengers before; the foundation of their call to worship Him is one, even though distortion occurred among some who carried the scriptures.

The verse continues: “Indeed, Allah is All-Aware and All-Seeing of His slaves.” He knows their inner states and sees their deeds, so His choice of who bears the message comes from knowledge: “Allah knows best where to place His message.” [6:124].

This prepares for what follows. Just as He chose the Messenger ﷺ to receive and convey the Book, He chose servants to inherit it and carry its trust. The truth of the source meets the wisdom of the choice, revealing our responsibility. What we hold is not material to reshape according to our desires. It is guidance to which we learn to respond, and a bounty to preserve and convey.


Verse 32

Then We made to inherit the Book those whom We have chosen from among Our slaves[15]. But among them are some who wrong themselves, some follow a middle course, and some are foremost in good deeds with Allah’s permission. That is the great bounty.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; third session, continued at the beginning of the fourth session.

The language of inheritance tells this community what has reached it: “Then We made to inherit the Book those whom We have chosen from among Our slaves[15].” An heir finds a treasure they did not create. Possessing it alone does not mean they have used it well: they may preserve it and develop its benefits, or neglect it and deprive themselves of its good. The Quran is both a gift of being chosen and a responsibility, not merely a name to take pride in while neglecting its recitation and practice. The Quran warns of people who inherited the Scripture yet preferred immediate worldly gain [7:169]. A new heir should not feel safe from similar failings. The Prophet ﷺ said: “By this Book, Allah would exalt some peoples and degrade others” [Muslim, 817]. Elevation lies in responding to the Book, rather than merely having it among us.

The verse then details the conditions of those chosen: “But among them are some who wrong themselves, some follow a middle course, and some are foremost in good deeds with Allah’s permission.” On the well-known interpretation that all three groups belong to this believing community, the one who wrongs themselves neglects a duty or commits something forbidden. The moderate person fulfils obligations and avoids prohibitions, without being foremost in voluntary deeds. The foremost hastens to obligatory and recommended acts and guards against what takes them away from complete obedience. Commentators gave various examples because religion has many doors: some considered prayer, others spending or a person’s relationship with the Book. The groups should not be confined to one example. One heir might neglect the Quran, wronging themselves by losing its good; another fulfils what is required; a third makes it the guide of their life, following its guidance and doing much of the good it shows.

Another interpretation understands the wrongdoer as a disbeliever or hypocrite, and makes the coming promise apply to the moderate and foremost. This differs from the first interpretation. Their conclusions should not be mixed, nor should “wrongdoer” always be assigned one meaning before considering the interpretation.

On the well-known interpretation, the verse remains a door of hope for a believer who falls short, rather than permission to be content with shortcomings. It is a door of humility for the foremost, rather than a certificate they issue to themselves.

Being foremost is qualified by “with Allah’s permission”. They got ahead only through their Lord’s enabling and help; left to themselves, they could not independently achieve good. Frequent remembrance helps sustain this aid. When a man complained that Islam’s teachings had become too many for him and asked for something to hold to, the Prophet ﷺ replied: “Keep your tongue moist with the remembrance of Allah.” [al-Tirmidhi, 3375]. This was not so he could leave obligations, but so his heart would remain connected to the One who helps him fulfil them.

The verse closes: “That is the great bounty.” Some understood it as referring to inheriting the Book, others to being foremost, and others to the Paradise that follows. These meanings meet in one reminder: the beginning of guidance is bounty, being enabled to act is bounty, and reaching the reward is bounty. The foremost should not become conceited, nor should those who fall short abandon hope. Both should recognise the value of what Allah has given them to inherit and work to preserve it.

Verse 33

They will enter Gardens of Eternity; they will be adorned therein with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their garments will be of silk.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

After the groups inheriting the Book comes the promise: “They will enter Gardens of Eternity;” [35:33]. On the interpretation that all three groups are believers, their final destination is Paradise, although their ranks and what they experience before entering differ. On the interpretation that excludes the wrongdoer from faith, the promise belongs to those who remain among the obedient. Entering does not make all deeds equal or declare sins free of reckoning. It explains Allah’s generosity while preserving the differences in His servants’ degrees.

The Arabic word ʿAdn [Eternity] conveys residence and stability. Its promise has a special place in the heart of someone who has left home or family seeking their Lord’s pleasure. Umm Salama’s account of migration brings this cost closer. When her husband Abu Salama wanted to leave for Madinah, her family prevented her from travelling with him. His family then took her son, separating husband, wife and child. She continued weeping until, after a time, she was permitted to join her husband and they were reunited [Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah].

Migration was not an easy word, but a test involving the ties dearest to a person. Those who made such sacrifices recognise in a promise of residence unthreatened by expulsion a meaning beyond the bare word. A report says the poor emigrants will precede the rich into Paradise by forty years [Muslim, 2979]. Someone who left wealth for Allah has not lost merely because they departed with few possessions.

The verse then describes what they receive: “they will be adorned therein with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their garments will be of silk.” They are shown adornment that will not vanish, after learning not to put passing adornment before truth. This does not forbid every good thing in this life. It means that obtaining such things must not become a goal that drives Allah’s rights and people’s rights from the heart.

This education appears in the commands given to the Prophet ﷺ: “Read”, “Stand up in prayer at night”, and “arise and warn” [96:1; 73:2; 74:2]. Knowledge comes from revelation, worship nourishes the heart, and conveying the message requires effort. The work does not rest on constant inactivity or preoccupation with luxury, even though the worker needs rest and the means of living.

The distinction between loving money for its adornment and wanting the good it enables appears when some Companions said: “the rich have taken away (all the) reward.” [Muslim, 1006]. Their sorrow was not that they lacked the same finery as those with wealth, but that the rich could give charity while they could not. They competed in the obedience money could support, rather than boasting of possessing it. The Quran returns our gaze to this measure: “Do not turn your eyes away from them, desiring the adornments of the life of this world.” [18:28]. From here, the heart understands the account of gold, pearls and silk. Giving precedence to Allah’s pleasure does not end in permanent deprivation, but in lasting honour from a Lord who has not allowed His servants’ sacrifices for Him to go to waste.


Verse 34

And they will say, “All praise be to Allah Who has taken away from us all sorrow. Indeed, our Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative,

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

When they enter Paradise, the Quran does not first describe their amazement at gold and silk. It describes their praise for the removal of what weighed upon their hearts: “All praise be to Allah Who has taken away from us all sorrow.” The Arabic expression “from us” brings close the image of a burden lifted from its bearer. Fear of the Fire has ended, the pains of the journey have ceased, and everything that troubles life has gone. Sorrow has been explained as fear of the final outcome, or the believer’s toil and hardship, or all the sorrows of this world. This good news should not be confined to one cause. In this life, the obedient combined earnest effort with fear: the Quran describes them spending the night prostrating and standing before their Lord, then saying, ‘Our Lord, turn the punishment of Hell away from us, for its punishment is unrelenting[38].’ [25:65]. Their night prayers did not make them feel safe from reckoning. When they saw their deliverance, they understood the greatness of the mercy.

What occupies people differs according to what they hold important. A person may exhaust their heart over a dispute about possessions, a fault in a car, or something they failed to obtain, until no room remains for concern about their religion or the good they could offer. This does not ask us to deny natural grief concerning ourselves or our families. Revelation puts our concerns in their proper order, so that immediate matters do not become the whole of life.

Night prayer with the Quran helped the believer do this: hearing what their Lord tells them about Himself and the Hereafter, and drawing from the verses renewed resolve after a day weighed down by events. Allah says: “Indeed, worship at night is more effective[3] and more conducive to proper recitation[4].” [73:6]. The heart is restored through more than information alone. It needs a renewed connection in which it lives with the meanings of revelation.

The believer asks Allah for help with difficulty through the supplication: “O Allah, there is no ease except in that which You have made easy, and You make the difficulty, if You will, easy.” [Ibn Hibban, 974]. The Arabic word ḥazn here means rugged ground, and is then used for a difficult matter. Its pronunciation should be distinguished from ḥazan [sorrow] in the verse, although both call for the servant to seek their Lord’s help in making things easy.

Sorrow disappears completely in Paradise. We should not imagine its bliss governed by everything that saddens us now, such as fear of separation or the expectation that a blessing will end.

Allah tells us that believers and their offspring who follow them in faith will be brought together [52:21]. His Prophet ﷺ also related what will happen to Ibrahim and his father on the Day of Resurrection: kinship does not make disbelief a route into Paradise. When Ibrahim asks concerning his father, he is told that Paradise is forbidden to disbelievers; his father’s form is then changed into that of a dhīkh, a male hyena [al-Bukhari, 3350]. Final destinies follow what Allah has explained, rather than people’s wishes. The people of Paradise will not remain captives of sorrow that contradicts His promise. We believe what He has told us. We do not add details about how it happens beyond what He has described.

One of the strongest reassurances that the effects of misery will pass is the report about the person of Paradise who had suffered most in this world. He is dipped once into Paradise, then asked about what he experienced. He swears to his Lord that he has not, saying: “never did I face any hardship or experience any distress” [Muslim, 2807]. Allah is not unaware of worldly hardships. Rather, bliss has overwhelmed them until they no longer remain in the person’s awareness as they once did.

Their praise is therefore followed by: “Indeed, our Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative”. They have seen shortcomings forgiven, goodness multiplied, and bliss that their short lifetimes could not match in an equal exchange. They recognise that their arrival comes from His mercy and bounty.

Verse 35

He Who has caused us to settle – out of His grace – in the everlasting home, wherein no weariness or fatigue will touch us.”

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

The people of Paradise describe the blessing of stability: “He Who has caused us to settle – out of His grace – in the everlasting home”. In this world they travelled, gave of themselves and left familiar things when truth called them. Then they entered a home from which they fear neither departure nor the end of its blessings. Accounts of such effort strikingly begin with movement away from the place where a person feels at ease: “Just as your Lord brought you [O Prophet] out of your home for a just cause[2]” in the setting of Badr [8:5], and “And [remember] when you left your home in the morning to position the believers for the battle [of ’Uhud]” in the setting of Uhud [3:121]. Regarding migration: “Whoever leaves his home emigrating for the cause of Allah and His Messenger, then death overtakes him, his reward has already been secured with Allah.” [4:100]. Seeking Allah does not remain a wish inside the house. At times we must get up and do what duty requires. A settled family life and rest are no cause for blame in themselves, but we must not let them keep us from the obedience and sacrifice that our duty calls for.

Then comes complete rest: “wherein no weariness or fatigue will touch us.” The Arabic terms naṣab [weariness] and lughūb [fatigue] describe weariness and exhaustion. Several explanations of their difference complement one another in denying hardship and fatigue; each word need not be confined to one type. The expression is beautiful in denying even their touch. This is no brief rest before pain returns, nor rest caused by inability to move. It is bliss in which they dwell, move and enjoy themselves without the fatigue familiar to people in this world.

Someone whose illness has lasted long may feel a promise of health in a way that a healthy listener does not. Likewise, someone who has tasted the difficulty of obedience and leaving familiar things recognises the meaning of a home without weariness. When reading, “So whenever you are free[5], strive in worship” [94:7], they understand life as a journey with an end through Allah’s mercy, rather than an endless succession of effort without purpose. They therefore do not waste health and free time. The Prophet ﷺ said: “There are two blessings that many people are deceived into losing: health and free time” [al-Bukhari, 6412]. They use their health and time for good without neglecting the rights of their body or family, knowing that the opportunity will not last.

Yet the people of Paradise do not say they entered by an independent power or a price that placed their Lord under obligation. They say: “out of His grace”. Deeds are the path they were commanded to follow; being enabled to perform them, their acceptance, and then abiding in bliss are Allah’s bounty. Whoever knows this strives without claiming to have done Allah a favour, and hopes without settling into idle reliance.

8:5 — [2] It is referring to the Battle of Badr.

94:7 — [5] Of your duties.


Verse 36

As for those who disbelieve, there will be the Fire of Hell; neither will they be sentenced to death, so that they may die, nor will its punishment be lightened for them. This is how We recompense every disbeliever.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

The complete rest described by the people of Paradise is contrasted with a state in which neither death nor a lightening of punishment brings rest: “As for those who disbelieve, there will be the Fire of Hell; neither will they be sentenced to death, so that they may die, nor will its punishment be lightened for them.” In this world, someone in pain may expect an episode to pass, hope to lose sensation, or wish for a life they can no longer bear to end. Here, those hoped-for ways of escape are closed.

Allah says elsewhere: “and death will come to him from every direction, yet he will not die.” [14:17]. He also relates their cry: ‘They will cry out, “O Mālik[28], let your Lord put an end to us!” He will say, “You are here to stay.”’ [43:77]. For someone who died in disbelief, the Fire is no slight punishment followed by escape. It is remaining in the torment against which they are warned before it arrives.

The ending connects with Allah’s names and attributes repeated earlier. There, the Lord is “All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative” [35:34], forgiving the people of faith and multiplying their good. Here: “This is how We recompense every disbeliever.” This concerns the person who persisted in denial, rejecting blessings and signs until their opportunity ended. The reader should not confuse this warning with the case of someone who sinned and then repented, or make the breadth of mercy cancel the consequence Allah has described. The mercy in His warning now is that the door has not yet closed for the listener. They hear the description of a destination they do not want to reach while they can still turn back.

43:77 — [28] The chief keeper of Hell.


Verse 37

They will be screaming therein, “Our Lord, take us out; we will do righteous deeds, unlike what we used to do.” [They will be told], “Did We not give you long enough lives to take heed for anyone who wished to take heed? And the warner did come to you. So taste! The wrongdoers will have no helper.”

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

The scene does more than tell us that the people of the Fire are being punished. It lets us hear their appeal: “They will be screaming therein”. This is no quiet call or passing objection, but an intense cry from a heart that knows the enormity of its loss. A sufferer’s pain grows when they call and find no one to relieve them. What, then, if the answer reminds them of the opportunity they neglected? Imagine a sick person crying for help without a response, with reproach for what they did to themselves added to their pain. This example only brings the effect of rebuke closer; it does not equate worldly pain with punishment in the Hereafter.

Believers persecuted for their religion heard in the Quran that injustice was not the end of the story: “On that Day you will see the wicked bound together in chains[39]”; “their garments will be of pitch and their faces covered with Fire.” [14:49–50]. Those who mocked them would meet the consequence of their actions [83:34–36]. Knowing Allah’s justice made them steadfast. Their worldly pain was real, but the reckoning did not end in the hands of their persecutors.

Among the reports showing the greatness of the Fire’s punishment is the Prophet’s ﷺ statement: “The molar tooth of an unbeliever or the canine teeth of an unbeliever will be like Uhud” [Muslim, 2851]. The conditions of that Day are not measured by the bodily dimensions familiar here. The Quran also reveals their continuing experience of punishment: “Whenever their skins are burnt through, We will replace them with fresh skins, so that they may taste the punishment.” [4:56]. Burning the skin will not bring the pain to an end.

Their request then becomes clear: “Our Lord, take us out; we will do righteous deeds, unlike what we used to do.” They had been active, but now know that much activity does not make deeds righteous, and people’s applause does not alter Allah’s judgment of them. The report about names says: “the truest are Harith and Hammam” [Abu Dawud, 4950]. A person is moved by an intention and carries out work. Descriptions of acting and intending may fit them even when some other name of praise does not. The outcome is clearer still in: “All men go out early in the morning and sell themselves, thereby setting themselves free or destroying themselves” [Muslim, 223]. The question is not whether a person spent their life, but on what.

The answer to their request to return is: “Did We not give you long enough lives to take heed for anyone who wished to take heed? And the warner did come to you.” They had both time to take heed and the explanation that warned them, like someone given the time and teaching needed for an examination who neglected it until it ended. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Allah will not accept the excuse of any person whose instant of death is delayed till he is sixty years of age” [al-Bukhari, 6419]. A longer reprieve increases responsibility. It does not permit anyone below sixty to remain heedless.

Someone may think one shock will change them forever, while finding in themselves that fear weakens unless their resolve changes. A reckless driver has an accident, drives cautiously on returning, then gradually forgets and becomes reckless again. This example shows that momentary pain alone does not establish lasting repentance. As for what the people of the Fire would actually do if returned, the All-Knowing has told us: “And even if they were sent back, they would surely return to what they were forbidden” [6:28]. His knowledge cannot be opposed by a wish imagined from outside the scene.

Finally: “So taste! The wrongdoers will have no helper.” Excuses have fallen away, and none of the means on which they relied can prevent recompense. It is Allah’s mercy that the reader hears this answer before becoming one of those asking for return. Taking heed and acting remain possible now.

Verse 38

Allah is the Knower of the unseen in the heavens and earth. He is All-Knowing of what is in the hearts.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

After the verses relate the people of the Fire asking to return to this world, an answer comes to a question that might occur to the reader: perhaps they would be truthful this time if sent back? “Allah is the Knower of the unseen in the heavens and earth.” He knows what is hidden from us about their condition. The difference between regret that changes a person and a promise made under pressure of pain, then forgotten when it passes, is not hidden from Him. Elsewhere He has told us: “And even if they were sent back, they would surely return to what they were forbidden, for they are indeed liars.” [6:28].

Then He singles out the inner self: “He is All-Knowing of what is in the hearts.” He does not need to hear an excuse to know its truth, and no intention concealed behind fine words escapes Him. This knowledge is not confined to the scene of punishment. It belongs to the meanings the surah has built from its beginning: His creation rests on knowledge, His governance rests on knowledge, and His choice of those who carry revelation rests on knowledge. His awareness of His servants has already been mentioned in connection with sending down and inheriting the Book. Next comes a reminder that He made people successors on earth.

Someone who knows that their Lord sees into their heart recognises the limits of what they themselves can know about the hearts of others. They do not judge another person’s intentions on mere suspicion. Knowing that Allah sees them as they are, they work to be sincere inwardly and look beyond the impression they make on others.


Verse 39

It is He Who has made you successors on earth. Whoever disbelieves will bear the burden of his disbelief. Their disbelief only increases the disbelievers in contempt before their Lord, and their disbelief only increases the disbelievers in loss.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

“It is He Who has made you successors on earth.” You live on land where others lived, benefit from what they left behind, and will be succeeded by others in turn. A person should not see the traces of those who have departed and then act as though continued life were guaranteed to them alone. This succession also conveys a trust: the ability and lifetime given to a person are bound to their responsibility before Allah. Humanity has borne the trust of moral obligation of which Allah says: “But man assumed it” [33:72]. Yet Allah has no need of His servants’ response: “Whoever disbelieves will bear the burden of his disbelief.” The harm of rejection returns to its doer and diminishes nothing of their Lord’s dominion.

The verse again warns against being deceived by outward success: “Their disbelief only increases the disbelievers in contempt before their Lord”. The Arabic term maqt [contempt] means intense abhorrence. Opposing truth does not become a virtue because a person grows richer or more famous. Qarun’s story shows how standards become confused. He appeared before his people in his finery, and those who wanted this worldly life said: “Oh, would that we had the like of what Korah has been given! He is indeed a man of great fortune.” [28:79]. His splendour obscured what they knew of his oppression, until the earth swallowing him exposed wealth’s inability to save him. Those who had wished for his position the previous day then changed their words [28:81–82]. The verse does not deny that an unbeliever may gain a worldly benefit. It denies that the benefit proves Allah’s approval of their disbelief.

This becomes clearer in the address to the people of the Fire: “Allah’s abhorrence of you when you were called to believe but you disbelieved was greater than your abhorrence of yourselves [Today].” [40:10]. There, a person hates the choice that ruined them, having once viewed it as a path to profit. That is why the verse ends with “loss”: however much the hand gathers, it cannot make up for loss in one’s relationship with Allah and final destiny on returning to Him.


Verse 40

Say, “Tell me about your associate-gods whom you supplicate besides Allah. Show me what they created on earth, or do they have a share in the heavens? Or have We given them a Book so they have clear proof? No, the wrongdoers promise one another nothing but delusion[16].”

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, verses 33–41.

The surah returns from scenes of recompense to ask the polytheists what their choice rests on: “Show me what they created on earth, or do they have a share in the heavens?” What do these beings possess that warrants directing worship to them? Have they created anything or shared in governing the heavens? If they have no creation, has permission to worship them come from Allah: “Or have We given them a Book so they have clear proof?” These two questions bring the claim back to its evidence, rather than the number of people accustomed to it. Creation and command belong to Allah: “Behold, His is the creation and the command.” [7:54].

A person may make a statue, then attribute to it powers it does not possess. Al-Samiri brought out for Musa’s people a calf with a body that made a lowing sound. The Quran confronted them with its inability: “Do they not see that it does not respond a word to them, nor does it have the power to harm or benefit them?” [20:89]. An arresting sound or appearance does not make a manufactured object a creator.

Without evidence, what remains is the verse’s ending: “No, the wrongdoers promise one another nothing but delusion[16].” People pass promises on, and those who come later feel reassured by earlier people’s words without asking what establishes them. Picture a skilled engineer asked about food placed before a statue that cannot eat it. He offers no answer about the statue’s entitlement to worship, but refers to customs he grew up with. Skill in an occupation is not enough by itself to prompt examination of inherited belief. A person may investigate the cause of a tiny fault carefully while never pausing over the basis of their worship.

The Quran warns of the danger of words inherited without knowledge in attributing a child to Allah: “They have no knowledge of it, nor did their forefathers[4]. What a monstrous word that comes out of their mouths! They say nothing but lies.” [18:5]. A report describes the virtue of memorising the first ten verses of Surah al-Kahf: they protect the one who memorises them from the Dajjal [Muslim, 809]. One of their meanings that should accompany memorisation is that the heart should not submit to an impressive claim without evidence. Just as Fatir began with Allah’s true promise and a warning against delusion, it returns here to expose promises supported by neither creation nor revelation.

Verse 41

Allah keeps the heavens and earth from vanishing. If they were to vanish, there is none to sustain them other than Him. He is Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fourth session, continued at the opening of the fifth.

After denying any share in creation to the supposed partners, the verse explains that creation itself cannot do without Allah for even a moment after coming into existence: “Allah keeps the heavens and earth from vanishing.” The world was not created and then left to stand by itself. Its continuing existence and stability depend on its Creator’s governance. Vanishing has been explained as their passing away and ruin, or their departure from the order Allah established for them. In either explanation, a creature does not possess the Creator’s power: “If they were to vanish, there is none to sustain them other than Him.”

To bring the difference closer, electricity may fail in a neighbourhood, and experts gather to repair a transformer or wire. This is a problem whose causes and means of repair can be envisaged. Yet the person repairing it already works within a stable world, benefiting from ground that supports them and laws they did not create. If the order sustaining the heavens and earth disappeared, to whom could creatures turn to restore it? The question returns the heart to a need that no abundance of skills and tools can remedy. It distinguishes repairing something within the world from sustaining the whole world.

Alongside this account of power, the verse ends with two names that someone seeing only strength might overlook: “He is Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving.” He can seize people in punishment, yet gives those who disobey time and opens forgiveness to them if they return. A blessing’s continuance does not show an absence of power; it bears witness to His forbearance. In Surah al-Isra, the general glorification of creatures is followed by the same Arabic ending: “Indeed, He is Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving.” [17:44]. The universe glorifies Him, while a person may turn away, and their Lord does not hasten to punish. Should this forbearance not lead them to repent rather than grow more deluded?


Verse 42

They swore by Allah their most solemn oaths that if a warner came to them, they would surely be more guided than any other community. But when a warner came to them, it only added to their aversion –

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fifth session, verses 42–45.

Before the Messenger came, they made a promise confirmed with solemn oaths: if a warner came, they would be better guided than their predecessors. Yet when what they requested arrived, it only increased their aversion. This contrast exposes the difference between wishing for guidance and being ready to accept its demands. A person may want a Book to boast about before others, rather than one that corrects their beliefs and restrains what they love. The next verse therefore explains the aversion as arrogance. The hadith warns against seeking knowledge “to contend with the scholars, or to use it to argue with the fools, and to have people's faces turn towards him” [al-Tirmidhi, 2654; judged hasan by al-Albani]. Seeking may look good while its purpose corrupts it.

Others sought truth before the ideal circumstances desired by those who made excuses were complete. Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl rejected slaughter for anyone other than Allah. He told Quraysh that Allah created the sheep, sent down its water and grew its food from the earth, yet they slaughtered it in another name [al-Bukhari, 3826]. Salman, may Allah be pleased with him, set out seeking truth rather than merely waiting for it to reach him where he was. The account of the beginning of revelation establishes that the Prophet ﷺ worshipped in the cave of Hira before revelation came [al-Bukhari, 3]. These examples do not mean human reason makes the divine message unnecessary. They show the difference between a heart that seeks and prepares, and one that turns every obstacle into a reason to refuse.

A familiar example is someone postponing Quran study until a teacher will come to their home, their time is free of demands, and companions will always follow their progress. When some of this becomes possible, they find another excuse. They should begin with what is possible while seeking help. In the sacred hadith Allah says: “if he comes one span nearer to Me, I go one cubit nearer to him” [al-Bukhari, 7405; Muslim, 2675].

Readiness is likewise not measured by enthusiasm before a duty is imposed. The Quran tells of people who requested fighting, then a group became afraid when it was prescribed [4:77]. It reminds others of wishing for death before meeting it [3:143]. A person may wait for prosperity before giving, then become so occupied with preserving their expanded business and work that they neglect the giving they promised.

Abu Ayyub al-Ansari’s account strikingly illustrates this trial. When Allah strengthened Islam and its supporters increased, some Ansar considered staying with their property to repair what had been neglected. He explained that “and do not throw yourselves into destruction[95]” [2:195] was revealed in this setting: turning to their wealth and abandoning their duty to support the religion [al-Tirmidhi, 2972; Abu Dawud, 2512]. The point is not to condemn maintaining a livelihood, but to prevent prosperity becoming a reason to forget duty. The measure is therefore not what I promise to do when circumstances improve. It is how I receive Allah’s command when it reaches me and conflicts with my comfort or pride.

Verse 43

due to their arrogance in the land and plotting evil, but evil plotting only backfires on its own people. Are they awaiting anything other than the fate of the earlier peoples? You will find no change in Allah’s way[17], and you will find no alteration in Allah’s way[18].

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fifth session, verses 42–45.

The verse names what lay behind their aversion: “due to their arrogance in the land and plotting evil”. The obstacle was not a lack of oaths or weak expressions of wanting guidance. It was a self unwilling to submit, and interests threatened by following revelation. This includes objecting that the Quran came to someone other than their chosen man of standing: “Why was this Qur’an not sent down to a great man from one of the two towns[12]?” [43:31], or asking the Messenger to change it [10:15]. When truth did not fit their wishes, rejection became organised obstruction. Such obstruction may wear the appearance of protecting religion, as Pharaoh said while resisting Musa: “For I fear that he may change your religion” [40:26]. A fine slogan is not enough; we must consider what its bearer actually protects. Waraqa ibn Nawfal therefore did not find hostility to the Prophet ﷺ surprising. He said: “Anyone (man) who came with something similar to what you have brought was treated with hostility” [al-Bukhari, 3]. The account of Heraclius also shows hesitation when accepting the message met his people’s attachment to their existing beliefs [al-Bukhari, 7]. Knowledge alone is not enough when the will refuses its demands.

Allah then steadies the bearer of truth: “but evil plotting only backfires on its own people.” The Arabic verb yaḥīq [backfires] conveys the sense of descending upon and surrounding. Plotting does not let its doer escape their Lord’s reckoning. Earlier, the surah forbade the Messenger from destroying himself with grief over those who turned away. Here it addresses fear of their schemes: their guidance is not in your hands, and their plotting is not beyond Allah’s dominion. This promise does not mean every plot reverses immediately before our eyes, or every believer sees their opponents’ end within their own lifetime. The account of Allah’s ways corrects our understanding of the outcome; it does not set a date of our choosing.

Examples of the difference between absolute power and familiar events include Allah holding back the sun for a prophet, as explained at verse 13, and clearing Musa of accusations made by some Israelites. Musa put his clothes on a stone while bathing alone. The stone moved away with them, and he followed until his accusers saw that he was free of the defect they alleged [al-Bukhari, 3404; Muslim, 339]. The Creator of causes can bring about something beyond the usual course and is not unable to fulfil His promise.

Yet the people of truth may experience visible pain. At Uhud, Abu Sufyan’s call rose: “O Hubal, be high!” The Prophet ﷺ told his Companions to answer: “Allah is Higher and more Sublime.” Abu Sufyan then said: “We have the (idol) Al `Uzza, and you have no `Uzza.” The reply was: “Allah is our Helper and you have no helper” [al-Bukhari, 3039]. The truth of worshipping Allah alone did not change because Muslims were wounded or lost a round of fighting.

We must distinguish what happens to an individual from the message’s continuance after them. Khadija, may Allah be pleased with her, died before the conquest, and Musab died at Uhud. Their deaths before victory became manifest did not mean their sacrifices were lost. When Haram ibn Milhan was stabbed while carrying the Prophet’s ﷺ message, he said: “I have succeeded, by the Lord of the Ka`ba!” [al-Bukhari, 4091]. He saw success in being true to Allah and in what he hoped for with Him, rather than emerging physically unharmed from every trial.

Likewise, in the account of the people of the trench, a mother hesitated before the fire with her child. Her child affirmed that she was upon the truth and urged her: “mother, endure (this ordeal) for it is the Truth” [Muslim, 3005]. The painful worldly scene is not the believer’s final chapter.

Conversely, someone on falsehood may die without people seeing an outward punishment. Abdullah ibn Ubay was shrouded in the Prophet’s ﷺ shirt, and the Prophet offered his funeral prayer before the prohibition on praying for hypocrites was revealed [al-Bukhari, 1269]. This did not turn the hypocrisy Allah disclosed into guidance or righteousness. A person’s ultimate outcome cannot be judged from the appearance of their death alone, nor Allah’s promise measured by what becomes visible in one day or generation.

Communities also bear responsibility that cannot be reduced to the righteousness of scattered individuals. Letting oppression spread harms society. The Prophet ﷺ warned that when people see an oppressor and do not restrain him, Allah may soon encompass them in punishment [Abu Dawud, 4338]. Reform must follow what Allah has prescribed and remain within one’s ability. Resisting oppression must not become fresh oppression.

The verse then says: “Are they awaiting anything other than the fate of the earlier peoples?” Are they waiting to experience the consequence of denial themselves after learning what befell earlier peoples? “You will find no change in Allah’s way[17]” means that what Allah has told us of His way is true. “and you will find no alteration in Allah’s way[18].” rules out imagining that one can divert His decree from those who deserve it. One explanation of the distinction is that a creature cannot turn the punishment Allah intended into reward, or transfer it to someone other than whom Allah intended it to strike. This addresses believers too: they should not seek a path that abandons obedience while demanding its fruit. Dispute and disobedience at Uhud had their effects, and the warning came: “and do not dispute with one another, or else you will lose courage and your strength will weaken.” [8:46]. Confidence in the outcome goes with correcting one’s deeds. It does not cancel the means or justify demanding immediate results.

 [18] In transferring punishment to others in place of them.

Verse 44

Have they not traveled in the land to see what was the end of those who came before them? They were more powerful than them. Allah is not such that something can escape Him in the heavens or on earth. He is indeed All-Knowing, Most Capable.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fifth session, verses 42–45.

After discussing Allah’s way with those who deny, the verse calls them to consider what they can see of earlier peoples’ history and remains: “Have they not traveled in the land to see what was the end of those who came before them? They were more powerful than them.” The strength they boast about is not new to history. Earlier peoples were stronger, and this did not put them beyond Allah’s dominion. Travel that yields a lesson does not stop at admiring a building. It asks who built it and how their story ended. Learning the histories of nations belongs to this reflection, as well as moving from place to place.

Then comes the comprehensive denial: “Allah is not such that something can escape Him in the heavens or on earth.” No creature can flee somewhere beyond His knowledge or beyond the reach of His power. The verse ends with “All-Knowing, Most Capable”. A person may know what should be done but lack the means, or possess many tools without knowing how to use them. A craftsperson needs both knowledge and suitable ability to work well. Even with both, they remain limited and liable to error and inability. Allah’s knowledge encompasses everything and His power is complete. What an oppressor does is not hidden from Him, nor is He unable to seize them. Delayed punishment therefore does not mean a power has escaped Him. A believer does not need to see immediate punishment in every case to trust this truth. Looking at history removes pride in strength, while knowing Allah’s knowledge and power prevents despair when the full outcome is not yet visible.


Verse 45

If Allah were to take people to task for what they did, He would not leave a single living being on earth, but He gives them respite for an appointed term. When their appointed time comes, then Allah is All-Seeing of His slaves.

Quran translation: Rowwad Translation Center.
Source: Commentary on Surah Fatir — Dr. Ahmad Abd al-Munʿim; fifth session, verses 42–45.

The surah’s ending prevents us from understanding its earlier warnings as immediate punishment for every sin: “If Allah were to take people to task for what they did, He would not leave a single living being on earth”. If people immediately received everything they deserved, their present reprieve would not continue. Allah is forbearing and gives time: “but He gives them respite for an appointed term.” An oppressor’s ability to go on living therefore does not mean Allah is unaware of them. In Surah Ibrahim He says: “Never think that Allah is unaware of what the wrongdoers do[29]; He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare [in horror][30]” [14:42].

People become troubled when they add a specification Allah never gave to His promise, then treat the failure of their own deadline as a broken divine promise. The report of the Roman victory illustrates this. The Quran said they would win within a few years. Abu Bakr agreed on six years with some polytheists, before wagering was forbidden. Six years passed without victory; the Romans then prevailed in the seventh year [al-Tirmidhi, 3194]. Allah’s message did not fail. The deadline was narrower than “within a few years.” [30:4]. A believer must not choose a particular person, date or form of victory and make faith depend on victory appearing in that form.

They must also bring together Allah’s teaching about causes and His ways. The promise of victory does not cancel warnings against dispute and disobedience, and respite does not cancel the reckoning. An oppressed person may die without seeing outward justice in this world, yet what happened to them is not lost. In the report about the supplication of the oppressed, Allah says: “By My might and majesty, I will help you in due time.” [al-Tabarani, al-Muʿjam al-Kabir, 3718]. His help may come after a period of waiting.

One of the clearest pictures of complete justice is the report about the bankrupt person. A man comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, fasting and zakah, but has insulted one person, slandered another, consumed another’s wealth, shed another’s blood and struck another. Those whose rights he violated receive from his good deeds. If these run out before all claims are met, some of their sins are placed upon him, and then he is thrown into the Fire [Muslim, 2581]. An aggressor’s death without a worldly trial does not close the claims of those they harmed.

After mentioning those who persecuted believers, Surah al-Buruj reminds us: “and Allah is Witness over all things.” [85:9]. A right does not need a surviving human witness for Allah to preserve it. Fatir closes with the same meaning: “When their appointed time comes, then Allah is All-Seeing of His slaves.” He sees oppression and patience, and knows what every servant gave. The one granted time must not be deceived; the oppressed must not despair; and the worker must not treat obedience as a favour that obliges Allah to produce an outcome of their choosing. Among the early commands were: “and do not do a favor for a greater return[3]”; “and be patient for the sake of your Lord.” [74:6–7]. The ending thus returns the heart to the One praised at the surah’s opening: the powerful Creator, vast in mercy, knowing His servants, with whom no deed or right is lost.
