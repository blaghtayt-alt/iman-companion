## 55:1

> The Most Compassionate,

The surah opens with the name ‘The Most Compassionate’, a name belonging to Allah alone, glorified and exalted is He. It awakens hope in the heart and the desire to seek refuge in Him before the account of His blessings unfolds. The believer is already familiar with this name from the basmalah and the opening chapter of the Quran: ‘All praise be to Allah, the Lord of the worlds’ and ‘the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful’. Being introduced to the Lord is thus joined to being introduced to His mercy. 

This name appears in different settings throughout the Quran, notably in Surah Maryam. Here, however, it stands as a verse on its own at the beginning of a surah recounting Allah’s favours and blessings. It is followed by acts that show His mercy: teaching the Quran, creating the human being, teaching clear expression, placing creation at our service, and establishing the balance. Mercy is not confined to food, money, and bodily health. Among its greatest forms are Allah showing human beings the path of guidance, giving them the ability to understand it, and providing what protects their lives from injustice and corruption. 

His mercy to this community includes choosing Muhammad ﷺ for it and revealing the Quran to him. Revelation is not an extra burden added to human life, but a blessing that preserves its direction and points towards what benefits it. The coming commands and prohibitions concerning the balance should be read in this light: they too are expressions of mercy, just as creation, teaching, and placing creation at our service are. Opening our hearts to this name at the beginning of the surah calls us to recognize His mercy in His guidance and legislation, as well as in His worldly gifts.

## 55:2

> has taught the Qur’an,

The first blessing mentioned after the name of the Most Compassionate is that He ‘has taught the Qur’an’. Before the creation of the human being and the things placed at humanity’s service are mentioned, the surah presents what guides people and directs their purpose. Allah spoke this Quran and made it possible for people to learn it, understand it, and take heed of it. They have thus received both the gift of the Book’s revelation and the gift of being enabled to benefit from it. 

Considering the order of the surahs—An-Najm, then Al-Qamar, then Ar-Rahman—sheds light on this meaning. An-Najm affirms that revelation is knowledge from Allah, rather than speech born of desire. It exposes the invented names and conjecture that oppose revelation: ‘These [idols] are no more than names you and your forefathers have made up, for which Allah has not sent down any authority’, followed by ‘They follow nothing but conjecture and what their souls desire, although there has come to them guidance from their Lord’. 

Guidance has arrived, yet desire drives people to turn away from it. Near its end, the description ‘while heedlessly lost in amusement’ portrays them as distracted and preoccupied with amusement. Al-Qamar then opens with the visible sign they had requested: ‘The Hour has drawn near and the moon has split asunder’. Even so, ‘Whenever they see a sign, they turn away and say, “Same old magic!”’, and ‘They deny [the truth] and follow their desires, but every matter will reach its inevitable end’. 

Asking for more signs is not always a sincere search for guidance. Someone intent on turning away may see the very sign they requested, then give it another label to justify continuing to reject it. Al-Qamar repeatedly recounts the destruction of those who denied the truth and asks, ‘Then how were My punishment and My warnings?’ Alongside this warning, it recalls the blessing already available: ‘We have certainly made the Qur’an easy to understand and remember; is there anyone to take heed?’ Whoever wants to learn a lesson can find a warning in the fate of earlier peoples and sufficient guidance in the Quran made accessible to them. 

Demanding visible signs must not become an excuse for ignoring the sign they can already recite. From this perspective, one can discern a connection between the opening of Ar-Rahman and what precedes it: teaching this community the Quran—the greatest of the revealed Books and the guardian over earlier scriptures—is itself a mercy. It should not belittle this gift while seeking others. [In the Arabic phrase عَلَّمَ الْقُرْآنَ, the Quran is named as what Allah taught, but the recipient of that teaching is not stated. One explanation is that He taught it to the human being.] Since human beings first come into existence and then learn, why is their creation mentioned afterwards? 

The order here shows the importance of the purpose, rather than the chronological sequence of events. What guides human beings and sets their lives right is mentioned first so that its value is recognized before their gifts of existence and ability are described.

## 55:3

> He created man,

‘He created man’ recalls the fundamental gift that human beings cannot credit themselves with bringing about. Its position after the teaching of the Quran prevents existence from being separated from guidance. Merely being alive and moving does not fulfil the potential of the reason and natural disposition a person has been given. A person rises through receiving the light of revelation and acting upon it. To the extent that someone turns away from that light, they fall short of their human moral potential and their natural disposition is distorted. 

This does not mean that they cease to be biologically human, but that they neglect the understanding, insight, and upright conduct that elevate them. Quranic comparisons used in the context of turning away from Allah’s signs make this danger clear: ‘And relate to them [O Prophet] the story of the one whom We gave knowledge of Our revelations, but he abandoned it, so Satan took hold of him and he became one of the deviants’, followed by ‘If We had willed, We could have elevated him thereby, but he clung to the [life of the] earth and followed his desires. His likeness is that of a dog’. 

The opportunity to rise through those signs was open, but the person chose desire; the comparison reveals the consequence of that choice. Another image is: ‘The similitude of those who were given the Torah but failed to uphold it, is like that of a donkey laden with books’. The Book is present, yet the responsibility to understand it and act upon it has not been fulfilled, so its bearer has not benefited from what they carry. Possessing knowledge, or the means and texts through which it is acquired, is not enough if it leaves no effect on the person. 

Another connection appears with Surah At-Tin. One way of reflecting on its opening is to see references to places where revelation descended. The human being is then mentioned: ‘Surely We have created man in the best form’, ‘then We will reduce him to the lowest of the low’, with the exception ‘except those who believe and do righteous deeds; they will have a never-ending reward’. One lesson drawn from this context is that revelation protects our natural disposition and opens the way to being raised, whereas turning away from it leads to decline in this life and loss in the Hereafter. 

The gift of creation should therefore be read together with what comes before and after it: the Most Compassionate created the human being, taught what would guide them, and gave them the capacity to understand and act.

## 55:4

> and taught him speech.

The words ‘and taught him speech’ mean more than producing sounds or speaking eloquently. Clear expression involves both understanding and making oneself understood: a person grasps a meaning, then can express it and convey it to someone else. Even describing humans as beings capable of speech refers not simply to their voice, but to the ability to learn and understand on which speech depends. When someone says that they are in pain, they understand what pain means and choose a word to express it; the listener can then understand what they intend. 

Every sentence contains words whose meanings the speaker knows. They also understand the meaning produced by bringing those words together and connect the sentence to what comes before and after it. The listener shares in understanding this structure. In everyday conversation, all this seems simple, yet it is an immense gift combining comprehension of things with the ability to express them. Its origin is connected with what Allah taught Adam: ‘And He taught Adam the names of everything’. 

When the angels were asked about what they did not know, they said, ‘Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. Indeed, it is You Who are the All-Knowing, the All-Wise.’ Adam’s knowledge came through Allah’s teaching. He came down to earth equipped with this capacity: to observe and learn, distinguish and connect, draw conclusions and infer meanings, then explain what he understood. The gift of expression is therefore not limited to conversation; it includes the capacity to learn, discover, and pass on knowledge. 

The highest purpose towards which this capacity should be directed is the Quran, already mentioned at the opening. A person learns it to guide their reason and heart, then teaches it to others. In this regard, the Prophet ﷺ said, ‘The best of you are those who learn the Qur’an and teach it.’ The excellence of this learning and teaching comes from the excellence of the words being received and conveyed. Eloquence, however, does not make everything spoken true. 

A lie can be clearly phrased, and false information can be conveyed by someone skilled at arranging their words. There is a difference between being able to explain and using that ability to explain the truth. We therefore need to receive from the Quran and Sunnah what corrects our understanding, so that our words accord with reality and neither our minds nor our tongues become tools of deception and falsification. When truthfulness in speech breaks down, so do the standards by which people deal with one another: expression, given as a mercy, can be used to cause harm. 

The three blessings come together here: revelation that guides, a sound natural disposition receptive to guidance, and expression through which a person understands and conveys what they receive. Consequently, distancing people from the Quran, corrupting their natural disposition, and preventing them from understanding the language of their Book oppose this mercy and increase their hardship and distress. Estrangement from Allah’s words is not merely a lack of verbal fluency. 

Arabic words themselves can become unfamiliar to someone who needs to understand them, distancing that person from the meanings of the Quran and Sunnah. Benefiting from clear expression therefore calls us to learn what enables us to understand revelation and to use our ability in the service of truth, rather than simply admire beautiful speech.

## 55:5

> The sun and the moon follow their precise courses,

After the gifts of teaching, creation, and expression, attention turns to the sky: ‘The sun and the moon follow their precise courses’. The sun and moon move according to Allah’s precise reckoning and decree, rather than at random. The earlier actions explicitly show that Allah is the One who taught and created. Here, the One who made the sun and moon follow this reckoning is not named again, because the context and our natural disposition point to Him. 

Human beings did not establish their regularity, and no person can claim to direct them. This was the basis of Abraham’s argument against the man who disputed with him about his Lord: ‘It is Allah Who brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west’. The disbeliever was left dumbfounded: Abraham had brought him back to an evident reality over which he could not claim control. This reckoning is part of the mercy with which the surah opens. 

Allah mercifully gave the world regular laws that people can learn and use, so that they do not begin each day as though they knew nothing about the world they inhabit. We can appreciate this by considering the regularities on which life depends: the measures relating the sun and earth, the delicate balances within the body, including the functioning of hormones, and what people know about how iron, wood, and water respond to heat. If these things did not follow regular patterns but changed unpredictably every day, people would repeatedly have to rediscover what they had already learned: how to use iron, when water boils, and how to care for their bodies. 

Stable knowledge, industry, and work would become impossible. The point is to recognize the blessing of consistent laws, not to suggest that created things never change or that bodies always remain in one condition. The One who made the sun and moon follow a precise reckoning has provided human beings with a world in which they can live and learn its laws. As we benefit from the precision of His creation, we must not overlook the Creator’s mercy.

## 55:6

> and the stars[1] and trees prostrate [to Allah][2].

According to one interpretation, the verse ‘and the stars and trees prostrate [to Allah]’ turns our attention from the sun and moon in the sky to plants on earth. [In that interpretation, the Arabic النَّجْمُ—rendered as “stars” in the translation above—means plants without an upright stem, while الشَّجَرُ refers to plants with a trunk or stem.] Both types of plants are thus united in submission to Allah, just as the heavenly bodies follow His reckoning. 

[After يَسْجُدَانِ, the Arabic does not state to whom they prostrate, because the intended meaning is known: they prostrate to Allah, who created them and subjected them to His command.] None but Him deserves worship. The picture of His blessings therefore includes not only precisely determined measures, but also the obedience of His creatures to their Lord. Connected with this is His statement: ‘It is He Who made the earth smooth for you, so travel through its regions and eat from His provisions’. 

The earth on which human beings travel and whose good things they enjoy was not made suitable for them by their own power, but by Allah’s making it serve their needs. Here the difference becomes clear between these creatures subjected to His command and the human being charged with the trust. Humans have been given the ability to choose, shown the way of guidance, and required to respond to it and strive against what distracts them from it. This ability does not authorize them to dispense with their Creator’s guidance or make desire the final standard for every choice. 

It is the basis of their responsibility. As they see creation around them ordered by Allah’s command, they themselves need something to govern the actions they choose. The scene of prostration thus connects with the balance established in the next verse. Having creation around us placed at our service is not sufficient for a sound human life unless we know how to use what we have been given and what is right when our wishes conflict with those of others.

## 55:7

> He raised the heaven and enjoined justice

‘He raised the heaven and enjoined justice’ brings together the display of power in raising the heaven and the display of mercy in establishing what enables people’s lives to be set right. The balance has been interpreted as justice, and also as the instrument used to weigh things and determine their measures. On the first interpretation, its scope includes the standards by which rights are recognized and words and actions assessed. On the second, it provides a tangible example that prevents giving people less or more than their due. 

Linking the raising of the heaven with the establishment of the balance invites several reflections. One is the contrast between a world operating under Allah’s command and human life, with its choices, which needs a standard to refer to. Another is that the heavens and earth are founded upon justice, and that creation’s affairs cannot be set right through chaos and oppression. In revelation, the balance is inseparable from the Book. The Book explains what is true and also teaches how matters should be weighed against it. 

Allah therefore says, ‘We have sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and sent down with them the Scripture and the criteria of justice, so that the people may uphold justice.’ The messengers brought clear proofs not simply so that people would possess information, but so that they would know what is right and uphold justice through it. This broad meaning includes revelation guiding the mind towards sound reasoning. It teaches what is lawful and unlawful and provides meanings through which scholars, equipped with the proper methods of inference, understand situations and apply established rulings to analogous cases. 

New events continue to arise, while the Quran’s revelation is complete. This does not mean that its guidance no longer addresses new circumstances: scholars return to its meanings and indications to understand the rulings relevant to them. This understanding has a place in Allah’s words, ‘If they referred it to the Messenger or to those in authority among them, those with sound judgment among them could know it.’ Inference relates a situation to its evidence; it is not an opinion devised independently of revelation and then given a religious label. 

[We can thus understand the connection between the Arabic phrases عَلَّمَهُ الْبَيَانَ and وَوَضَعَ الْمِيزَانَ: Allah gave human beings the capacity to understand and did not leave them without guidance on how to use it.] Not everyone who can speak or compare things has reached a just conclusion. A sound standard and the knowledge to apply it properly are necessary. Whenever people abandon this balance, they distance themselves from the mercy through which Allah protects their rights, even if they retain measuring instruments and the ability to argue.

## 55:8

> so that you may not transgress the limits of justice.

‘so that you may not transgress the limits of justice’ explains why the balance was established: so that people do not overstep what is right or corrupt the standard that brings them back to it. Transgression does not always begin with an obvious act of aggression. It may begin by changing what trustworthiness and fitness mean, until someone who causes harm is promoted and someone capable of doing good is excluded. One of its clearest forms is assigning responsibility to those unfit to bear it. 

A Bedouin came to the Prophet ﷺ while he was speaking to his Companions and asked when the Hour would come. The Prophet ﷺ continued speaking, then returned to the questioner when he had finished and said, ‘When honesty is lost, then wait for the Hour (Doomsday).’ When asked how honesty would be lost, he replied, ‘When the power or authority comes in the hands of unfit persons’, again telling him to await the Hour. He gave a practical example of trust being lost, rather than restricting it to safeguarding money or returning a deposit. 

It includes responsibilities in government, the judiciary, giving religious rulings, and all other work entrusted to someone. [The Arabic وُسِّد means that a matter has been assigned to someone and placed in their care.] When an office becomes a source of comfort and personal advantage for someone unable to fulfil it, rights connected with that work are lost. The harm goes beyond the person who received an undeserved position; it reaches everyone whose affairs have been placed in their hands. 

This relates to the reversal of standards described by the Prophet ﷺ: ‘Before the Dajjal, there will be deceptive years in which the truthful will be denied and the liar will be believed.’ The narration goes on to describe the trustworthy being accused of treachery and the treacherous being trusted. The calamity is not simply that betrayal and lying exist, but that people’s standards change until they regard the traitor as worthy of trust and accuse the person who faithfully fulfils it. 

Someone may be admired for their abilities and outward appearance while their inward corruption is ignored. In the hadith about the removal of trustworthiness, a man is praised with the words, ‘What a wise, polite, and strong man he is’, even though his heart contains less faith than the weight of a mustard seed. Admiration for skill and strength is therefore insufficient to judge a person’s uprightness and trustworthiness. When the standard of truth collapses, power prevails, and the weak are the first to suffer injustice. 

The Quran shows how this is connected with denying recompense: ‘Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense?’ and ‘Such is the one who repulses the orphan harshly’—pushing the orphan away violently. Someone who forgets the reckoning, surrenders to desire, and accepts no standard restraining them from wrongdoing treats the rights of those unable to defend themselves as insignificant. Preserving the balance thus protects people; it is not merely an abstract theory or a narrow matter of personal etiquette. Corrupt standards of trust, rights, and authority open the way to forms of oppression that retaining the same names and slogans cannot remedy.

## 55:9

> Therefore establish weights in justice, and do not give short measure.

After prohibiting transgression, the surah commands positive action: ‘Therefore establish weights in justice, and do not give short measure.’ It is not enough to know that truth has a standard; we must uphold it justly and not diminish it. This includes giving full measure and respecting people’s rights. When the balance is understood as justice, it also includes preserving revelation’s standards in assessing and judging. Just as exceeding what is right causes harm, so does discarding the standards that do not suit one’s wishes. 

We need to know what is true and false, what is bad and good, what constitutes a good life, and how the merit of an action or the soundness of a path should be measured. Sheer abundance is not proof of goodness. Allah says, ‘Evil and good are not equal, even if the abundance of evil may impress you.’ We may be impressed by abundance, but admiration does not change what a thing is. The balance brings us back from being dazzled by quantity to recognizing goodness. 

This insight becomes especially necessary when trials are made attractive and standards are reversed, culminating in the tremendous deception of the Dajjal, the false messiah. Yet those whom Allah guides will not be confused about him. The Prophet ﷺ said that the word kāfir, meaning disbeliever, would be written between his eyes, ‘which every believer will read, whether he is literate or illiterate’. Another report says that ‘everyone who would resent his deeds would be able to read’, or, in the alternative wording transmitted, every believer would read it. 

It is therefore not simply a matter of reading skill, but of faith that exposes the trial and is not deceived by its attractive appearance. This connects with sound guidance in Allah’s words: ‘But Allah has endeared faith to you and made it pleasing to your hearts, and has made disbelief, wickedness, and disobedience hateful to you. Such are the rightly guided’. Part of sound judgment is seeing disobedience for what it is and disliking it, rather than having one’s loves reshaped until one approves of what harms oneself. 

Keeping the balance upright concerns what the heart loves and hates, as well as what the mind knows. This balance is then tested in ordinary life: how do we judge a person or an event? How do we choose work or a way to earn a living? Do we consult people of knowledge about what pleases Allah, or consider profit alone? Within a family, spouses need to know their rights and the rules governing their relationship, so that strength or desire does not become their measure of fairness. 

Markets and community affairs likewise have rights and responsibilities that are corrupted when desire alone decides. These matters are not remote from the surah’s opening; they are places where human beings must uphold the balance established by the Most Compassionate. The reflection returns to the trust humanity accepted and to Allah’s words: ‘Indeed, We offered the Trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, yet they refused to bear it and were afraid of it. But man assumed it; he is indeed wrongful and ignorant’. 

Bearing that trust properly requires striving against these two failings: injustice through justice, and ignorance through knowledge. Doing what is right cannot rest on knowledge that is not put into just action, or on an intention to be fair without knowing how matters should be judged. Both knowledge and justice are present in this opening passage: teaching the Quran and clear expression opens the way to knowledge, while establishing the balance and commanding fairness directs action. 

The end thus connects with the beginning. The mercy that made the Lord known to human beings, taught them, and placed creation at their service calls them to learn about Allah and act justly towards His servants, so that their abilities bring about good rather than corruption.
