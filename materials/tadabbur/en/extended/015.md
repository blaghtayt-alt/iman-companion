## 15:1

> Alif Lām Ra. These are the verses of the Book and a clear Qur’an.

The surah opens by declaring that what people have before them is “the verses of the Book and a clear Qur’an”. Revelation is clear in itself; the problem is not its inability to explain, but the barriers erected around it that prevent it from reaching people or turn their hearts away. Preservation is one of the surah’s prominent themes: preserving the Reminder, guarding heaven against the devils’ eavesdropping, protecting Allah’s sincere servants from Iblis’s authority, saving whomever Allah wills from punishment, and protecting the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) against the mockers. This need not be the surah’s only subject, but it is a clear thread recurring in several places. It helps us understand the struggle between revelation that reaches people, devils that seek to turn them away from it, and human beings who choose either to respond or to turn away.

## 15:2

> The disbelievers will soon wish that they were Muslims.

The verse “The disbelievers will soon wish that they were Muslims” opens a window onto a moment when the truth they now reject becomes clear to them, until they wish they had embraced Islam. One interpretation places this wish at the time when Islam’s victory and ascendancy become evident; another places it when people’s destiny in the Hereafter becomes clear and the people of Paradise enter Paradise. On either reading, their present rejection does not alter the truth. It merely delays their acknowledgement until a time when wishing may no longer help. This fits the recurring theme of truth becoming evident when punishment arrives. A person may resist many signs, only for evasion to collapse when something they cannot ward off descends upon them; what they had disputed then becomes clear.

## 15:3

> Let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted with false hope; they will come to know.

The words “Let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted with false hope” describe an atmosphere of heedlessness: preoccupation with desire, pleasure, and hopes of a long life, until an ever-stretching worldly future takes the place of preparation for what lies beyond it. The fault is not in food or enjoyment themselves, but in their becoming distractions from revelation. This is one avenue of Satan’s work. Unable to tamper with the preserved Reminder, he distracts people from it through earthly attractions and endless hopes. The beginning of the surah thus connects with its ending: someone whose heart is filled with the Quran does not need to gaze longingly at every passing attraction, whereas someone inwardly empty of revelation is more easily drawn to whatever distracts them from it.

## 15:4

> We never destroyed any town except that it had a destined time.

The verse “We never destroyed any town except that it had a destined time” establishes that punishment is not a hasty reaction brought on simply by people demanding it or mocking it. It has a decree and an appointed time known to Allah. A delay in recompense should therefore not be mistaken for inability or inattention: the One who sent down revelation also controls the timing of punishment. This steadies those who see the followers of falsehood given respite and enjoying themselves. The continuing respite is itself part of the decree, and may provide an opportunity to call people to faith or for them to return before what cannot be repelled arrives.

## 15:5

> No nation can advance its appointed time, nor can they delay it.

The verse “No nation can advance its appointed time, nor can they delay it” completes the preceding meaning: no nation reaches its appointed end ahead of time or outlasts it. The timing belongs neither to the mocker nor to the caller to faith who longs for swift relief; it belongs to Allah. The truth of the promise is therefore not measured by how quickly people’s expectations are fulfilled. Nor should delayed punishment become a source of doubt in the heart, because the respite itself remains under the authority of the One who set the appointed time.

## 15:6

> They say, “O you to whom the Reminder has been sent down, You are surely insane!

Their words “O you to whom the Reminder has been sent down, You are surely insane!” contain a striking contradiction. In their mocking phrasing, they implicitly acknowledge the claim that a Reminder has been sent down to him, without sincerely accepting it, then shift the discussion from the content of revelation to ridicule of its bearer. This pattern has recurred with the messengers: unable to confront the message, a stubborn opponent may turn to maligning or mocking the messenger. Ridicule is therefore no proof that truth is weak. It may instead be a screen used when revelation itself is difficult to answer.

## 15:7

> Why do you not bring us the angels, if you are truthful?”

Their demand “Why do you not bring us the angels, if you are truthful?” is not merely an innocent request for further evidence. The context shows that someone who has chosen to turn away may ask for a tangible sign, then reinterpret it in a way that preserves their previous position. Angels are no passing detail in this surah: they appear in connection with revelation, the good news given to Abraham, the punishment of Lot’s people, and the account of Adam. Their demand that angels descend therefore raises a question: do they want mercy and revelation to descend, or punishment after which no respite remains?

## 15:8

> We do not send down the angels except for a just cause, and then [the disbelievers] would be granted no respite.

The verse “We do not send down the angels except for a just cause, and then [the disbelievers] would be granted no respite” explains that the visible descent of angels is not a game to satisfy curiosity. They may descend with a decisive command, including punishment. If a conclusive, tangible sign comes and denial persists, the respite ends. It was therefore part of Allah’s mercy that Gabriel should descend with revelation, rather than angels descending to destroy the stubborn rejecters. 

The surah itself shows angels bringing Abraham good news and bringing Lot’s people punishment. Similarly, the angel of the mountains offered to crush those who had harmed the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) between the two mountains known as al-Akhshabayn. The Prophet chose instead to hope that among their descendants would be people who worshipped Allah [al-Bukhari: 3231; Muslim: 1795]. The blessing of revelation’s descent calls for acceptance before truth comes in a form that leaves no room to choose.

## 15:9

> It is We Who have sent down the Reminder, and it is We Who will preserve it.

The verse “It is We Who have sent down the Reminder, and it is We Who will preserve it” stands at the heart of the surah’s theme of preservation. The One who sent down the Reminder has undertaken to preserve it. No devil can alter the revelation itself or cut off its descent to the Messenger. This meaning also appears in another passage about revelation: “This [Qur’an] is not sent down by the devils”; “it is not for them to do this, nor is it within their capability” [Ash-Shu‘ara: 210–211]. Believers nevertheless retain a different kind of responsibility: to preserve the Quran in their hearts, protect its meanings against alteration, false interpretation, and fragmentation, and convey it to those prevented by barriers from hearing it. Allah’s preservation of revelation itself does not release its people from the trust of bearing it.

## 15:11

> No messenger came to them except that they ridiculed him.

The verse “No messenger came to them except that they ridiculed him” presents ridicule as a longstanding pattern on the path of the messengers. Someone calling to faith should not interpret it as a sign that the truth they carry is weak. The recurrence of this response among different peoples frees the heart from waiting for people’s acceptance as a test of the message’s truth. The task of the Messenger, and of those who bear revelation after him, is to convey it. Mocking reactions are neither new nor capable of changing the reality of what he brought.

## 15:12

> This is how We let this [denial] enter the hearts of the wicked.

In the verse “This is how We let this [denial] enter the hearts of the wicked”, the pronoun has been interpreted in more than one way. [The point concerns the Arabic suffix -hu in naslukuhu (نَسْلُكُهُ), meaning it.] One interpretation takes this pronoun to refer to denial or ridicule: the rejection they have chosen enters their hearts and settles there until they become unreceptive to the Quran. Another takes it to refer to the Reminder, meaning that it enters their hearts but the wrongdoer does not benefit from it because of the choice they have made. Both interpretations reveal the danger of persistence in rejection. The heart does not remain a neutral vessel forever. Someone who repeatedly refuses may reach a point where what should guide them becomes burdensome instead.

## 15:13

> They will not believe in it, although the examples of the earlier people are well established.

The verse “They will not believe in it, although the examples of the earlier people are well established” connects their condition with an earlier pattern: the presence of signs does not compel a heart that has chosen wrongdoing to believe. A believer should therefore not imagine that the problem is always a shortage of evidence or a failure to present it effectively. The explanation may be clear, yet denial remain. This does not remove the duty to convey the message clearly, but it prevents us from making guidance depend on the number of proofs alone.

The point is not to diminish the value of tangible signs, but to expose how a stubborn heart can turn even direct observation into fresh material for rejection.

## 15:15

The verse “Our eyes have been dazzled; rather, we have been bewitched” gives the response expected from a heart unwilling to accept: instead of acknowledging that it has seen, it claims to have been bewitched. Seeing more does not always mean believing more. Sometimes the problem lies in a will that reinterprets every piece of evidence to avoid having to change. This explains why the Quran’s answer to their demand was not simply to send another tangible sign, but to expose the underlying refusal to listen.

## 15:16

> We have placed constellations in the heaven and adorned it for the onlookers,

The verse “We have placed constellations in the heaven and adorned it for the onlookers” presents another kind of adornment. Allah has adorned heaven so that people look upwards and their hearts rise towards the signs of His dominion. In contrast, Iblis later says: “I will surely make [sins] alluring to them on earth”. The very direction of one’s gaze thus becomes part of the struggle: adornment that leads to remembrance, and adornment used to immerse the heart in earthly concerns. The point is not to abandon worldly life, but to prevent its attractions from monopolising the gaze and turning it away from revelation and what lies above.

## 15:26

> We have created man from sounding clay, made of aging mud.

The verse “We have created man from sounding clay, made of aging mud” recalls the body’s origin. [The Arabic word masnūn has been explained as poured and shaped, or as clay left until its smell has changed.] The description of this mud recurs in the surah’s account of Adam’s creation, highlighting the transformation of that humble origin into an honoured human being after the spirit is breathed into him. This offers a lesson echoed through the surah: just as the body cannot live without a spirit, a person cannot rise spiritually while cutting themselves off from revelation, which the Quran elsewhere calls “a revelation by Our command” [Ash-Shura: 52]. [In that passage, the Arabic word rūḥ means spirit; the published English translation renders it as revelation.] Matter alone therefore does not explain human dignity or purpose.

## 15:82

The verse “They carved houses in the mountains, feeling secure” captures an illusion of protection in its description “feeling secure”. ## 15:87

> We have surely given you the seven oft-repeated verses and the great Qur’an.

Before the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) is told “Do not look longingly”, Allah reminds him of His gift: “the seven oft-repeated verses and the great Qur’an”. The seven oft-repeated verses have been understood as Al-Fatihah, alongside other interpretations. The point here is that the revelation he received is richer and more lasting than enjoyment that passes away. A Hadith states: “Whoever does not recite Qur'an in a nice voice is not from us” [al-Bukhari: 7527]. [One interpretation of the Arabic verb yataghannā is to find sufficiency in the Quran rather than in other things.] This meaning connects with an inward human need.

The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) related that after Allah fashioned Adam, Iblis began circling him. Seeing that he was hollow, Iblis recognised that Adam was a creature unable to keep himself fully under control [Muslim: 2611]. Human beings have an inner space and needs. The problem is not that they need something, but what they fill that need with. Just as the body is filled with food, the heart needs provision. If revelation does not fill it, it will keep looking longingly for an endless succession of attractions to make up the lack. An unconfirmed report about the occasion of this verse’s revelation says that the Muslims looked at caravans carrying wealth and goods, which were then contrasted with the Quran they possessed. A caravan passes away, whereas revelation forms people, its effects are renewed, and its riches do not run out.

Then “nor grieve over them” prevents the heart from being drained by the rejection of those who chose that path, while “but lower your wing [in humility] to the believers” directs its energy towards caring for those who turn to revelation, rather than remaining captive to those who turn away.

## 15:90

> just as We sent down [punishment] upon those who divided [their religion],

The words “just as We sent down [punishment] upon those who divided [their religion]” have been interpreted in several ways. One interpretation identifies these people as those who divided responsibilities and swore together to fight the truth, a meaning close to the words about Salih’s people: “Let us all swear by Allah that we will kill him and his family by night.” [An-Naml: 49]. Another says they divided their opinions and positions concerning the Quran, each group adopting a different kind of attack. 

Another identifies them as those who shared out Makkah’s roads and entrances to deter newcomers from hearing the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him). One would warn of a sorcerer, another of a poet, and another of a soothsayer. Objections multiplied until a newcomer felt that reaching the truth was impossible. These interpretations show that resistance to revelation can be organised, with distributed roles, rather than merely a passing objection.

## 15:91

> those who split the Qur’an into different portions.

The words “those who split the Qur’an into different portions” have been understood as dividing the Quran into parts, accepting some and abandoning others. As Allah says: “Do you then believe in part of the Scripture and deny another part?” [Al-Baqarah: 85]. [On this interpretation, the Arabic word ʿiḍīn means parts or pieces.] Another interpretation is that they spread differing accusations about it—sorcery, poetry, soothsaying, and tales—to stop people approaching it with open hearts. Two kinds of preservation emerge here: Allah preserved the words of the Reminder against alteration, and the people of the Quran must protect its meanings against fragmentation. Religion should not be confined to the mosque, with its rulings and ethics separated from the rest of life, nor should what suits one’s desires be accepted and what opposes them rejected.

## 15:94

> Then proclaim what you are commanded, and turn away from those who associate partners with Allah.

The words “Then proclaim what you are commanded” answer the enclosure built around the call to faith. [The Arabic word ṣadʿ means splitting or breaking, as glass cracks; the command here is to proclaim openly.] When barriers aim to restrain the caller and prevent movement, kill him, or expel him, the Quran gathers those methods in the words: “And [remember] when the disbelievers plotted against you, to take you as captive, kill you or expel you.” [Al-Anfal: 30]. The answer is not for the bearer of truth to keep revelation behind yet another barrier, but to act, reach people, and proclaim what was commanded. Everyone has some sphere in which they can break part of the isolation from the Quran: at home, at work, in their studies, or in their surroundings.

## 15:95

> We will surely suffice you against the mockers,

The verse “We will surely suffice you against the mockers” connects Allah’s protection with carrying out the duty of conveying the message, not withdrawing from it. This meaning appears elsewhere. Allah says: “O Messenger, convey what has been sent down to you from your Lord.” … “Allah will protect you from the people.” [Al-Ma’idah: 67]. At the end of Surah Al-Jinn, conveying the message is also presented as the refuge from Allah’s command [Al-Jinn: 20–23]. 

This protection does not mean that a bearer of truth will never be harmed. Prophets and believers may suffer harm and be killed, but conveying the message neither brings the appointed time forward nor delays it. Fear of calling to faith does not create a new lifespan, just as going forth in Allah’s cause does not shorten the span already decreed: “Even if you had been in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have come out to their killing places.” [Al ‘Imran: 154].

## 15:96

> those who set up beside Allah another god; they will come to know.

The verse “those who set up beside Allah another god; they will come to know” shifts attention from personal offence to the central issue. The mockers did not merely mistreat the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him); they associated partners with Allah. This helps someone calling to faith avoid making personal dignity the centre of the struggle. They carry a message greater than themselves. A person who insults or mocks them should not be answered merely to vindicate the self, but reminded of the truth they have neglected.

## 15:97

> We know that you are distressed by what they say.

The verse “We know that you are distressed by what they say” does not deny the pain of being hurt; it acknowledges it and gives direction. The surah was revealed during a Makkan period of intensified restriction and mockery. One view places it late in that period, when Quraysh’s oppression of the believers increased. Steadfastness does not mean that the heart is unaffected, but that distress does not bring worship and conveying the message to a halt. Allah knows what weighs upon the breast before prescribing the remedy.

## 15:98

> So glorify your Lord with praise and be among those who prostrate [to Him],

The remedy follows immediately: “So glorify your Lord with praise and be among those who prostrate [to Him]”. In the face of people’s words, the heart returns to declaring Allah free of imperfection, praising Him, and prostrating to Him. Glorification restores a right understanding of the One who holds power and determines the appointed time. Prostration returns the servant to their true place, instead of leaving the self captive to what others say about it. Worship is not merely a rest from calling to faith; it is provision that protects the worshipper from being consumed by harm.

## 15:99

> and worship your Lord until the certainty [of death] comes to you.

The verse “and worship your Lord until the certainty [of death] comes to you” brings the path to its close without a human timetable for victory: continue worshipping and conveying the message until death, whether you see the fruit of your work or your appointed time comes while you are still on the way. Preserving this religion and granting it victory do not depend on one person’s life. The individual’s task is to remain firm in what they were entrusted to do until the end. Whoever fills the heart with revelation, worships their Lord, and openly proclaims what they were commanded has fulfilled their role, even without witnessing the final scene of victory.
