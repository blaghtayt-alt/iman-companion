## 62:1

> All that is in the heavens and all that is on earth glorifies Allah, the Sovereign, the Most Holy, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise.

Al-Jumuah follows As-Saff in a striking sequence. As-Saff describes a confrontation that requires believers to stand in ranks like a firmly joined structure. Al-Jumuah returns the community, in its ordinary daily life, to the regular gathering for remembrance and prayer, so that individuals’ worldly activities do not turn into division and dispersal. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) repeatedly recited this surah to the Companions in Friday prayer, together with Al-Munafiqun. 

Meanings repeated at these gatherings are not superfluous: the heart needs to hear them again and again to keep its judgment sound. The surah therefore begins with ‘glorifies’. [The Quran expresses glorification in Arabic through a verbal noun, past-tense and present-tense verbs, and commands. Here the present-tense form يُسَبِّحُ conveys continuity.] The universe never stops glorifying Allah; the angels glorify Him night and day without tiring. 

Someone leaving a gathering of remembrance or becoming distracted by trade does Allah no harm. He is free of need; the person who leaves deprives themselves of the honor of living in harmony with an obedient universe. Then come the names ‘the Sovereign, the Most Holy, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise’. He is the Sovereign because everything belongs to Him and is under His authority; the Most Holy because He is free of every imperfection. Believing in this name should lead a servant to strive to purify their own soul for Allah. 

Purifying oneself is also among the interpretations mentioned for the angels’ words ‘proclaim Your holiness’. [The Arabic وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ has been understood, among other interpretations, as purifying ourselves for You; the published translation expresses proclaiming Allah’s holiness.] He is the All-Mighty, who cannot be overcome, and the All-Wise, whose commands and choices never depart from wisdom. This opening connects with the trust that the heavens, earth and mountains declined to bear out of fear, while the human being undertook it. 

Humans have the capacity to choose and are called to fulfill Allah’s will on earth amid the competing claims of the self, desire and Satan, rather than becoming a discordant presence in a universe that glorifies Him. The mention of the Messenger’s mission immediately after glorification suggests a connection: revelation shows human beings how to join this unceasing glorification by their own choice.

## 62:2

> It is He Who has sent among the unlettered [Arabs] a messenger from among themselves, reciting to them His verses and purifying them, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom [i.e., Sunnah], although before that they were clearly misguided.

‘It is He Who has sent among the unlettered [Arabs] a messenger from among themselves’ connects the sending of the Messenger with the divine names just mentioned. The perfection of Allah’s sovereignty, might and wisdom includes His choosing whom He wills to bear His message. The unlettered people have been understood as the Arabs, who had not received a scripture and most of whom could neither read nor write. The Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) brought them a Book, transforming a people without scripture into a community entrusted with revelation. 

This choice conferred honor and responsibility, not a permanent racial privilege. Arabic was honored by the revelation of the Quran in that language. An Arab who devotes their greatest efforts to worldly life while accepting ignorance of their scripture’s language neglects a gift they have received. This does not remove the need to learn other people’s languages and useful sciences as religious outreach and everyday life require. A report condemning worldly expertise combined with ignorance of the Hereafter states: ‘Verily, Allah the Exalted hates everyone who is knowledgeable of the Worldly life, but ignorant of the Hereafter’. 

Scholars have differed over the authenticity of this report; its inclusion in Sahih al-Jami should be read alongside the discussion of its weakening in Al-Silsilah al-Daifah. The Prophet’s being unlettered was not a deficiency in him. Rather, one indication of his messengership is that he did not acquire this Book through a human school: Allah taught him. The verse then defines the mission’s tasks: ‘reciting to them His verses’. People need to hear their Lord’s words, rather than have them replaced with an abundance of human speech. 

The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said: ‘The lengthening of prayer by a man and the shortness of the sermon is the sign of his understanding (of faith)’. Explanation should lead people to the Quran, not obscure it. Then comes ‘purifying them’. [The Arabic word تَزْكِيَة combines purification and growth.] The Quran removes the soul’s inherited ideas, habits and desires from the time of ignorance, then develops a new outlook and character: cleansing away impurity and adorning the soul with truth. 

A related pairing appears in ‘Whoever rejects Tāghoot [i.e., false gods] and believes in Allah’. Purification is mentioned first here because knowledge entering a hard, unpurified heart may become an instrument of pride and argument rather than guidance. Next comes ‘teaching them the Book and Wisdom [i.e., Sunnah]’. Wisdom has been interpreted as the Sunnah; it has also been understood to include placing what one learns in its proper setting and wisely applying what Allah asks of us. 

The Quran’s effect is therefore more than an accumulation of information: revelation is recited, the outlook is purified, and knowledge and practice are developed. The words ‘although before that they were clearly misguided’ then become clear. What people had previously treated as familiar and normal is exposed by the Quran as clear error, just as the jinn, after hearing it, recognized that the foolish one among them had spoken outrageous falsehood about Allah.

## 62:3

> And [he is also sent] to others of them who have not yet joined them, and He is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise.

Allah did not confine the fruits of the mission to Arabs or the first generation: ‘others of them who have not yet joined them’. These others have been interpreted as non-Arabs, specifically Persians in view of the report concerning Salman, or the generation after the Companions and those who followed them. The meaning encompasses those who join their path through Islam, knowledge and action. When the verse was revealed and the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) was asked about these people, he placed his hand on Salman al-Farisi. 

The authentic narration conveys that, even if faith were as far away as the Pleiades, men from among those people would attain it. The image is powerful: faith may seem as distant as the stars, yet some will strive until they reach it. By contrast, someone may live close to a mosque or a lesson and still make no move to attend. The surah thus makes joining the earlier believers a practical undertaking, not a wish. Admiration alone is not enough: one learns the Quran, purifies the soul through it, acts on what one learns, and struggles to close the distance between one’s condition and theirs. 

The words ‘who have not yet joined them’ indicate that they have not arrived yet, while the way remains open. [The Arabic لَمَّا يَلْحَقُوا expresses not having joined yet.] A later generation or a non-Arab people can follow the same path; history has seen great non-Arab scholars render immense service to the Quran and Sunnah. This also invites reflection on how one family member’s decision can change the course of generations. Islam enters a home and the descendants become Muslims; or the preservation of religion within a family weakens, leaving the next generation with an inherited distance whose beginnings they did not choose. 

The principle ‘That no bearer of burden will bear the burden of another’ remains intact, yet upbringing and choices have lasting effects. This relates to Ibrahim’s prayer, ‘join me with the righteous’, and the joining of descendants to believers mentioned in At-Tur. The question for those born after the Companions is not whether they lived in their time. It is whether they truly want to follow their path and are willing to undertake the learning, purification and action it requires.

## 62:4

> That is the bounty of Allah that He gives to whom He wills, for Allah is the Lord of great bounty.

‘That is the bounty of Allah that He gives to whom He wills, for Allah is the Lord of great bounty’ returns every preceding gift to its source. Prophethood is a gift; so are the choice of a community to carry the message, the language in which the Book was revealed, and the ability of later believers to follow those who went before. These have all been mentioned as interpretations of what the verse points to. They need not conflict: they are different expressions of a single source of generosity from Allah. 

This guards against two opposite failings. Those who serve the religion must not become conceited and claim the credit for themselves; those who are far away must not despair, imagining that the path of the earlier believers is closed to them. Allah is ‘the Lord of great bounty’. His generosity is open to those who turn to Him sincerely and strive for faith, knowledge and action. Yet being chosen does not become a hereditary guarantee. Someone whom Allah entrusts with a responsibility can be replaced if they neglect its demands: ‘If you turn away, He will replace you with other people’. 

The very next verse therefore presents a community given a Book whose trust they failed to fulfill properly. Allah’s favor raises a question that cannot be avoided: what will you do with it? Seeking to follow the Companions means seeking neither a title nor status, but asking Allah to employ us in the work they performed. A fitting prayer is to ask Him to use us in His service and not replace us.

## 62:5

> The similitude of those who were given the Torah but failed to uphold it[1], is like that of a donkey laden with books[2]. How terrible is the similitude of those who reject the verses of Allah! And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.

The surah moves from the gift of the mission and the opportunity to follow its first bearers to the danger of wasting that gift: ‘The similitude of those who were given the Torah but failed to uphold it’. [The Arabic حُمِّلُوا has been interpreted as being charged with acting upon the Torah, entrusted with preserving, understanding and conveying its text, or bearing it through sound understanding followed by practice.] In each case, the problem is not simply possessing a book, but accepting a trust and failing to fulfill it properly. 

The history of the Children of Israel therefore matters to the Quran’s community: they passed from weakness to strength, and forms of deviation and replacement later appeared among them. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) warned his community against imitating earlier peoples: ‘You will follow the ways of those nations who were before you, span by span and cubit by cubit’. Then comes the striking comparison, ‘like that of a donkey laden with books’. 

[The Arabic أَسْفَار means books, and its root is associated with uncovering and making clear.] The verse names books, not just any load: what could have illuminated a path and a life becomes weight on the back instead of light in the heart. More books make a heavier burden without changing the animal. The warning concerns not ignorance alone, but knowledge that never becomes action. Someone may memorize texts and boast of information, while outside the mosque their behavior remains governed by desire and self-interest. 

This connects with As-Saff: ‘why do you say what you do not do?’ When conduct contradicts speech, knowledge becomes evidence against its bearer rather than in their favor. Faulty understanding also enters this warning when a text is distorted or selectively used to serve personal advantage; the problem is not limited to neglecting practice after understanding correctly. The Quran addresses worship, wealth, family, politics, the marketplace and character. 

It is not a book to be recited in isolation from life. Confining it to a shelf or a gathering of worship while building one’s life on a different authority approaches the condition of carrying it without benefiting from it. The danger grows when a person earns through their knowledge and is tempted to conceal or alter the truth, or please someone in power at its expense. Knowledge then becomes a resource for protecting interests instead of governing them. 

Hence ‘How terrible is the similitude of those who reject the verses of Allah!’: those described knew the verses, but their refusal to bear them in life and their practical distortion amounted to denial through deeds. The warning reaches the Quran’s community directly. Allah has preserved the Quran’s text, but the community is still responsible for preserving its meanings, acting on it and conveying it. A distinction can also be drawn between Quranic comparisons: here, knowledge carried without effect is likened to a donkey’s load; the dog in Al-Araf illustrates someone who cast off the signs after receiving them. 

These portray different forms of spiritual illness. The surah’s ending develops two dangers that can lead to replacement: learning without acting, and abandoning the place of learning altogether. The way to safety reverses both: sit and listen, then strive to apply what you hear.

## 62:6

> Say, “O you who are Jews, if you claim that you alone are Allah’s allies apart from all other people, then wish for death, if you are truthful.

After portraying those who possess a Book without properly bearing its responsibility, the surah tests their claim: ‘O you who are Jews, if you claim that you alone are Allah’s allies apart from all other people, then wish for death, if you are truthful’. It does not teach people to seek death for its own sake. It exposes the contradiction between claiming a uniquely elevated standing with Allah and a heart that wants only to cling to this world. 

Someone truly close to Allah does not regard meeting Him as the worst thing that could happen. They know that worldly life is a passage and the Hereafter the place of recompense. This connects directly with the description in Al-Baqarah: ‘And you will surely find them the most greedy people for life’. Knowledge and religious affiliation had not kept a material outlook from taking hold of the heart. Among the reflections mentioned in interpreting the opening of An-Naziat is a connection between intense attachment to worldly life and difficulty parting from it. 

This is a reflection, not a basis for judging any particular person’s condition at death. Here it raises a fitting question: what has love of this world done to a person’s relationship with revelation? If wealth, position and longevity become dearer than Allah’s pleasure, a person will look for a way around His law whenever it conflicts with their interests. Closeness to Allah is therefore not tested by titles or the history of one’s group, but by knowledge’s effect on the heart. Has it brought the Hereafter nearer and loosened attachment to worldly life, or has knowledge itself become a covering for desire?

## 62:7

> But they will never wish for it because of what their hands have sent forth[3]. And Allah is All-Knowing of the wrongdoers.

‘But they will never wish for it because of what their hands have sent forth. And Allah is All-Knowing of the wrongdoers’ brings the claim back to the record of deeds. A person may give themselves grand titles, yet inwardly know what they have done. They may dread the reckoning because they know the distance between their words and actions. This directly connects with verse 5: the problem was not that the Book had failed to reach them, but that what reached them failed to become action, until knowledge became a burden rather than a means of salvation. 

The phrase ‘because of what their hands have sent forth’ makes responsibility personal: neither ancestry, group membership nor history can conceal it. ‘Allah is All-Knowing of the wrongdoers’ removes any remaining hope of hiding behind a public image, because Allah knows the concealed as well as the visible. A discerning person therefore asks not only how much they know, but what they have done with that knowledge. Someone may know a great deal about what is lawful and unlawful, yet fear of worldly loss may outweigh everything they have memorized. Knowledge remains on the tongue without deeds to bear witness to it.

## 62:8

> Say, “The death from which you run away will surely overcome you, then you will be brought back to the Knower of the unseen and the seen, and He will inform you of what you used to do.”

‘The death from which you run away will surely overcome you’ overturns the very image of escape. A person moves away from death with all their strength, yet the road ends in a meeting they cannot avoid. Material progress can treat many illnesses, raise average life expectancy and improve people’s lives, but it cannot abolish death. Nor does humanity possess the secret of the soul, of which Allah says: ‘The soul is only known to my Lord’. The created cannot rival the Creator in originating creation: ‘can never create even a fly, even if they all come together for that’. 

The danger lies neither in seeking treatment nor in developing the world. It lies in turning civilization into an attempt to flee the end, making a long life an independent goal even at the expense of religion. A Prophetic report illuminates the condition of a community numerous in people but weakened by a disease of the heart: ‘The people will soon summon one another to attack you’. Asked about this weakness, the Prophet replied: ‘Love of the world and dislike of death’. 

This helps explain how someone knowledgeable may recognize the truth yet neglect to apply it. Faced with a choice between avoiding a worldly loss and obeying Allah, the world grows so great in their heart that they reinterpret what they know. Then comes ‘then you will be brought back to the Knower of the unseen and the seen’. Both the heart’s claims and outward conduct are known to Allah; maintaining one image before people and another inwardly cannot succeed. 

The ending, ‘He will inform you of what you used to do’, is particularly fitting. [The Arabic تَعْمَلُونَ means what you do, rather than تَعْلَمُونَ, what you know.] The people in the comparison possessed scripture and knowledge, but the reckoning concerns what that knowledge became in action. The surah returns to the bond between faith and deeds: knowledge that leaves its bearer under the domination of worldly life still needs purification.

## 62:9

> O you who believe, when the call for prayer is made on Friday, then hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trading. That is better for you, if only you knew.

After warning about a community entrusted with a Book who did not act upon it, the surah addresses the new community: ‘O you who believe, when the call for prayer is made on Friday, then hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trading’. The connection is profound. A community that does not want to possess scripture while failing to bear its responsibility needs an appointed time to return to revelation, hear about Allah, the Hereafter and what He asks of them in their circumstances, and meet fellow believers. 

They then go back into worldly life with a renewed standard of judgment. This explains the warning against neglecting Friday prayer: ‘Whoever abandons three Fridays out of negligence, Allah seals his heart’. Repeatedly missing this gathering through negligence means more than losing an hour; it means losing a regular renewal of remembrance and community. The instruction ‘hasten’ does not mean running on foot, contrary to the instruction to approach prayer calmly. 

[The Arabic فَاسْعَوْا conveys purposeful effort and earnestness here.] Just as people strive for their livelihood, they should strive to fulfill what Allah asks of them. Remembrance here has been interpreted as the sermon and prayer. The sermon teaches: people receive shared instruction and then pray together, so religion does not become an isolated individual activity. The verse specifically says ‘leave off trading’. [The Arabic الْبَيْع names selling.] This is the moment the merchant awaits to receive the fruit of their effort. 

If those few seconds conflict with Allah’s call, the call takes precedence. The Sunnah also describes Allah’s guidance of this community to Friday: ‘We (Muslims) are the last (to come) but (will be) the foremost on the Day of Resurrection’. The narration goes on to explain that the earlier communities differed about the day prescribed for them, while Allah guided this community to it. The Friday gathering is itself a gift of guidance. The context also shows that Islam does not require an entire day withdrawn from worldly affairs. 

It protects a time for worship within the working day and then returns people to the marketplace. Bringing prayer and remembrance into employment, study and commerce is part of this way of life: religion is not confined to the mosque, and worldly affairs are not detached from Allah’s rule. Friday therefore provides a basic gathering that society should not lose. People see one another, hear about their Lord and the Hereafter, and go out on a shared foundation. 

Further circles of learning and purification strengthen them. But abandoning even this gathering, then wondering why hearts have hardened and worldly life dominates, resembles a community that has closed its Book while keeping only its name.

## 62:10

> When the prayer is over, disperse in the land and seek from the bounty of Allah, and remember Allah much so that you may be successful.

‘When the prayer is over, disperse in the land and seek from the bounty of Allah’ prevents Friday from being understood as a call to abandon worldly life. After gathering, people return to work, trade and seeking provision; Allah even calls it ‘the bounty of Allah’. The difference is that they now leave a mosque, remembrance and prayer, and should not return to the market with their judgment untouched. Immediately comes ‘remember Allah much’: do not forget what you heard when your dealings begin; remember Allah’s ruling when a transaction is offered, His favor when profit arrives, and the Hereafter when advantage tempts you to cheat. 

This brings out a contrast between dispersing here and breaking away in the next verse. [In this reflection on the context, انْتِشَار is an orderly dispersal after a sound gathering, while انْفِضَاض describes people breaking away toward an immediate attraction.] Each person returns to their work carrying a shared purpose and source of guidance, rather than everyone being pulled at random by whatever catches their attention. Gathering for remembrance and prayer thus allows people to disperse to different tasks without becoming divided in what guides them. 

This is reinforced by An-Nur: ‘men who are not distracted by buying or selling from the remembrance of Allah, or from performing prayer’. It does not describe people who never trade, but people whose trade does not rob them of remembrance. The same day contains worship and earning; the same heart can attend to worldly affairs without making the world its master. The fruit of the sermon becomes clear here. Someone who leaves and resumes dealings that contradict what they heard has recreated the separation against which the surah warns: they sat to learn but did not carry what they learned into practice. Someone who remembers Allah often amid work and trade has carried the mosque into everyday life through its guidance, not its physical location.

## 62:11

> When they see some merchandise or amusement, they rush towards it and leave you standing. Say, “That which is with Allah is better than amusement and merchandise, and Allah is the Best of Providers.”

The surah closes with an incident that reveals how demanding it is to remain faithful to this pattern: ‘When they see some merchandise or amusement, they rush towards it and leave you standing’. Authentic narrations establish that a caravan arrived while the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) was delivering the sermon, and people left until only twelve men remained with him. Some reports mention need and high prices, and some scholars have discussed how the ordering of the sermon and prayer at that stage might help explain the circumstances. 

The Quran nevertheless did not let the episode pass as an insignificant event. It was forming the generation that would embody the religion in practice, correcting desires, words and conduct. The phrase ‘they rush towards it’ matters: it reverses the gathering brought about by Friday and the orderly dispersal of the previous verse. Each person followed an immediate attraction. Most striking is ‘leave you standing’. The Prophet remained standing; he did not abandon his place because others left. 

If the person calling others to Allah also departs, to whom can they return? A group willing to stay and sacrifice preserves a way back for society. People must not all disperse whenever prices rise, marriage becomes difficult, jobs grow scarce or trade beckons. Economic hardship is a real trial, and the next surah exposes financial pressure deliberately used as a weapon: ‘Do not spend on those who are with Allah’s Messenger so that they may desert him’. 

Some understand that making worldly life difficult is a powerful way to draw people away from religion. The remedy is not to deny hardship, but for some to remain steadfast, carrying knowledge, teaching the Quran, answering doubts and serving people. If everyone abandons their responsibility because of pressure, who remains? Reports in the works of Quranic interpretation mention a severe warning about what would have happened if everyone had left. 

The authentic narration establishes that twelve remained, which itself reveals the value of those who stayed. Then comes a direct answer: ‘That which is with Allah is better than amusement and merchandise, and Allah is the Best of Providers’. This conviction steadies those who remain: what is with Allah is better, and provision rests with Him, not with the caravan alone. The surah’s pattern is now complete. It begins with a Messenger who recites, purifies and teaches; its middle portrays people who learned without acting; its end portrays people who left the place of learning for a worldly attraction. 

Following the Companions requires reversing these failures: sitting to learn, then striving against the self to act; hearing, then obeying. Repeating Al-Jumuah at the Friday gathering continually trains the community not to carry its Book without fulfilling its responsibility, and not to abandon remembrance whenever trade or amusement passes by.
