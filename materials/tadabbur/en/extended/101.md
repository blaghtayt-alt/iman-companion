## Introduction

Allah did not create people without purpose or leave them without responsibility. He made the purpose of their existence clear: “I have not created the jinn and mankind except to worship Me” (51:56). For this purpose He sent messengers and revealed scriptures, and He honored this community with Muhammad ﷺ and the Qur’an, which stands as a guardian over the scriptures before it. Salvation does not lie in becoming absorbed in life while forgetting its end. It lies in holding firmly to Allah’s Book and the Sunnah of His Prophet, and knowing the path that human beings were created to follow.

One of the Qur’an’s purposes is to warn the heedless and awaken them before it is too late. It was revealed to the heart of the Prophet ﷺ as a warning: “to your heart [O Prophet] so that you may be one of the warners” (26:194). Allah also says: “This is a Book that has been sent down to you [O Prophet], so do not let your heart be troubled by it; so that you may warn thereby and as a reminder to the believers” (7:2). Al-Qari‘ah is part of this warning, confronting indifference with the force of truth. Its subject is not remote from the listener’s life: it is the day when their deeds will be weighed and their destiny determined. It therefore opens by interrupting the distractions of the ear and heart, and closes by calling the listener to examine what they have done.

We may memorize these surahs and repeat them until their words become familiar, yet lose sight of what their meanings did to those who first received them. Explaining how the Qur’an was revealed, ‘A’ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, explained that the first revelation was “a Sura from Al-Mufassal, and in it was mentioned Paradise and the Fire” (Al-Bukhari, 4993). She also explained that rulings on what is lawful and unlawful were revealed when people had turned to Islam. Remembrance of the Hereafter changes what a person desires and fears, preparing the heart to receive commands and prohibitions. News of Paradise and Hell is not an optional addition to putting life right; it helps reorder life itself.

Reflect on the succession of these surahs in the Mushaf: in Az-Zalzalah the earth shakes and brings out its burdens; in Al-‘Adiyat comes the sound of charging horses; then Al-Qari‘ah arrives with its resounding impact and its scales. After it, the listener encounters “Competition for worldly gains distracts you [from Allah]” (102:1), followed by “By the time” and “man is in utter loss” (103:1–2), and then the warning in Al-Humazah to the person who gathers and counts wealth, imagining that it will make them immortal. It is as though these surahs expose what distracted the person whom the earthquake did not awaken and who paid no heed to Al-‘Adiyat and Al-Qari‘ah: competition for more, a lifetime passing, and wealth that cannot grant immortality.

These meanings address a soul that knows fear and hope, yet can become so absorbed in worldly adornments that it forgets its end. The One who revealed the Qur’an created that soul and knows what will put it right: “Does He not know His Own creation[4], when He is the Most Subtle, the All-Aware?” (67:14). Read the surah as though hearing it for the first time—not merely to learn a name for the Day of Resurrection, but to discover what deserves greater weight in your life before your deeds are weighed.

## 101:1

> The Striking Calamity!

“The Striking Calamity!” Before the scene is described, there is a single Arabic word: [al-qāri‘ah, الْقَارِعَةُ, a word that strikes the ear and demands attention]. The Arabic root conveys one object striking another and producing a sound; it is also used for a severe calamity. It can describe rebuking or admonishing someone with forceful words. This warning does not pass through the ear like an ordinary piece of news. It awakens the listener to ask what lies behind it.

Imagine hearing this word for the first time in Makkah, among people who deny resurrection, find it inconceivable that bodies could return after decaying, and are preoccupied with the wealth and status they fear losing. Imagine this warning in a society absorbed in its markets and interests, where usury, drinking intoxicants and burying infant girls alive occur. The Prophet ﷺ stands to convey his Lord’s words, and they hear: “The Striking Calamity!” What are they being warned about—a war, a calamity, an approaching punishment? The word is so forceful that even someone turning away may look up, wanting to know what news has been announced with such power.

The meaning of warning is brought closer by the Prophet’s parable in which a man tells his people: “I am the naked warner” (Al-Bukhari, 7283) [The naked warner is an old Arabic expression for someone raising an urgent alarm about approaching danger]. It is a warning of danger that cannot be met with heedlessness. Likewise, the Qur’an’s warning does not depend on the listener agreeing beforehand. The Prophet ﷺ conveys what he has received; his people’s denial does not make him stop reminding them of what they will face.

The surah’s opening with this single word led commentators to consider what is implied alongside it: a warning to beware of the Striking Calamity, a statement that it is coming, or an opening whose meaning is explained by what follows. In every reading, its impact remains: a word delivered to the ear that makes the listener want to hear the rest, rather than a long introduction that softens its force. The listener cannot avoid asking: “What is the Striking Calamity?”

A person may receive one piece of news after another about their health, child, money or car, until a single report eclipses all the rest. They put everything aside and attend to it because their life and future are at stake. The surah directs that attention toward the Hereafter: what news deserves your concern more than the day that will reveal the weight of what you lived for? Do not let the flood of immediate news conceal the news whose consequences you cannot escape.

## 101:2

> What is the Striking Calamity?

“What is the Striking Calamity?” The question magnifies the event. The scene is still hidden, and the same name returns with greater impact. The word is not mentioned once and left behind: it is repeated in a question that opens the mind to the magnitude of what it will hear. Familiarity with a name does not mean that a person understands its reality, nor will every calamity they use for comparison provide a measure of this day.

We rank our fears by what seems close to us: an illness, a loss, or news that changes the household’s circumstances. This question makes us reconsider the whole ranking. What do you fear most? What event, if you were certain it was approaching, would change your choices? When you hear “What is the Striking Calamity?”, let the question concern more than the meaning of a word: consider how present this day is in your heart.

## 101:3

> How do you know what the Striking Calamity is?

“How do you know what the Striking Calamity is?” The question heightens the awe. However vividly the terrors of that day are brought near, your imagination cannot encompass their reality. You have not seen this unseen world with your own eyes; Allah has made it known through revelation. The limits of imagination are not grounds for doubt. Receive Allah’s news with certainty, until its meanings are present in your heart as though you were witnessing them.

The place of such certainty becomes clearer when news is expressed in the language of seeing: “Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the people of the Elephant[1]?” (105:1). A believer does not approach Qur’anic news as a story whose truth is doubtful, but trusts the One who tells it and brings what He describes to mind. When reminding others of the Hereafter, the believer’s words should carry this confidence, not the coldness of someone relating something that neither concerns them nor commands their belief.

Imagine seeing a great fire burning homes and possessions, then hurrying to warn people whom it has not yet reached. They tell you they do not believe you and that there is no fire. Would you abandon the warning and discuss something else because they rejected the news? Your knowledge of the danger and concern for them would drive you to keep warning them. In the same way, certainty in the Qur’an moves a person to continue reminding others—not because their acceptance is guaranteed, but because the outcome being warned of is real.

The surah chiefly presents the realities themselves: the day when people and mountains will be transformed, then those whose scales are heavy and those whose scales are light. It does not begin by listing what its audience has done or directly rebuking their distractions, as in “Competition for worldly gains distracts you [from Allah]” (102:1). Instead, it sets before them what will happen whether they accept it or deny it. Do not remove resurrection from your invitation because someone rejects it. Present what you believe with clarity and certainty.

Before revelation, the Prophet ﷺ had lived among his people for many years; they knew his speech and conduct. Then he brought them this Qur’an. Allah says: “I have spent a lifetime[20] among you before this. Do you not then understand?” (10:16). Where did this news, this certainty and this heart-stirring power come from? Knowing the source of the words is part of receiving them: they are revelation, not human speculation about an unknown future.

## 101:4

> On that Day people will be like scattered moths[1],

The explanation begins with “On that Day people will be like scattered moths[1]”. The Striking Calamity is a day that is coming, whether or not those who deny it acknowledge it. After the impact of the name and the questions comes the visible scene. People whom you know as rational, capable and dignified become, in their confusion and dispersal, like moths scattered in different directions. The image combines lightness, weakness and bewilderment. In that situation, their familiar composure and security are gone.

Imagine a scene you cannot yet see clearly. You hear frightened cries and shouting, and wait to discover what is happening. Then the sight unfolds: confused movement in every direction. This is the sequence in which the surah reaches you—first the impact of Al-Qari‘ah, then the sight of people in that state. The preceding surah similarly moves from sound to sight: “By the galloping, panting horses[1]” followed by “striking sparks of fire [with their hooves][2]” (100:1–2). You hear the horses panting, then see the sparks struck by their hooves. The words lead the reader through the scene rather than merely stating its outcome.

[Al-mabthūth, الْمَبْثُوثِ, evokes dispersal and scattering.] These people are not a group proceeding calmly toward a destination they have chosen. Imagine an earthquake in which houses collapse and fires break out: people abandon their occupations and rush in different directions. If an event in this world can do that, what of the day when human turmoil accompanies the transformation of the mountains themselves? The ayah describes more than a great number of people: it describes human weakness before a terror beyond anything familiar.

Moths offer another heart-awakening image: they rush toward fire, drawn by what they see, and fall into what destroys them. The Prophet ﷺ compared his relationship with people to a man who lights a fire and tries to keep the insects from falling into it. He said: “I am holding you back from the fire, but you are slipping from my hand” (Muslim, 2285). He seeks to save them from destruction, while they slip away by pursuing its causes. This helps us understand that a forceful warning need not be harshness toward the listener; it can arise from concern for their salvation.

Examine your movements in this life before that scene arrives. Do you pursue every desire simply because it has been made attractive to you, and every doubtful idea simply because it impresses you? A person may be constantly active yet unable to distinguish what benefits them from what destroys them. They need revelation to give them insight and steadiness, so that they do not live like a moth enticed by every light.

## 101:5

> and the mountains will be like carded wool[2].

“and the mountains will be like carded wool[2]”. Attention turns to something heavier than a human being and apparently more stable. [Al-‘ihn, الْعِهْنِ, means colored wool; al-manfūsh, الْمَنْفُوشِ, describes fibers loosened and separated until they become light.] These rocky mountains, which seem firmly rooted and serve as examples of strength, become like flying wool. Their mass and imposing appearance no longer remain as you knew them.

The surah’s scene expands before you. It began with a sound; then people appeared moving like moths; now the colors of loosened wool fill the scene. If you turn toward people, you find no reassurance; if you look toward the mountains, you find none of their familiar stability. What, then, remains firm? To what can the heart attach itself when the heaviest thing it sees becomes light and scattered?

The listener is now prepared for the language of weight in the next ayah. Reliance on what seemed a material fortress has fallen away, and another weight will be discussed: the weight of the scales. What will benefit you then is neither the amount you owned nor the solidity of your shelter, but the value of what you offered to Allah. One fruit of this scene is a change in your priorities before Resurrection: do not spend your whole life magnifying what will scatter away while neglecting what will be weighed.

## 101:6

> Then the one whose scales of good deeds are heavy,

“Then the one whose scales of good deeds are heavy”. This introduces what the heart has been seeking amid all that lightness. When people become like moths and mountains like wool, deeds still have weight, and salvation is not determined by the outward appearance familiar in this world. One connection with Allah’s words “and they will be safe from the horror of that Day” (27:89) is that terror will not be everyone’s final state. Some will attain security, while those whose scales are light will find nothing to save them.

[Mawāzīn, مَوَازِينُ, is plural.] It brings to mind the many things a person has done and the variety of their words, deeds, stances and intentions. Life was not an empty stretch without consequences: there is a weighing that reveals the truth of what a person regarded as insignificant or great. Allah says: “We will place the scales of justice on the Day of Resurrection, and no soul will be wronged in the least. Even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed[18], We will bring it forth. Sufficient are We as Reckoners” (21:47). Nothing, however small, is lost in this reckoning, and its judgment involves neither injustice nor inattention.

A man may hold office, exercise power and command a hearing in this world, only to find that such prestige does not benefit him at the reckoning. The Prophet ﷺ said: “A huge fat man will come on the Day of Judgment weighing less than a mosquito wing in the sight of Allah” (Al-Bukhari, 4729). The standards by which people habitually weigh one another are overturned. Someone honored among them has no weight before Allah, while someone they overlook may attain a high rank in the Hereafter. This recalls the description of Resurrection: “it will bring some low and raise others up[1]” (56:3). Worldly prominence is not the final judgment.

The opposite is illustrated by ‘Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud, may Allah be pleased with him. He was gathering a tooth-stick from an arak tree when some people laughed at his thin legs. The report conveys that the Prophet ﷺ swore by the One in whose hand is his soul that those two legs would weigh more in the Scale than Mount Uhud (Ahmad, 3991; authentic through supporting narrations). Compare those thin legs with the size of the mountain, and recognize that the issue is not the weight visible to the eye. Those legs carried him in obedience to Allah: reciting the Qur’an openly, emigrating, striving in jihad, seeking knowledge and teaching it. Do not reduce a person’s worth to bodily appearance or to the status, wealth and outward display that people admire.

The difference between these standards is also evident in prayer. A man who had slept until morning without getting up for prayer was mentioned to the Prophet ﷺ. He said: “That is a man in whose ears—or, he said, in whose ear—the devil urinated” (Al-Bukhari, 1144). In a dream, the Prophet ﷺ also saw a man whose head was being struck with a rock; it was explained that this represented someone who receives the Qur’an but rejects it and sleeps through the obligatory prayer (Al-Bukhari, 7047). People may admire someone’s intelligence and cleverness while that person neglects the duty of worshiping the One for whom they were created. Human admiration cannot cancel the consequences of turning away from Allah.

The warning against staying away from congregational prayer was so severe that the Prophet ﷺ said he had considered appointing a man to lead the people in prayer, then going to those who stayed away and burning their houses down upon them (Al-Bukhari, 644). Neither a house’s beauty nor its owner’s position measures honor before Allah. Houses, intelligence and offices may impress people, while their owners fit the description “They only know the apparent life of this world, but they are heedless of the Hereafter” (30:7). The lesson is to recognize the worth of obedience and not allow success in some worldly matter to conceal a failure in your duty to your Lord.

The question therefore moves from comparison with others to self-examination: what are my words, decisions and inward deeds worth before Allah? What have I done with what I know of the Qur’an? Receiving the surah with an attentive heart brings the scene of weighing close to your daily choices. Allah says: “Indeed, in that is a reminder for anyone who has a [mindful] heart or lends an attentive ear” (50:37). Listen as though witnessing what you recite, then turn to a deed you can put right today, before it appears on the scales.

## 101:7

> will have a pleasant life[3].

Those whose scales are heavy will receive the outcome described by “will have a pleasant life[3]”: a life that satisfies them and brings their souls peace. They are pleased with Allah and have attained His pleasure. After the resounding calamity, terror and dispersal comes a life of security and contentment. The reader is not left confined to the terrifying scene, but sees that faith and deeds lead to an outcome that delights their doer. This is the life worth working for, rather than a passing satisfaction granted by one approving glance and taken away by another.

One connection between this reward and a person’s worldly life is that steadiness on the day of terror befits a heart that was not easily swept away by every trial. When desires presented themselves, it did not rush toward them without reflection; when doubts were raised, it did not abandon known truth at the first new voice. It possessed something that held it steady: faith and revelation. Allah says: “We will send down upon you a heavy Word[2]” (73:5). The weight of faith is not bodily size, but a heart that carries this Word and acts upon it.

Do not make yourself the daily follower of whatever glitters before you, or imagine that steadfastness begins only when the Striking Calamity occurs. You learn to hold firmly to revelation now, discovering through it what to pursue and what to leave. Then the pursuit of a pleasant life becomes a path you follow, rather than a wish disconnected from your actions.

## 101:8

> But the one whose scales of good deeds are light,

“But the one whose scales of good deeds are light”. The other outcome is now opened before us. A person may suffer a worldly hardship for which someone can help: an illness for which treatment can be sought, even if travel is needed, or a debt someone might pay on their behalf. But to reach the reckoning and find one’s scales light is a loss that cannot be repaired by returning to perform new deeds. The time for striving has ended, and its results have arrived.

Imagine someone whose deeds are presented one after another, waiting for something to make the scales heavy. The deeds are nearly finished, yet nothing has appeared to save them. Then they run out, and the person faces the consequences of light scales. What they kept postponing in this world is now what they need most; their preoccupation with other people’s affairs has not added the weight they imagined. Here you understand why news of Al-Qari‘ah deserved more attention than so many reports that consumed a lifetime.

As long as you hear this ayah before that day, time for repentance and action remains. Do not dismiss a good deed because people consider it unimportant, or put off returning to Allah because you can postpone it for another hour. Light scales make the question urgent: what will I offer now, rather than what will I wish I had offered once the time for offering is over?

## 101:9

> his abode will be the abyss[4].

“his abode will be the abyss[4]”. The abode of the person whose scales are light is described in a way that shakes the heart. [One interpretation of ummuhu, أُمُّهُ, literally ‘his mother’, is his refuge and place of return, which embraces him and takes charge of him.] Just as a child returns to a mother who feeds, gives drink and clothes, this person comes to the abyss—but everything encountered there is punishment. Neither food, drink nor clothing brings relief; every aspect of existence lies within this terrifying destiny.

[Hāwiyah, هَاوِيَةٌ, evokes the depth of the Fire and falling into it.] This refuge is not a place of mercy to escape pain: it is itself a place of punishment. Allah’s words convey how inescapable that outcome is: “Hellfire is sufficient for them. What a terrible resting place!” (2:206). Whoever seeks a worldly refuge that preserves their comfort, even at the expense of truth, should ask where their deeds are taking them.

Another interpretation understands the expression as referring to the crown of the head: the person falls headfirst into the Fire, the head entering the abyss first. The image becomes more forceful when you recall a head held high in arrogance and rejection, its owner deceived by an attractive desire or doubt. What seemed an elevation in that person’s own mind is overturned: the direction pursued leads not upward, but downward.

A further interpretation finds an expression of lament in the wording. [Arabic expressions such as ثَكِلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ, a mother bereaved of her child, or وَيْلَ أُمِّهِ, woe to his mother, convey how grievous someone’s calamity is and how deeply it arouses sorrow.] Yet other people’s sorrow cannot make that person’s scales heavier or change their outcome. Whatever interpretation of the wording is considered, the ayah does not leave light deeds as an abstraction: an abode and a destiny follow.

## 101:10

> And how do you know what it is?

“And how do you know what it is?” The question renews the sense of awe before revealing the abyss’s reality. Just as the Striking Calamity exceeds your imagination, so does the destination into which the person with light scales falls. Do not let hearing the word ‘Fire’ so often make you pass over it as though you had fully understood it. A familiar name does not make its reality familiar.

This pause comes after the abode has been described and before it is named at the surah’s end, keeping the heart alert. What abyss is this? What has been prepared for someone whose deeds are finished and whose scales are light? The answer then comes in two Arabic words, whose impact does not leave a heart that has received the question sincerely.

## 101:11

> It is a Blazing Fire.

“It is a Blazing Fire”. Every fire known to human beings is hot; what, then, of the Fire singled out here as [ḥāmiyah, حَامِيَةٌ, intensely hot]? It is unlike the worldly fire whose name is familiar and whose fierceness we know in part. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Your fire is a one-seventieth part of Hellfire” (Al-Bukhari, 3265). When the listeners remarked that worldly fire would have been enough, he explained that Hellfire exceeds it by sixty-nine parts, each equal in heat to worldly fire. Knowledge of worldly fire therefore heightens fear of this news without encompassing the reality of the Fire of the Hereafter.

Consider the heat of furnaces that melt iron and the human inability to withstand fire, then hear this warning. The aim is not to confine the reality of the Hereafter to earthly experience: sharing a name does not mean sharing the same reality. Ibn ‘Abbas, may Allah be pleased with them both, said of Paradise: “There is nothing in Paradise of the things we have in the world but names only.” (Al-Bayhaqi, Resurrection and Gathering, 332; a report attributed to the Companion, not to the Prophet). Just as the bliss of the Hereafter exceeds the pleasures you have known, do not measure its punishment by familiar accounts of worldly suffering.

The description in Al-Humazah adds to this meaning: “It is the kindled Fire of Allah” (104:6). Do not picture the Blazing Fire as a fire dying down, reassuring its sufferer that it will go out. Bring to mind the severity of the punishment being warned of. The surah began with a striking sound that awakens the heart; then the movement of people and mountains became visible, and the scales appeared, until the person with light scales reached this terrifying answer.

When the surah ends, return to your deeds. What deserves to be increased, and what should you stop? The fruit of reading is not to finish describing the scene and then return unchanged, but to keep the weight of deeds before Allah present when you speak, choose, proceed or refrain. Security and a satisfying life, or the abyss and the Blazing Fire, are not two reports disconnected from what you do today.

Read the surah to your family and children, and remind those around you through it. Allah says: “But remind with the Qur’an those who fear My warning” (50:45). Convey its realities as they have come, addressing people with the certainty of someone concerned for them, not the gloating of someone awaiting their destruction. The mercy of the Prophet ﷺ included warning people about their destiny in the Hereafter and awakening them from the heedlessness in which worldly distractions competed for their attention. A listener may respond or may turn away. Do not abandon what can save them simply because their response is not yours to control. Begin with yourself, and carry to others the Qur’an that awakened your heart.
