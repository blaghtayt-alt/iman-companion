Reflecting on Surah Saba

A Makkan surah • Fifty-four verses

In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.

Verse 1

All praise be to Allah, to Whom belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is on earth, and to Him is the praise in the Hereafter; He is the All-Wise, All-Aware.

Praise of Allah opens the way into this surah. Before it presents the power, wealth and plotting of those corrupted by luxury, it brings the heart back to the truth about ownership: “to Whom belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is on earth.” Whatever people possess has not been wrested from their Lord’s authority. He created it, owns it and has the right to dispose of it.

This gives believers a clear perspective when they find themselves weak. The superior wealth and resources of those who serve falsehood may drive some people to despair or withdrawal. It may tempt them to compromise and change a sound course, or so unsettle the oppressed that they imagine the stronger side must be right. The surah steadies believers who are working for their faith, awakens those dazzled by power, and reminds the arrogant that the very thing they boast of is a gift that can be taken away.

This is part of what the Quran does in life. It was revealed to hearts facing difficulties within themselves, their homes and society, and in the struggle against falsehood. They needed more than explanations of unfamiliar words. Speaking of its gradual revelation, Allah says: “so that We may strengthen your heart thereby” (25:32). If revelation strengthened the Prophet’s ﷺ heart, those who follow him have still greater need of its light. Allah therefore describes what a newly revealed surah does for believers: “As for those who believe, it does increase them in faith and they rejoice.” (9:124). Understanding the explanation opens up the meaning; the life, light and guidance of revelation should then appear in conviction, action and the way we judge things.

Five surahs open with praise: al-Fatihah, al-An‘am, al-Kahf, Saba and Fatir. The description following that praise sheds light on an aspect of each surah’s purpose. In al-Fatihah we read, “All praise be to Allah[1], the Lord[2] of the worlds[3]” (1:2), followed by the request for guidance to the straight path. Deliverance from anger and misguidance comes through the Lord’s favor and guidance. Al-An‘am begins, “All praise be to Allah Who created the heavens and earth, and made darkness and light” (6:1). The servant is reminded of the blessing of clear guidance and the distinction between the right path and error; the surah exposes forms of idolatrous folly that extend even to killing children. Al-Kahf opens, “All praise be to Allah Who has sent down upon His slave the Book, and has not allowed any crookedness therein[1]” (18:1). Amid the trials of authority, wealth and knowledge, believers praise their Lord for the Book that guides them. Here in Saba, faced with the many resources that make the followers of falsehood proud, they praise Allah because all dominion belongs to Him.

Praise acknowledges His perfect attributes and His inherent right to be praised. It does not depend on our understanding the benefit in everything that happens to us. Gratitude, by contrast, responds to goodness and blessings received. We praise Him when food is available and when it is lacking, during a financial crisis and after it ends, before gaining power and after gaining it. Limited understanding or an overwhelming love of immediate things may hide the wisdom from us; that does not diminish His right to praise. A report from ‘Aishah describes the Prophet ﷺ praising his Lord when he saw something he liked and when he saw something he disliked: in the first case, “Praise is to Allah by Whose grace good deeds are completed”; in the second, “Praise is to Allah in all circumstances” (Ibn Majah, 3803).

Mentioning the heavens first draws our attention beyond the narrow sphere of earthly power to the vastness of Allah’s dominion. Whatever people possess, however formidable their machinery, is small beside the heavenly bodies and far horizons they cannot encompass. Beyond what the eye sees are the unseen world, the angels and the Hereafter. The heart must not confine its understanding of reality to the small portion visible to it.

Then come the words, “and to Him is the praise in the Hereafter.” There, truths about wisdom and recompense that were hidden in this life become clear, and His right to praise becomes unmistakable. Something similar is conveyed by “Master[5] of the Day of Judgment.” (1:4). Allah owns every day, but on the Day of Judgment His dominion is manifest, and no one can claim authority alongside Him. In this world, a servant may be puzzled by a believer’s suffering or the respite given to an oppressor. When the outcomes are revealed, it becomes clear that what looked like humiliation was elevation, and that some respite opened a door to repentance while other respite allowed sin to increase. Allah says, “We only give them respite so that they may increase in sin” (3:178). The disclosure of what was previously difficult to understand is brought closer by the words, “This is the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.” (18:82). One interpretation of “On the Day when He calls you, you will respond by praising Him” (17:52) is that, at resurrection, creation acknowledges its Lord’s greatness after the veils that concealed it have fallen away.

His abundant generosity and the justice of His recompense are also evident in good deeds being multiplied from ten to seven hundred times, while an evil deed is recorded only once; even refraining from an intended evil may earn a good deed. The report concludes, “Allah does not put to destruction anyone except he who is doomed to destruction” (Muslim, 131). At the time of intercession, the Prophet ﷺ prostrates before his Lord, and Allah opens to him forms of praise with which to praise Him (al-Bukhari, 4712). Praise accompanies the disclosure of truth and the beginning of judgment between creation.

The opening ends with “He is the All-Wise, All-Aware.” A person may own great wealth yet spend it wrongly through ignorance or foolishness. Allah’s dominion, however, is joined to perfect wisdom and all-encompassing knowledge. Wisdom is putting a thing in its proper place; deep awareness includes knowing its realities and consequences. A human doctor may study and still need the experience of practice. In the account of the Night Journey, Musa advised the Prophet ﷺ about the number of prayers because of his experience with the Children of Israel (al-Bukhari, 3207). Allah acquires no knowledge through experience and does not wait for something to happen before knowing its outcome. He knows what was, what will be, and how something that did not happen would have unfolded had it happened. The One who governs the trials and changing circumstances believers witness owns everything and is All-Wise, All-Aware. Their peace rests on knowing this, even when they cannot grasp every detail of His wisdom.

Verse 2

He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the heaven and whatever ascends to it, and He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving.

The all-encompassing dominion is followed by all-encompassing knowledge. The verse opens before the heart a universe alive with movement: what enters the earth and comes out of it, and what descends from heaven and rises to it. This is not limited to the familiar sight of rain and growing plants. Allah knows every particle, drop and hidden movement. He encompasses every provision, blessing or punishment that descends, and every supplication, glorification, expression of praise or deed that rises.

His knowledge includes all the good and evil recorded in people’s actions, down to a glance and the ill suspicion concealed by the person who casts it. Recording evil deeds does not mean that they rise to Him as accepted deeds. He says, “To Him ascend good words, and righteous deeds raise them[1].” (35:10). If people were asked to count everything entering, emerging, descending and ascending in a single moment, they could not do it. No part of Allah’s knowledge distracts Him from another.

The heart needs this wider view when events seem to close in. It may magnify an oppressor’s authority until it imagines the whole universe depends on whether that person lives or dies. Yet that person is a weak creature whom the smallest cause may disable. Strengthening the heart is not an optional addition to carrying the message. When Musa was sent to Pharaoh, he asked for his heart to be opened, his task to be eased and the impediment in his speech to be removed. He and Harun said, “Our Lord, we fear that he may hasten to punish us or transgress all bounds.” (20:45). The answer came: “Do not be afraid; I am with you both, hearing and seeing.” (20:46). They already knew that Allah hears and sees, but in that fearful moment they needed this particular reassurance. Revelation is therefore recited repeatedly, renewing the heart’s conviction in what it knows and steadying its steps.

People’s deeds and their provision are among the things that rise and descend. If complete recompense were immediate in this world, so that every sin brought its full punishment, the well-being people enjoy despite their shortcomings could not continue. Allah says, “If Allah were to take people to task for what they did, He would not leave a single living being on earth” (35:45). But this is a world of respite and testing; complete recompense belongs to another day. A hadith tells us that none is more patient than Allah in the face of hurtful words He hears: people ascribe a child to Him, yet He continues to provide for them and grant them health (al-Bukhari, 6099).

This helps explain the ending, “and He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving.” Although He knows everything they do, He does not cut off their provision at every sin. He shows mercy, gives respite and forgives. From another perspective, such care and generosity call for immense gratitude, yet people fall short, and He meets them with mercy and forgiveness. The contrast between those who act gratefully and those who turn away from blessing will become clear in the household of Dawud and the people of Saba. Continued well-being must not make us forget what we owe the Giver.

Verse 3

Those who disbelieve say, “The Hour will not come to us.” Say, “Yes, by my Lord, it will surely come to you.” [He is] the Knower of the unseen; not even an atom’s weight[1] is hidden from Him in the heavens or on earth, nor is there anything smaller or bigger than that, except that it is in a clear Record[2],

After this account of dominion, knowledge and wisdom, the deniers’ words are presented: “The Hour will not come to us.” Their objection here is directed toward the Hereafter rather than the details of what is in the heavens and earth. Accountability stands in the way of a person’s wish to do whatever they please and have every consequence end with their life. The Quran reveals this motive: “But man desires to persist in sin[2]” (75:5). The answer is therefore direct and emphatic: Say, “Yes, by my Lord, it will surely come to you.”

The oath by the Lord calls to mind His care and wisdom. The Lord who nurtures His servants through blessings and sends them guidance does not leave life without purpose, nor does the oppression someone suffered have the final word. The fire into which the people of the trench were thrown was not the end of their story: the believers who held firmly to faith have their reward in Paradise. The force of the answer matches the force of the denial. They denied that the Hour would come to them, and the reply addresses those very people: it will certainly come to you, reinforced in Arabic by an oath and an emphatic verb ending. Accountability is no longer a discussion about unknown people; you yourselves are concerned.

There is precision here in answering an objection: address the point at issue and leave none of its implications concealed. Throughout the surah, the command to say recurs in response to multiplying claims, so that relentless plotting by night and day is not left alone to shape people’s understanding. The Lord is then described as “the Knower of the unseen.” What they have not seen does not cease to exist, and He knows it. The Arabic expression for something not being hidden from Him means that it does not escape His knowledge. Not an atom’s weight in the heavens or earth, nor anything smaller or larger, is absent from the clear Record.

This detail answers the denial of resurrection in two connected ways. Knowledge that encompasses every part of creation, however scattered or hidden, shows that gathering those parts cannot defeat Him. Recording every deed, however small, removes the hope of escaping accountability. Luqman’s words to his son bring both points into view: “O my dear son, even if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed – whether in a rock or in the heavens or in the earth – Allah will bring it forth. Indeed, Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware.” (31:16). If the seed’s weight is understood as a concealed deed, the point is that Allah brings it forth for judgment. If attention is directed to a thing hidden within a rock or somewhere in the vast heavens and earth, His knowledge of its location and His power to bring it forth become apparent. No deed is lost, and gathering what has scattered is never beyond Him. The Hour they denied is coming through His knowledge, power and wisdom.

Verse 4

so that He may reward those who believe and do righteous deeds; it is they who will have forgiveness and a generous provision.

The verse explains one purpose of the Hour’s coming: faith and righteous action will receive their recompense. Allah’s wisdom does not permit those who patiently endure and give of themselves to be treated exactly like those who turn away, or those who pray at night and fast to be equated with those who do not obey. In everyday life, people recognize the difference between someone who works or studies diligently and someone who neglects both. How, then, could they imagine that a life of moral responsibility ends in an absolute leveling of those who act and those who refuse?

Yet believers do not approach their Lord proudly relying on their deeds. Forgiveness is mentioned before the generous provision. However devoted they have been, they have not adequately thanked Allah for His blessings and still need His pardon for their shortcomings. Then comes the generous provision. One explanation of its generosity is that it arrives without toil or hardship: the people of Paradise find what they desire without the going out, searching, waiting and finding the money that accompany the needs of this world. Another explanation is that it never ends; its delight is followed by no exhaustion of the supply. They receive both pardon for their shortcomings and gifts that honor them.

Verse 5

But those who strive against Our verses[3], seeking to discredit them, for such there will be a punishment of agonizing pain.

Set against the believers is a group who do more than turn away: they work to oppose the signs. Striving means expending effort and exhausting one’s capacity. Preventing people from Allah’s path should not be imagined as a passing amusement or an unplanned remark. A person may spend money and time, stay up at night and study how to corrupt the very source of people’s guidance. Entertainment scheduled during worship is one example: keeping viewers watching after a meal until they become sluggish about prayer, or diverting their attention at an hour they might have given to the Quran. A program’s effect cannot be separated from its timing and the portion of the viewer’s life it occupies. Allah says, “If you are suffering harm, they too are suffering harm just like you” (4:104). Those explaining the truth need to recognize that effort is also expended in the service of falsehood.

The signs are the object of that striving. One explanation is that these are the verses of revelation, as the connection with the next verse suggests: “whatever has been sent down to you from your Lord” (34:6). Another includes the signs Allah has placed throughout creation. The Arabic preposition here suggests becoming immersed in attacking them, rather than making a superficial objection. Cutting people off from the source of their faith has a deeper effect than contesting wealth that changes hands or power that passes away. This is related to the jinn’s acknowledgment: “and that the fool among us [i.e., Satan] used to say outrageous things about Allah[2]” (72:4). Corrupting what is said about Allah corrupts the foundation of the relationship with Him.

One explanation of the form معاجزين [seeking to discredit them] is that they seek to make the signs appear incapable of accomplishing what they guide toward. The alternate reading معجّزين, with a doubled consonant and no alif, has been explained as discouraging people and turning them away from approaching the signs (Ibn al-Jazari, al-Nashr, the section on Surah al-Hajj). Two ways of obstructing guidance thus meet: objections that distort revelation, and desires that keep people too occupied to reach it. These roles may be divided between people attacking the Quran, others attacking the Sunnah and those who transmit it, and others consuming people’s time with amusement.

Among the explanations of “just as We sent down [punishment] upon those who divided [their religion][48],” (15:90) and “those who split the Qur’an into different portions[49].” (15:91) is that they divided their accusations among descriptions of the Messenger as a poet, a madman or a soothsayer. Another explanation connects the word with swearing an oath. On the interpretation of divided accusations, their different claims converge on preventing people from reaching the Quran, however their wording varies. Refusing to listen and creating a disturbance come together in the words, “Do not listen to this Qur’an, but make noise [when it is recited], so that you may prevail.” (41:26). They did not merely demand that people turn away: they wanted to fill its place with noise so that it would never reach the heart. One explanation of “while heedlessly lost in amusement?” (53:61) is that they sang when the Quran was recited, replacing attentive listening to its reminder with absorption in entertainment.

The obstructers’ attachment to worldly things does not prevent them from spending those things to keep others from the truth. They may see the spread of faith as a threat to what they want to preserve for themselves, and surrender some of what they love in order to keep the rest. Allah says, “The disbelievers spend their wealth to prevent [people] from the way of Allah. They will continue to spend it, then it will be a source of regret for them” (8:36). This explains the warning of a punishment of agonizing severity; one explanation of the Arabic term for that punishment is its intensity. They sought to withhold guidance from an entire society and keep it captive to desires and deception. That kind of corruption goes beyond a person committing a sin whose effects are confined to themselves, and helps explain the severe warning following the description of their efforts.

Verse 6

Those who are blessed with knowledge can see that whatever has been sent down to you from your Lord is the truth and that it guides to the path of the All-Mighty, the Praiseworthy.

Despite everything spent on distorting revelation, the truth remains clear to those granted knowledge of it. The Quran does not portray believers as creating the clarity of its verses through their own effort. They believe and do righteous deeds, and the Quran’s influence appears in their faith, actions and conveying its message. Those who would deny its guidance, however, must work continually to conceal that clarity. The expression “can see” conveys clear perception and firmness amid deception. The very signs that opponents try to portray as helpless or confused are recognized as true by people of knowledge.

Not everyone who engages in argument sees clearly during trials. Beneficial knowledge here is knowledge connected to revelation and leading to recognition of Allah; neither a title nor a mass of information can replace that foundation. Those who see must explain what they know. When the oppressed acknowledge the effect of “conspiracy of night and day” (34:33), the need for continuous work to dispel confusion becomes clear. Minds must not be abandoned to propaganda.

The revelation is not merely described as true, but as “the truth.” The separating pronoun in Arabic adds emphasis and exclusiveness, fitting a setting in which claims multiply until people are meant to despair of knowing the truth and say that they trust no one and do not know whom to follow. People of knowledge do not dissolve Allah’s guidance among alternatives that contradict it. The deniers revealed how intensely they resisted this revelation’s unique claim to truth: And [remember] when they said, “O Allah, if this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the heaven or bring upon us a painful punishment[19].” (8:32). They did not ask for guidance if it was indeed the truth; they preferred punishment to submitting to it. They also demanded, “Bring us a different Qur’an, or make changes in it[19].” (10:15).

The further words “it guides” add a practical dimension. They see its truth and its guidance for life, in contrast to those who try to persuade people that the Shariah cannot put their affairs right, or that its rulings on dealings, wealth and society cannot be put into practice. The Quran guides and affects real life when people believe in it and act upon it; its truth is not detached from the fruits of following it. It leads along “the path of the All-Mighty, the Praiseworthy”: a straight path to the Lord who cannot be overcome and who deserves every praise. Might answers the accusation of powerlessness, and praiseworthiness answers the faults attributed to revelation. Among the meanings of the Praiseworthy is that He is praised in Himself and His actions, and that He commends the obedient deeds of His faithful servants. Following His guidance is a path to honor and praiseworthy action. The believer began this journey through the surah by praising the Lord for His dominion and wisdom.

Verse 7

The disbelievers [mockingly] say, “Shall we show you a man[4] who will tell you that when you are completely disintegrated into dust, you will be created anew?”

The deniers move from announcing their own rejection of the Hour to spreading that rejection among others. First they said, “The Hour will not come to us.” (34:3). Now they address other people: “Shall we show you a man[4]”. They do not present a question in search of evidence. Instead, they use curiosity and mockery to steer their listeners: here is a man with astonishing news, presented as though merely hearing it were enough to make one laugh. Referring to him indefinitely as a man belittles him, even though they know exactly whom they mean. The expression about his telling them news heightens the impression of a strange announcement.

They do not simply say, when you die. They say, “when you are completely disintegrated into dust,” evoking the fullest scattering of the body’s parts and setting it against a new creation to make their audience dismiss it as impossible. Someone mocking religion may do something similar by cutting out a sentence from a ruling or a report about the Hereafter and turning it into a joke, or setting an image of a disintegrated body beside one of the person alive. The emotional reaction gets ahead of consideration of the evidence. The Quran exposes the root of this dismissal: producing arguments[23] against Us while forgetting his own creation[24]. He says, “Who can give life to the bones after they have crumbled to dust?” (36:78). The person denying resurrection fixes his attention on decayed bones and forgets the first creation, to which the question ought to return.

Misleaders do not always announce their judgment at once. They may build a scene until listeners imagine they have reached a conclusion independently, when they have merely been led to it. In Musa’s story, those calling people to assemble said, “Will you join the gathering” (26:39), then stated the direction they wanted them to take: “so that we may follow the magicians, if they are victorious?” (26:40). The option they promoted was following the magicians if they won. They did not invite people to follow Musa if the truth became evident on his side. A similar narrowing of the options here prepares for the next verse’s restriction of the Messenger’s news to either lying or madness. Curiosity and mockery become the introduction to a verdict in which the audience is meant to feel it has taken part.

Verse 8

Has he fabricated a lie against Allah, or is he mad?”[5] No, but those who do not believe in the Hereafter will be in the punishment, and they are extremely misguided.

Having prepared their listeners through ridicule and the suggestion of impossibility, they restrict the matter to two alternatives: fabrication against Allah, or madness that makes the man say what no rational person would say. Truthfulness has been excluded from the question itself. The comparison cannot be fair when the conclusion they dislike is removed before any examination begins.

Their words, “Has he fabricated a lie against Allah,” feign zeal for Allah, making their attack appear to defend religion. Pharaoh said something similar about Musa: “For I fear that he may change your religion or spread corruption[5] in the land.” (40:26). Such speech may spread widely and attract admirers through its sensational appeal. A large following does not establish its truth. The Quran nevertheless brings attention back to the real disease: “No, but those who do not believe in the Hereafter will be in the punishment, and they are extremely misguided.” Their confusion does not arise merely from hearing surprising news, but from rejecting the abode in which they will be called to account.

One explanation is that the punishment is what they will face in the Hereafter, while the misguidance is what they live in now. Another includes punishment that reaches them in this world. Without belief in recompense, a person may witness an injustice and imagine that justice has come to an end: someone is assaulted or harmed through their family, and beyond the aggressor’s power there appears to be no reckoning or redress. That picture weighs heavily on the victim and lets the aggressor imagine escape. Belief in the Hereafter preserves the meaning of justice when its appearance is delayed and connects life with Allah’s wisdom. What people do, or what is done to them, is not a final ending beyond all reconsideration.

Verse 9

Do they not see all that is before them and all that is behind them of the heavens and earth? If We willed, We could cause the earth to swallow them up, or cause fragments of the sky to fall upon them. Indeed, there is a sign in this for every slave who turns to Allah in repentance.

This question brings the deniers back to the heavens and earth, both encompassed by Allah’s dominion. They live within them and cannot prevent Him from acting upon them. The One who owns the earth can make it swallow them, and the One who owns the sky can cause pieces of it to fall upon them. No place offers independent shelter from their Lord’s authority, and familiarity with their surroundings is no proof that those surroundings cannot change.

The warning connects with the surah’s opening. The stories that follow make power and dominion visible in particular examples: Allah softens iron for Dawud, subdues the wind and jinn for Sulayman, causes the molten metal to flow for him, and gives a people gardens and safe roads. Then, when they turn away, He removes what made them proud. The reader sees both giving and taking away; dominion is no longer an abstract idea. One effect on a believer suffering oppression is to make them look at the mountains, which obey Allah’s command, and recognize that the harm they face is not caused by any lack of power in their Lord. As He says, “if Allah had willed, He could have subjected them to punishment, but [He ordered you to fight] to test some of you by means of others” (47:4).

All of this prepares for the coming challenge, which the Messenger is commanded to declare: “Call upon those whom you claim [to be gods] besides Allah.” (34:22). Which of these acts have the supposed deities performed? Who can grant these blessings or restore them when they are taken away? The warning is thus connected to the stories’ evidence of divine power, and then to the exposure of the helplessness of what people call upon besides Allah.

Verse 10

Indeed, We bestowed favors upon David: “O mountains, echo Allah’s praise with him, and the birds too!” And We made iron malleable for him:

Allah’s favor to Dawud comes at a point where believers need to recall the abundance of their Lord’s giving. The wealth and resources amassed by those serving falsehood do not mean that they possess every source of strength, or that the treasuries of favor have been closed to believers. The One who softened iron for Dawud and subdued the wind for Sulayman can give to whomever He wills. Differences in what people receive do not reflect any limitation in Allah’s power, nor do they necessarily mean that someone given less has a lower standing with Him.

The problem cannot be reduced to a lack of money. If money alone could establish the message, merely having it would suffice even without people who carry the message out of faith. A report about ‘Umar illustrates this: in a gathering where some wished for gold and jewels to spend in Allah’s cause, he wished instead for men who would carry out His work (Ahmad, Fada’il al-Sahabah, 1280). Money and resources change hands. The fundamental strength opponents attack is revelation and what it nurtures in people.

Dawud’s great devotion accompanied Allah’s favor to him. A hadith recounts that Adam saw Dawud among his descendants, admired his light and gave him forty years of his own life (al-Tirmidhi, 3076). Other reports describe Dawud’s fasting and prayer as the fasting and prayer most beloved to Allah (al-Bukhari, 1131), and say that he ate from the work of his own hands (al-Bukhari, 2072). The recitation of his scripture, the Zabur, was made so easy for him that he finished it before his riding animals had been saddled (al-Bukhari, 3417). Kingship did not distract him from worshipping his Lord or earning his food through his own work.

One fruit of striving is that Allah makes an act of obedience easier after it was initially difficult. People who remain close to the Quran find a familiarity and ease in reciting and memorizing it that occasional readers do not. A person may become accustomed to night prayer and waking for Fajr after a period of effort and struggle. Then the scene of Dawud’s praise opens before us: “O mountains, echo Allah’s praise with him, and the birds too!” The mountains and birds repeat his glorification, joining the worshipper and the creatures around him in remembrance of Allah. This honor awakens the heart to the power of the One whose command the mountains obey. A believer harmed in Makkah can look at the surrounding mountains and know that the Lord owns them, and that human power does not place them outside His command.

Alongside this scene are the words, “And We made iron malleable for him.” What was known for its hardness and strength became easy for Dawud to shape, something we can picture by thinking of dough in the hands of its maker. This subjection of creation is connected with what the verses cultivate in those working for Allah’s religion. They must not say that the demands of calling people to faith, looking after the poor or public service leave no time for remembrance and night prayer. Dawud was a prophet and a king, yet he maintained this share of worship. The heavier the burdens, the greater the heart’s need for what helps it carry them.

The Prophet ﷺ was therefore told: Stand up in prayer at night except a little, half of it, or a little less, or a little more, and recite the Qur’an at a measured pace. (73:2–4). The connection between that prolonged prayer and carrying the message was then explained: We will send down upon you a weighty Word[2]. (73:5). Obedience supplies the strength for work; it is a mistake to let work become a reason to cut off that supply.

Nor was softening the iron a blessing without a purpose. Allah says of iron, “And We sent down iron, in which there is great might and benefits for people, so that Allah may make known those who help His cause and His messengers without seeing Him. Indeed, Allah is All-Powerful, All-Mighty.” (57:25). It can repel aggression and serve people’s needs, and its possessor is tested in how it is used. The ending, All-Powerful, All-Mighty, returns the favor to Allah. He needs neither His creatures’ equipment nor their strength. They need to fulfill the responsibilities of what He has given them. That is why the softening of the iron is followed by Dawud’s commission to make something useful.

Verse 11

“Make coats of mail, and maintain balance in the linking rings, and do [all of you] righteous deeds, for I am All-Seeing of what you do.”

Softening the iron was not the end of the favor. It was directed toward work people needed: “Make coats of mail.” These are ample coats of armor covering the body, so that someone is not protected on one side and exposed on the other, like a person holding a plate in front of themselves that does not surround them. This helps convey the idea of ample coverage: protection must reach where it is needed.

Then comes the command, “and maintain balance in the linking rings.” The Arabic term for linking conveys joining things together in an ordered succession. The same root is used of speech whose parts follow one another coherently. Here it concerns making the armor’s rings connect securely and proportioning what joins them so that the links do not fail or open up when the wearer moves. Just as pieces of fabric are joined by sewing, joining iron requires skilled work if the finished object is to serve its purpose.

The inclusion of this detail shows that good workmanship is part of gratitude. Saying that a project is charitable, or that it is being done for Allah, is no excuse for poor work. Worship, calling others to faith, serving people and preparing their protection all deserve to be done well. Of preparation Allah says, “Prepare against them whatever force[35] you can and cavalry, to deter Allah’s enemies and your enemies[36]” (8:60).

Behind this lies the responsibility attached to every blessing. A gift for teaching or speaking, money to spend, or the capacity to memorize and work is never detached from what its recipient will be asked about. A person must not imagine that giving of it is a favor in which the Giver has no right. Just as Dawud was given the ability and then told to work, Allah told Musa, “I have chosen you, so listen to what is being revealed” (20:13). He also said: O Moses, I have chosen you above other people, by giving you My messages and speaking to you. So hold fast to what I have given you, and be of those who are grateful. (7:144). Being chosen brings a responsibility; receiving a gift calls for action and gratitude.

Notice the shift in Arabic from the singular commands to make and to proportion, to the plural “and do [all of you] righteous deeds.” One reflection suggested by this is that a useful craft should not remain confined to its first practitioner. It should be taught to those who will carry it on, so that someone can sustain the skill when the first person is absent. This applies to religious knowledge, practical crafts and people’s affairs. Successful doctors, engineers, teachers and officials do more than perform their own work: they prepare others to learn from them and take responsibility after them. A person may become ill, leave or die; the entire good work should not depend on their continued presence. Related to this is the reminder, “Muhammad is no more than a messenger; there were messengers who passed away before him[63]. If he dies or is killed, will you turn back on your heels[64]?” (3:144).

The instruction is not simply to act, but to “do [all of you] righteous deeds.” The survival of a craft is not enough if the next generation uses it for corruption and domination. The same tools can protect or oppress. How they are used needs watching so that they do not become instruments for asserting power over others. A gift for speech can teach and advise, or mislead and corrupt. Work is therefore joined to awareness of Allah’s sight: “for I am All-Seeing of what you do.”

This corrects the assumption that every gift signals approval of everything about its recipient. Blessing is a test. Musa told his people, “It may be that your Lord will destroy your enemy and make you successors in the land to see how you will do.” (7:129). Moving from oppression to power was not the end of testing; it was entry into another kind of test. After deliverance and crossing the sea, some of the Children of Israel asked, “O Moses, make for us a god just as they have gods.” (7:138). Neither passing through a trial successfully nor possessing useful tools removes the need for enduring faith and watchfulness over how Allah’s gifts are used.

Verse 12

And to Solomon [We subdued] the wind: its morning course was a month’s journey and its evening course was a month’s journey. And We caused a stream of molten brass to flow for him, and there were some jinn who worked for him by his Lord’s permission; whoever among them deviated from Our command, We made him taste the punishment of the Blazing Fire.

Sulayman was granted several forms of mastery over creation: the wind, a spring of molten metal, and jinn working before him. In the morning the wind covered a month’s journey, and in the evening another month’s journey. In a single day he therefore traveled a distance that would take others two months. The lecturer explains the molten metal here as copper, which Allah caused to flow for him. Some of the jinn were also required to work by their Lord’s permission. This power did not make Sulayman independent of his Master, nor did it mean that the jinn bestow kingship on whomever they please. The text expressly says, “by his Lord’s permission,” and makes anyone departing from the command liable to punishment.

One way this blessing has been connected with another part of Sulayman’s story concerns the horses said to have distracted him from remembrance. The words “and he began striking [their] shanks and necks[14].” (38:33) have been interpreted as his slaughtering them; it has been said that Allah replaced them with the wind, which was faster (Ibn Kathir, commentary on 38:33). This interpretation reminds us that the means at our disposal must not distract us from their Lord. Work to uphold the truth cannot be sound if it cuts the worker off from remembering Allah.

‘Ali’s report provides a practical example. The Prophet ﷺ taught bedtime remembrance consisting of glorifying Allah thirty-three times, praising Him thirty-three times and declaring His greatness thirty-four times. ‘Ali kept to this remembrance, saying that he had not omitted it even on the night of Siffin (al-Bukhari, 5362). The severity of the situation, the demands of battle and its trials did not make him abandon the Prophet’s guidance.

The close of al-Muzzammil brings relief alongside illness, travel and fighting: “He knows that there are some among you who will be ill, and others traveling in the land, seeking the grace of Allah, and others fighting in Allah’s way. So recite as much of it as is easy for you” (73:20). One explanation of recitation here is recitation during night prayer. A person with an excuse maintains a connection through what is manageable, rather than making hardship a reason to abandon worship altogether. When something distressed the Prophet ﷺ, he prayed (Abu Dawud, 1319). Prayer is a place to seek help when the work becomes burdensome and matters grow difficult.

Another reflection on the order of these blessings is that iron, used to make armor, precedes copper, used in building. Protecting a society preserves its ability to develop, travel and live securely. Expanding the means of livelihood is of little benefit if people neglect what repels aggression. The hadith linking ‘inah transactions, attachment to cattle and cultivation, and abandonment of jihad to humiliation lasting until people return to their religion brings this meaning closer (Abu Dawud, 3462). Seeking provision and building a life cannot become an excuse for neglecting other responsibilities, just as public service cannot excuse neglecting worship.

The verse’s ending returns every form of subjection to Allah’s command: any jinn departing from it tastes the punishment of the Blazing Fire. One interpretation is that this warns of punishment in the Hereafter; another understands a punishment brought upon them in this world for disobeying. Power placed at someone’s disposal does not free them from responsibility, and a gift does not remove accountability for its use.

Verse 13

They made for him whatever he wished of sanctuaries[6], images[7], basins as large as reservoirs, and huge fixed cooking pots. “Do [good deeds] in gratitude, O household of David!” But few of My slaves are grateful.”

The context identifies some of the uses to which the power granted to Sulayman was put. The blessing was more than an increase in authority: there were places of worship, images, great serving dishes like water basins, and immovable cooking pots. The places of worship are not limited to the familiar modern niche where an imam stands; someone may have a place of worship within their home. One explanation of the Arabic name connects it with the worshipper’s struggle against Satan there. Placing worship first draws attention to what should come first in building a community. After arriving in Madinah, the Prophet ﷺ ordered the mosque to be built (al-Bukhari, 428), giving people a place to gather in remembrance of their Lord and receive His guidance.

The images are mentioned as something permitted under Sulayman’s law. That permission is not to be transferred to the law of the Prophet ﷺ, which prohibited them (al-Qurtubi, commentary on 34:13). The serving dishes are compared with large water basins: the scene conveys great size and abundance. Beside them stand pots so large and heavy that they cannot be moved. This highlights the use of ample means to feed and benefit people, rather than merely boast about possessions.

One application is that those able to do so should widen the avenues of public good through food, water and education, while attending to the scale of need. Giving a very small amount to a household with substantial expenses may not meet its need. The aim, according to one’s ability, is for the benefit to reach the level required. This does not reject a small gift from someone who can afford no more; it concerns making good use of the abundance a person has been given. Sincerity of intention is fundamental. One feeds others seeking Allah’s pleasure, even if the recipient is a non-Muslim or an animal. Benefiting creation is not detached from seeking the Creator’s approval.

The extraordinary gifts in Sulayman’s kingdom also raise a question for people of knowledge and industry: when the means of working iron and copper become available and their ability to build increases, what will they use that ability for? The blessing tests how it is used. Merely possessing it is not a certificate of righteousness.

Then comes the comprehensive responsibility: “Do [good deeds] in gratitude, O household of David!” Gratitude is action that shows recognition of the Giver’s favor. The household shares its responsibility just as it shared the blessings. The words “But few of My slaves are grateful.” awaken the recipient of blessing from pride in abundant possessions or in the number of people following the same path. In contrast to this example, another people will be told, “Eat of your Lord’s provision, and be grateful to Him” (34:15), only to answer the blessing by turning away.

The words “They made for him whatever he wished” also prepare us to understand the story’s ending. These jinn were working under compulsion. Once Sulayman’s death was discovered, it became apparent that they had been waiting to escape it. This will reveal the difference between the power of a command that moves bodies and the power of faith that moves people to work.

Verse 14

Then when We decreed Solomon’s death, nothing showed them that he was dead except a creature of the earth[8] eating away his staff[9]. When he fell down, the jinn realized that if they had truly known the unseen, they would not have remained in the debasing servitude[10].

These scenes of creation being placed at Sulayman’s service end with an event exposing the limits of bestowed power. Allah decreed Sulayman’s death. He remained leaning on his staff while the jinn worked, unaware that he had died, until the creature of the earth ate through the staff and he fell. Having the wind and jinn subdued for him did not place him beyond the decree of death. Nor did the jinn, whose strength people had witnessed, know an unseen matter so close to them. A small creature, by eating the staff, revealed what their power had not revealed. Had they known he was dead, they would not have remained at work that they regarded as humiliating punishment.

One explanation of the Arabic expression rendered “the jinn realized” is that their true condition became apparent: their lack of knowledge of the unseen was exposed. Another is that their followers discovered that the leaders who had given them the impression of knowing the unseen did not possess that knowledge (Ibn ‘Atiyyah, al-Muharrar al-Wajiz, commentary on 34:14). Abundant activity or extraordinary feats must not lead us to attribute knowledge to them that Allah has not given them.

The story’s ending connects with the surah’s beginning. All dominion belongs to Allah, who subdues whatever He wills of mountains, birds, iron, copper, wind and jinn. All knowledge belongs to Him, and those placed at another’s service know nothing hidden from them unless He teaches them. One reflection prompted by this ending concerns the people working alongside someone who serves Allah’s religion. In a time of weakness, that person may imagine that all they need is money and followers who do everything they wish, with no judgment or understanding of their own: give an order and they move. The labor of the subdued jinn reveals the limit of that kind of obedience. If someone works through compulsion alone, they wait for the person they fear to disappear so that they can escape the task. A message that continues after its bearer dies needs people who believe in it and understand why they give of themselves for it.

The closing days of the Prophet’s ﷺ life show another picture. On Monday morning he lifted the curtain of his home and saw his Companions in rows for Fajr, with Abu Bakr leading them. They were almost overcome by joy at seeing him, and he signaled to them to complete their prayer (al-Bukhari, 1205). The rows remained in place and worship continued. Their gathering did not depend on his personally directing every movement. Among his teachings was that whoever prays the morning prayer is under Allah’s protection (Muslim, 657). That scene combined their ordered prayer with their attachment to their Lord.

They then carried responsibility after his death. Abu Bakr showed his firmness regarding those withholding the right of zakah: “By Allah, I would fight against them even to secure the cord (used for hobbling the feet of a camel) which they used to give to the Messenger of Allah (as zakat) but now they have withheld it.” (Muslim, 20). Obedience was not a duty whose disappearance they awaited, but a trust for which they considered themselves responsible. Speaking of the Dajjal, the Prophet ﷺ said that if he appeared while the Prophet was among them, he would contend with him on their behalf. If he appeared afterward, “Allah would take care of every Muslim on my behalf (and safeguard him against his evil)” (Muslim, 2937). Hearts are thus directed back to Allah. Their connection with His protection is not severed by their prophet’s death.

Abu Bakr makes this truth especially clear. He was among those closest to the Prophet ﷺ in companionship and love, yet he stood firm when others were shaken after the Prophet’s death (al-Bukhari, 3667–3668). His love had not disappeared. It rested on a faith in Allah too firmly rooted for the message to end with the absence of the beloved. When Abu Bakr and ‘Umar visited Umm Ayman after the Prophet’s death, she wept. They reminded her that what Allah had prepared for His Messenger was better. She explained that she was weeping because revelation had ceased, and they wept with her (Muslim, 2454). This expresses something more precise than grief over companionship alone. They understood the value of what had reached them from heaven, and its cessation caused them pain.

This is the kind of upbringing that connects people with revelation. Allah says, “Be devoted servants of your Lord because of your teaching of the Scripture and because of your study thereof.” (3:79). A good teacher deepens students’ attachment to Allah’s Book instead of making the teacher’s own person the endpoint of their understanding and work. One effect of the trial at Uhud, when the Prophet ﷺ was rumored to have been killed, was to prepare hearts for the meaning of “Muhammad is no more than a messenger; there were messengers who passed away before him[63]. If he dies or is killed, will you turn back on your heels[64]?” (3:144). Loyalty to the Messenger’s guidance is shown by remaining faithful to it after losing him.

This does not mean that every individual should need no direction or division of tasks. Some people need to be shown what to do according to their abilities. The fault is in making the entire work depend on carrying out orders without understanding, faith or love for the work itself. Nor does it mean that a leader’s absence leaves no grief or loss. The Prophet ﷺ said that he was a source of security for his Companions, and his Companions a source of security for his community (Muslim, 2531). His death had consequences, but those consequences were not the collapse of the message or everyone’s abandonment of it.

The word for the staff adds to the reflection. It denotes the staff with which a herder drives and holds back the animals being led; the root also carries a sense of delaying. Its disappearance reminds us that movement sustained only by the stick ends when fear of it ends. Conviction and faith, by contrast, prompt giving even when the person issuing the orders is absent. Some Companions wished to give still more, until at one stage they were told, “Restrain your hands [from fighting][55], establish prayer and give zakah” (4:77). The motivation was already alive within them, and Allah’s command then governed it.

The beginning of the call to prayer gives another example of their concern for the message. As the Muslims increased in number, they needed a way to gather for prayer and consulted over a suitable means. ‘Abdullah ibn Zayd left carrying the same concern that occupied the Messenger ﷺ about gathering the people (Abu Dawud, 498). He did not treat it as a remote problem, waiting for an order to reach him. He carried it in his heart, then saw the dream of the call to prayer. When he told the Prophet ﷺ, the Prophet confirmed it and instructed him to teach it to Bilal, whose voice carried better. ‘Umar reported having seen a similar dream (Abu Dawud, 499). The dream approved by the Messenger was thus connected with a heart concerned for the community. The work was then done well by assigning the actual call to the person whose voice was better suited to it.

The Quran gives another example of such initiative: A man came from the farthest end of the city, rushing, and said, “O my people, follow the messengers. (36:20). There were already messengers in the town: When We sent them two [messengers], but they rejected them, so We strengthened them with a third, and they said, “We are sent to you as messengers.” (36:14). Yet the man did not conclude that their presence relieved him of participation. He came from the farthest part of the city to urge his people to follow them. Not every task must wait to be formally assigned. Each person can recognize a need and contribute an idea or ability within their capacity.

This clarifies the difference between someone carrying the message out of faith in it and someone regarding the work as a degrading burden they hope to escape. Abundant money and equipment cannot make up for the absence of faith. A generation nurtured by the Quran and aware of its responsibility can carry the trust and teach those who follow, allowing the call to continue after the caller has died.

Verse 15

There was a sign for [the people of] Sheba in their dwelling place: two gardens – one on the right and one on the left. “Eat of your Lord’s provision, and be grateful to Him; a pleasant land and a Forgiving Lord.”

The story of Saba stands opposite the gratitude shown in Dawud’s household. Both stories involve abundant giving; what differs is what people do with it. Saba was the name of a man from whom the people traced their descent. Their tribes dispersed, six in Yemen and four in al-Sham (al-Tirmidhi, 3222). The story’s purpose is not to stop at genealogies, but to make us see blessings close to everyday life: a pleasant home, ample provision, and then the safety of the roads described in the following verses.

These are the two blessings Allah reminded Quraysh of: “Who fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear[4].” (106:4). Of the sanctuary He says, “Have We not established for them a safe sanctuary [in Makkah] to which fruits of all kinds are brought as provision from Us?” (28:57). Saba’s history is not remote from the audience’s own condition. The One who gave those people provision and safety, then took them away after they turned aside, can do the same to anyone who becomes proud of blessings.

Beginning with their dwelling touches something basic to a person’s sense of stability. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Whoever among you wakes up in the morning healthy in his body, secure in his dwelling, and has his food for the day, it is as if the whole world has been given to him.” (al-Tirmidhi, 2346). Safety, health and sufficiency together are among the foundations of a settled life.

The sign in Saba’s dwelling was “two gardens – one on the right and one on the left.” One explanation is that gardens surrounded the settlement on both sides; another is that each home had two gardens, one on either side. Both show abundant provision thoughtfully arranged close to the residents. The many gardens, dense trees and gathered shade help us picture comfortable living, with food nearby and little cost or difficulty in obtaining it. The story begins with a sign and ends, “Indeed, there are signs in this for everyone who is patient and grateful.” (34:19). The blessing itself is a source of evidence, and the consequences of turning away open further paths to reflection.

Permission to enjoy the gift is not separated from the Giver’s right: “Eat of your Lord’s provision, and be grateful to Him.” Obedience does not mean rejecting good provision. It means enjoying it while recognizing that it comes from Allah and giving Him thanks. This applies to material and spiritual gifts alike. The angels told Maryam, “O Mary, Allah has chosen you, purified you, and chosen you over all women.” (3:42). Then came the responsibility: “O Mary, worship your Lord devoutly, prostrate yourself and bow down with those who bow down.” (3:43). In the same way, Musa’s selection for the message and for Allah’s speech was followed by “So hold fast to what I have given you, and be of those who are grateful.” (7:144). The honor of receiving a gift does not remove the need to act; it calls for action.

The people of Saba had both a pleasant land and a forgiving Lord. One explanation of “a Forgiving Lord” is that He did not hasten to punish them for their wrongdoing. He gave them time, reminded them and made the truth clear. Continued prosperity must not be treated as evidence that Allah approves of ingratitude. Those enjoying a blessing must not overlook the door to return that He has opened. A pleasant land is one blessing, forgiveness another, and both call the heart to thank its Lord.

Verse 16

But they turned away, so We sent against them a devastating flood[11] and replaced their two gardens with two others of bitter fruit, and tamarisk bushes, and a few lote trees.

They answered blessing and the command to give thanks by turning away. The very place that had gathered provision for them became a witness to its loss. Prosperity may last so long that a person forgets its Giver and regards well-managed means as a guarantee of its survival. Such was the owner of the two gardens: He entered his garden, having wronged himself[22], and said, “I do not think that this will ever perish, (18:35). He saw trees, water and careful arrangements, and thought his garden could not disappear. A people may similarly feel so secure in their dam and water channels that they imagine danger has become impossible.

The Quran exposes the root of this pride. It recalls the weakness of human creation in “created man from a clinging clot.” (96:2), and that knowledge is a gift in “taught man what he did not know[1].” (96:5). Then it says: Indeed, man transgresses when he deems himself to be self-sufficient. (96:6–7). A person is not truly self-sufficient, but sees themselves that way. At first they may be needy and afraid. Then their employment becomes secure, their body healthy and their money plentiful, and the problems with their vehicle and living arrangements are put right. They imagine that they no longer need anyone and grow bold in disobedience. If they sin and the blessing remains for a time, their pride may increase: they mistake respite for a guarantee of permanence.

The words “so We sent against them a devastating flood[11]” return the matter to the One who owns the means. What people have secured cannot frustrate Allah. He can turn the very source of their development into the cause of their lost prosperity. The Arabic word al-‘arim [a devastating flood[11]] has been explained as a violent flood, or as the dam or structure holding water between the mountains. Controlling the floods and releasing water according to need had sustained agriculture and the spread of gardens. When that foundation collapsed, the civilization built upon it suffered.

A country’s strength may similarly depend on a single resource, such as oil, and its people imagine that wealth is guaranteed as long as the resource remains. Yet resources themselves are subject to Allah, as He reminds them: Say, “What do you think, if your water were to sink deep into the earth, who can bring you flowing water?” (67:30). In the surah, immense gifts stand beside small causes exposing the weakness of those who boast of them. Allah placed great things at Dawud’s and Sulayman’s service, yet a creature eating a staff disclosed what was hidden from the jinn, and Saba’s dazzling prosperity vanished. The human body offers its own lesson: strength and health may come together in someone, only for a tiny ailment they never considered to disable them.

A striking example is the account of Ya’juj and Ma’juj. Their threat becomes so great that Allah reveals to ‘Isa that he should take the believers to al-Tur, because no one can fight them. Then Allah sends worms against their necks, and they perish (Muslim, 2937). Another narration describes their drinking the waters of the earth and becoming so arrogant that they cast weapons toward the sky. The weapons return stained with blood, and they imagine they have overcome its inhabitants; then this small creature destroys them (Ibn Majah, 4079). The size of a cause in human eyes does not measure Allah’s power.

Saba’s recompense was not merely losing the fruit. Two gardens remained to remind them of what the gardens had once been, with pleasant produce replaced by bitter and inferior food. Khamt [bitter fruit] has been explained as bitter fruit, or as the arak tree with its bitter fruit. Alongside it was tamarisk, which offered none of the pleasing fruit they had known. As for the lote tree, the best of what remained in this description, there were only “a few lote trees.” In Arabic, the initial reference to something remaining is followed by an explicit description of its scarcity, emphasizing how little benefit was left after abundance. It intensifies the regret of those who knew a blessing and then lost it.

For Makkah, with its provision, standing and commercial resources, this was a warning close to home. Economic strength cannot replace gratitude, and neither a country’s location nor its people’s money shields it from Allah’s authority.

Verse 17

This is how We punished them for their ingratitude; would We punish anyone except those who are utterly ungrateful?

The loss of blessing was not an event detached from the people’s response to it. Their recompense was linked to their ingratitude, making clear that receiving a gift does not remove responsibility and its continued presence does not permit denial of the Giver. These words warn the polytheists and reassure believers at the same time. Those with money and resources must not be proud of their possessions, and the oppressed must not imagine that an opponent’s power has passed outside Allah’s dominion.

This resembles the believer’s reply to the owner of the two gardens: it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden, and send upon your garden a thunderbolt from the heaven, turning it into a barren land. Or its water may sink, so you will never be able to get it back.” (18:40–41). The One who gave can take away, and can give to the person the owner despised for being poor. This reassurance returns to the surah’s opening. Allah owns everything in the heavens and earth; even the special subjection of iron, wind and copper for His prophets was His gift.

Believers therefore need to distinguish a heart at rest with Allah from a heart at rest with its money. Allah says: There are some people who worship Allah on the edge [of faith][6]: if something good comes to them, they are content; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they turn back[7], thus losing both this world and the Hereafter. That is the evident loss. (22:11). The description “they are content” connects the person’s sense of security with the good received. It is like someone whose heart will not settle until they have checked that the money is still in their possession; when it is lost, their religion is shaken with it. The Arabic word for good can also mean wealth, as in “if he leaves behind any wealth – a bequest should be made in favor of parents and immediate family members[78] with fairness” (2:180), and “and he is truly extreme in his love of wealth.” (100:8). The peace of faith, by contrast, rests on “for indeed in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find tranquility.” (13:28). The servant’s attachment to the Lord remains firm in prosperity and trial.

One explanation of “would We punish anyone except those who are utterly ungrateful?” is that it refers to this kind of punishment, which uproots the blessings of those who deny Allah, in contrast to a believer’s affliction that disciplines them and brings them back to their Lord. What matters here is the different stance toward Allah and the trial’s effect. A believer should not equate being drawn on into greater ingratitude with a calamity that awakens someone to repentance.

Verse 18

We placed [a chain of] towns between them and the towns We had blessed[12], and We made between them well-measured distances[13]: “Travel through them by night and day safely.”

After the provision of the two gardens comes a fuller account of safety and ease of movement. The blessed towns have been interpreted as the towns of al-Sham. Between Saba in Yemen and those lands ran a route linked by visible settlements, where travelers could find food, rest and a place to spend the night. They did not have to carry provisions for a long journey on which they might be stranded. They would travel one stage, stop at a town, then continue and find another when they needed to rest again.

The words “and We made between them well-measured distances[13]” convey proportionate, nearby stages. One stage was not so long that it exhausted the traveler, with another unnecessarily short. The journey was measured in a way that provided comfort. It has been said that Allah inspired this arrangement. One possible explanation of the road’s security is that relations and agreements with the tribes along it made travel easier, with those tribes willing to serve a prosperous people. In either case, the ease was a blessing from Allah, even when it came through arrangements made by people.

The expression “Travel through them” pictures the towns as one connected place, as though the traveler were moving within their own homeland rather than setting out onto an interrupted, frightening road carrying heavy provisions. Mentioning nights before days makes the safety more apparent, since night is a time when travelers are especially likely to be afraid. The permission includes the time of greatest need for reassurance. The plural nights and days also conveys comfort and safety extending throughout the journey, rather than only one passing stage.

Quraysh knew a similar blessing in their own safety and provision, so the story of Saba reminded them of what they already possessed. One application is concern for what improves people’s lives: making provision easier to obtain, preserving security, and preparing roads and places of rest for travelers. Spending on what removes the difficulty and danger of travel is a way of thanking Allah for the ease He has given, and benefits both the development of a land and the lives of its people.

Verse 19

But they said, “Our Lord, make [the distance between] our journeys longer[14],” and they wronged themselves. So We made them tales [of the past], and dispersed them in all directions. Indeed, there are signs in this for everyone who is patient and grateful.

They answered the ease of the road with an astonishing request: “Our Lord, make [the distance between] our journeys longer[14].” In the selected imperative reading, رَبَّنَا corresponds to [Our Lord], and بَاعِدْ to [make [the distance between] our journeys longer[14]]. Another reading uses the intensified imperative بَعِّدْ in place of بَاعِدْ, strengthening the sense of the distancing requested (Ibn al-Jazari, al-Nashr, the section on Saba). One explanation is that they grew tired of easy travel and nearby stopping places, wishing for stages so far apart that they would need camels and carried supplies. What ought to have inspired gratitude became a source of irritation.

One interpretation of the request is arrogant discontent with blessing. A person becomes so accustomed to well-being that they cease to recognize its value and ask for change even if it brings hardship. The perversity of such a request is brought closer by the deniers’ words, “O Allah, if this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the heaven or bring upon us a painful punishment[19].” (8:32). Rather than asking Allah to guide them to the truth if it was true, they answered it by asking for destruction.

Another explanation is that Saba’s words expressed denial when they were reminded that safe roads and nearby settlements were Allah’s favor. It was as though they said they did not need this blessing and had enough strength to cope with greater distances. On this interpretation, the request arose from the boldness of imagined self-sufficiency, rather than merely a wish for a different road. Another view is that their leading and wealthy people wanted it. Easy travel had put them on an equal footing with the poor, and they wanted the value of their camels and resources to become apparent, so that only those with means could travel. This resembles someone owning a vehicle who resents others managing without one and wishes their needs were farther away so that their own advantage would stand out. Yet another explanation is that they wanted a road away from the townspeople’s eyes, allowing them to commit sins unobserved. The corrupt request thus accompanied wrongdoing against themselves.

These are different explanations of the motive. They share the reversal of blessing: instead of leading to gratitude, it became an occasion for denial, superiority or sin. The declarative reading, with رَبُّنَا and the past-tense بَاعَدَ, presents a complaint about what had happened (al-Nashr, the section on Saba). One explanation attributes it to the weak after the stages grew farther apart; another attributes it to those who had made the request and then tasted its result. They had been irritated by closeness, and now they were irritated by distance. The problem was not an absence of every blessing, but a heart that failed to appreciate what it had received.

Changing human needs appear in the account of the prayer for rain. One Friday a man complained of too little rain and the loss of property, and the Prophet ﷺ prayed. The following Friday brought an appeal for relief from damage caused by abundant rain, and he prayed that it fall around them rather than upon them (al-Bukhari, 1014). Asking lawfully for harm to be removed differs from Saba’s arrogant ingratitude. The account nevertheless helps us see how quickly human circumstances change, and how people need their Lord in both conditions.

Such forgetfulness can arise with familiar blessings of family, children, transport and livelihood. A person complains about them repeatedly, only recognizing their worth after losing them. One related reflection concerns the Children of Israel asking for a replacement for manna and quails: “So call upon your Lord to bring forth for us from what the earth produces – its herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” (2:61). Wanting what one directly produces may be accompanied by the illusion of controlling one’s own affairs and no longer waiting for a gift from the Lord. Yet both the ability to work and its fruit are His favor. Someone may similarly imagine that learning to bring down rain, discovering the secret of the soul, or finding a place to live beyond the earth would take them outside Allah’s authority. No means ever extends beyond His dominion: “you cannot pass without [Our] authority.” (55:33).

The verse then describes the end. People once known for their unity and comfortable lives became stories told about a vanished time. Their scattering became proverbial: scattered like the people of Saba. Gardens, safety and community no longer surrounded them as a living reality; they became a tale whose reversal astonished its hearers. This gathers the heart around the One who gives and withholds. Laughter may be followed by tears, safety by fear and abundance by hardship. The servant does not control these states independently of the Lord, and therefore turns toward Him: “I turn my face towards the One Who originated the heavens and earth, inclining to true faith, and I am not one of those who associate partners with Allah.” (6:79).

The story closes with patience and gratitude. Gratitude preserves the heart’s attachment to the Giver in good times, and patience brings it back to Him in hardship. Its answer to calamity is not merely flight to another place while continuing to turn away. The hadith explains that all the believer’s affairs are good: in prosperity they give thanks, and it is good for them; in adversity they are patient, and it is good for them (Muslim, 2999). Those who recognize Allah’s blessing while it is present and return to Him when it is lost benefit from the signs ignored by people who became proud of blessing and were then scattered in regret.

Verse 20

Satan found his assumption[15] about them[16] to be true, for they followed him, except for a group of the believers,

The selected wording for the construction is [found his assumption[15] about them[16] to be true]. The word is read both صَدَّقَ and صَدَقَ, with and without the doubled consonant (al-Qurtubi, al-Jami‘ li-Ahkam al-Quran, commentary on 34:20). In these people’s following him, Iblis found his assumption confirmed. He expected them to respond to his temptation, and they did what he hoped. This was not knowledge of the unseen, but a supposition matching their choices. He had previously announced that he would mislead people, stating the exception as “except for Your chosen slaves among them” (15:40).

Satan finds openings for this temptation in a person who enjoys luxury without gratitude, neglects remembrance, or continually resents their allotted share. Such conditions prepare them to respond to what he makes attractive. The problem is not confined to the rich. Someone with little money may be deeply resentful, and Satan waits for the first trial that will drive them off their path. This resembles worshipping Allah on the edge: pleased in prosperity, but turning back when tested with something unwelcome. Just as someone promoting falsehood selects those likely to accept their words, temptation finds an opening in a heart content with heedlessness and resentful protest.

Yet not everyone followed: “except for a group of the believers.” The existence of this group defeats an excuse based merely on a corrupt environment or a severe trial. The test occurred, but some preserved their faith and refused to join the majority in ingratitude.

Verse 21

even though he had no authority over them, except that We might distinguish those who believe in the Hereafter from those who are in doubt about it. Your Lord is Watchful over all things.”

Satan’s authority is not a force compelling people to disbelief and disobedience. He calls, makes things attractive and tempts, and finds those who accept his invitation. This does not remove their responsibility for choosing. An example may help distinguish the two: someone draws another person’s attention to something forbidden. They do not tie the person up and force them to look; they present something desirable, and the person already disposed toward it responds.

Satan therefore says when the matter is settled, “I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you responded to me[11]. So do not blame me, but blame yourselves.” (14:22). They heard an invitation to sin and accepted it, while hearing an invitation to obedience and turning away. An invitation they willingly accepted is no excuse that erases the difference between the two paths.

Denying compulsion does not deny whispering or the powerful attraction of temptation. The Prophet ﷺ said that Satan flows through the human being as blood flows (al-Bukhari, 3281). The presence of this trial reveals the heart’s certainty about the Hereafter, or its doubt. The more a servant recalls meeting Allah and the consequences of actions, the stronger the resistance to what is made attractive. Remembering the grave and seeing where human life ends weaken desire’s hold when the heart keeps that reminder alive.

This resembles a reckless driver who has a painful accident and undergoes a long recovery. They return to driving with great caution, but when they forget what happened, they return to their old habits. The problem is not that a warning never reached them, but that heedlessness erases its effect. Allah describes someone standing before Him as “the one who constantly worships during the night, prostrating and standing, fearing the Hereafter and hoping for the mercy of his Lord” (39:9). That fear accompanies the person’s words and deeds. Before acting, they ask about the outcome because they do not regard this world as the end of the road. As that certainty becomes rooted, they draw farther from following Satan, unlike someone whose doubts and desires have left the heart without fear of judgment.

Verse 22

Say, “Call upon those whom you claim [to be gods] besides Allah. They do not have even an atom’s weight of authority in the heavens or on earth, nor do they have any share in either of them, nor is any of them a helper to Allah.”

After the two stories have shown how Allah acts within His dominion—giving, subduing creation and then withdrawing blessings from those who are ungrateful—the challenge comes to those who call upon other deities. Let them ask those deities to do something for which they have placed their hopes in them. Did their power soften iron for Dawud, subdue the wind and jinn for Sulayman, or give Saba its gardens and then take them away?

The verse then dismantles, one by one, the grounds for attachment to these supposed deities. First is independent ownership, denied even to the extent of an atom. No star in the sky and nothing on earth is theirs to govern by their own power. Ibrahim’s argument demonstrates this helplessness: Abraham said, “It is Allah Who brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west.” Thus the disbeliever was dumbfounded (2:258). Next, the verse denies that they possess a share with Allah in the dominion of the heavens and earth. Someone may not own something alone yet have a share alongside others; that possibility is closed as well.

Then assistance is denied: “nor is any of them a helper to Allah.” None is an assistant Allah needs to establish and govern His dominion. The Arabic word describes someone who helps another and strengthens their support, whereas Allah sustains His creation without needing any of them. The object of worship thus owns nothing independently, has no share in Allah’s dominion, and provides no assistance He needs. This applies to anyone people attach themselves to besides Allah, including those He has honored, such as the angels or ‘Isa. A servant’s honor does not make them a partner of their Lord.

All three possibilities are denied of creation, unlike intercession, which is explained next. Something denied altogether must not be confused with something that occurs only by permission. When this meaning settles in the heart, it becomes devoted to Allah rather than torn between the demands of beings imagined to control its affairs. In the morning remembrance, the servant acknowledges entering the morning with all dominion belonging to Allah and gives Him praise (Muslim, 2723). Joining praise with the acknowledgment of dominion accords with the peace of knowing that everything belongs to one Lord.

The Quran illustrates this: “Allah makes a comparison of a slave owned by several quarrelsome masters, and a slave owned by only one master. Are they equal in comparison?” (39:29). Someone pulled between conflicting partners is not like someone devoted to one master. Similarly, once the heart knows that dominion belongs to Allah alone, it does not regard opponents’ abundant wealth and resources as an authority above His.

Verse 23

No intercession will be of any benefit before Him, except for those whom He gave permission. Until when their hearts are relieved of dread[17], they will say [to the angels], “What did your Lord just say?” They will say, “The Truth, and He is the Most High, Most Great.”

After ownership, partnership and assistance have been denied, a polytheist may still cling to the hope of mediation. The verse explains that intercession itself does not operate independently of Allah: “No intercession will be of any benefit before Him, except for those whom He gave permission.” Intercession is not denied absolutely, as the three preceding descriptions were. Its benefit is made conditional on His permission; no intercessor can impose anything upon Him. One interpretation is permission for the intercessor to intercede; another is permission concerning the person for whom intercession is made. Either defeats the claim that attachment to a deity guarantees its mediation before Allah. Angels, prophets and believers granted permission intercede as His submissive servants, not as holders of authority alongside Him.

One explanation of what follows is that those awaiting permission remain in fear and awe. Once permission is granted, fear is lifted from their hearts and they ask what their Lord has said. This has been understood of the intercessors and also of those hoping permission will be given to intercede for them. Even this honored position is preceded by waiting for permission and fearing Allah. The expression “their hearts are relieved of dread[17]” means that fear has been removed. In this Arabic usage, doubling the verb can express removal, as with the verb for nursing someone through an illness so that the illness is removed.

Another interpretation connects the scene with polytheists who followed Iblis until death revealed what they had ignored. Then they ask what Allah said, wanting to obey after the time for action has passed. On this explanation, the endpoint is connected with the earlier account of following Iblis.

Among the clearest interpretations is the scene of the angels when Allah decrees a matter in heaven. The hadith describes them beating their wings in submission to His words, with a sound like chains dragged over rock—explained here as smooth stone. When fear is lifted from their hearts, they ask what their Lord said, and the answer is the truth (al-Bukhari, 4800). The majesty filling heaven with awe belongs to no supposed deity, but to the One who speaks revelation and decrees affairs. Their word “The Truth” describes His speech as true and free from falsehood, just as a judgment is described as a judgment in truth.

The angels’ submission becomes more apparent in the report of Jibril seen on the Night Journey like an old, worn garment from fear of Allah (al-Tabarani, al-Mu‘jam al-Awsat, 4679; al-Suyuti judged its chain sound). This is Jibril, whose immense creation is described as having six hundred wings (al-Bukhari, 3232). His high rank and great size do not diminish the completeness of his servitude.

The connection with what precedes is clear. Just as dominion, giving and withholding belong to Allah alone, so does the revelation before which the angels submit. What revelation have the supposed deities brought that would entitle them to a share of worship? Even Musaylimah attached his false claim of prophethood to Allah; he did not make it evidence of some different lord sending revelation.

The lesson now turns toward earth. If the angels submit to Allah’s words, those hearing His revelation should ask what He intends so that they may comply, rather than oppose it with their desires or someone else’s opinion. Ibn ‘Abbas objected when a report from the Prophet ﷺ was countered with the words of Abu Bakr and ‘Umar, fearing the consequences of placing another person’s words before the Sunnah (al-Khatib, al-Faqih wa-l-Mutafaqqih, 1:377–378). This did not belittle their virtue. It honored the source they themselves followed. The same recognition of revelation’s worth lay behind Umm Ayman’s tears at its cessation, and Abu Bakr’s and ‘Umar’s tears with hers (Muslim, 2454). Hearts that understand its rank do not receive Allah’s words as passing speech.

Verse 24

Say, “Who provides for you from the heavens and earth?” Say, “Allah! And surely either we or you[18] are rightly guided or clearly misguided.”

After the stories and evidence, the repeated command to speak moves the surah into direct proclamation and argument. The question concerns a need the listener cannot escape: who provides for you? Rain from heaven and plants from earth are not owned by the deities being invoked. The One who gave to Dawud and Sulayman, showered Saba with gifts and then took them away is the One providing for the listener now. The command to answer “Allah!” returns the need to its true Provider, as does the question: Say, “What do you think, if your water were to sink deep into the earth, who can bring you flowing water?” (67:30).

Illness, fear or approaching death may uncover this truth for someone whose prosperity had hidden it. A storm at sea, for example, strips away many illusions sustained on land. The account of ‘Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl tells of his fleeing by sea and being caught in a storm. The crew said that their gods could not help them and that they must make their supplication sincerely to Allah. ‘Ikrimah recognized that deliverance on land, like deliverance at sea, could come only through sincere devotion to Allah. He resolved to return to the Prophet ﷺ if Allah saved him, then returned and accepted Islam (al-Nasa’i, 4067). There are not different lords of land and sea; hardship exposes heedlessness.

After these arguments comes a gentle way of reasoning: “And surely either we or you[18] are rightly guided or clearly misguided.” This is not doubt on the Messenger’s part about the truth he brought. It is an accommodation in speech, inviting an opponent to examine the two paths without beginning by provoking anger. The claim of one God and the claim of multiple gods cannot both be right. Saying that the supposed deities provide or intercede without permission contradicts the evidence of their helplessness. Nor does accusing the Messenger of dividing father from son remove the opponents’ worship of something besides Allah. One path must be guidance and the other error. The Arabic expression rendered “rightly guided” carries a sense of being firmly established upon guidance.

A useful principle for calling people to faith appears here: seeking to guide them takes precedence over inflicting a rhetorical defeat. Someone may be able to mock an opponent yet lose the opportunity to open their heart to reflection. Gentleness does not mean abandoning clear explanation. This address follows the arguments, stories and detail already presented. When the characteristics of truth and falsehood are explained from the Quran, people can recognize the two paths without the explanation depending on naming every individual: “This is how We explain the verses in detail, so that the way of the evildoers may become known.” (6:55).

In a time of weakness, making room for this explanation and dialogue is a great objective. The story of the people of the cave shows fear that prevents believers from openly expressing faith. The story of the two gardens, however, shows some room for discussion: His companion said, while conversing with him, “Do you disbelieve in He Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then fashioned you into a well-proportioned man[24]? (18:37). Having someone willing to hear an argument and allow its presentation opens an opportunity to work, even when the caller cannot change the whole situation. That is why clarity of truth is pursued alongside a gracious manner of speaking.

Verse 25

Say, “You will not be questioned about our misdeeds, nor will we be questioned about your deeds.”

The address becomes gentler still, using language contrary to what the listener might expect: “our misdeeds” on the side of the Messenger and believers, and “your deeds” on the side of their opponents. This does not acknowledge faith as a crime. It accommodates their accusation for the sake of establishing the argument: even assuming your charge against us, you do not bear responsibility for our actions, nor do we bear responsibility for yours. Why, then, prevent us from explaining what we believe?

Their actions are not described here with the corresponding term for criminal wrongdoing, despite the evident idolatry, because the purpose is to keep open the possibility of reflection and guidance. It is not to obtain a winning phrase and then walk away. Someone concerned for another’s guidance may say: examine the statement, even if your judgment of me remains unchanged; you have your responsibility and I have mine, and a judgment will come that settles the matter between us.

This connects with the previous verse. The two paths have already been shown unable to be true together. Separating responsibility then becomes an argument against obstructing the message and silencing its bearer: responsibility before Allah is not removed that way. Believers need such an approach when opportunities to convey the message narrow, combining firmness in the message with concern that dialogue remain possible.

Verse 26

Say, “Our Lord will gather us together, then He will rightly judge between us, for He is the All-Knowing Judge[19].”

After distinguishing the two sides’ responsibilities, the verse reminds them of the authority no one can prevent from acting. Allah will gather everyone, then decide between them in truth. The expression “then He will rightly judge between us” means that He will adjudicate. The Arabic term also evokes opening what was closed. A debate may end with people unwilling to listen, who possess money, resources and the power to restrict others. A believer must not take the apparent closure of the way to mean that truth has no judgment to vindicate it.

The decision belongs to Allah, “the All-Knowing Judge[19].” He knows circumstances, intentions and what each side deserves. Neither ignorance nor injustice enters His judgment. A human judge may decide while anger distorts their view; Allah’s adjudication is joined to all-encompassing knowledge, so no right hidden from people is lost before Him.

Reflecting on this brings greater hope in Allah’s opening when avenues for the message are closed, the means of power and public speech are monopolized, and a person upholding the truth cannot reach their goal. These meanings nourish those concerned for people’s guidance, connecting their weak efforts to a Lord who knows what they give and what obstructs them. One indication of sincere concern for serving the religion is the Prophet’s ﷺ statement that someone who dies without having taken part in a military expedition or considered doing so dies upon a branch of hypocrisy (Muslim, 1910). The report concerns military campaigning. One lesson drawn from it here is that the desire to support the religion cannot be a passing sentiment detached from the heart’s readiness to give. When that concern is sincere, the divine name al-Fattah [Judge[19]], literally the Opener, becomes evident in the servant’s hope and patience.

Verse 27

Say, “Show me those whom you have associated with Him as partners. No indeed! Rather, He is Allah, the All-Mighty, All-Wise.”

This argument ends with a request exposing the claim of partnership at its root: show me those you have made Allah’s partners, and show what establishes their entitlement to that position. The verses have shown Allah’s acts, dominion and governance. Where is any comparable act by those attached to Him as partners? A similar challenge appears in “Is He Who watches every soul in whatever it does [like others]? Yet they ascribe partners to Allah.” (13:33).

He provides for every soul, supplies what sustains it, knows its deeds and secrets, and determines its appointed end. Yet people assign Him partners who independently do none of these things. The point is not merely to display the names or images of the supposed deities, but to expose the falsehood of placing them alongside Allah in entitlement to worship. “No indeed!” rejects the claim. Then comes the clear affirmation: “Rather, He is Allah, the All-Mighty, All-Wise.” The truth is not changed by the many partners people invent, and their claims grant those partners none of the true God’s attributes.

Verse 28

We have not sent you [O Prophet] except as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner to all mankind, but most people do not know.

The Prophet’s ﷺ mission does not depend on the response of the Quraysh opponents he has been addressing. It is for all people. When one people turns away, Allah prepares others to carry what they rejected. This resembles His words, “If you turn away, He will replace you with other people, then they will not be like you.” (47:38). It became evident when Allah prepared people in Madinah who believed in and supported the message after the stubborn rejection in Makkah. People need the call that guides them; turning away does not make anyone independent of its guidance.

The description “a bearer of glad tidings and a warner” defines the duty entrusted to the Messenger. He gives glad tidings to those who respond and warns those who turn away. He is not commanded to make his mission dependent on every suggestion made by an obstinate opponent. They demanded signs like those given to Musa, a book sent down from heaven, and, in Makkah, that al-Safa be turned to gold (Ahmad, 2333; al-Hakim judged the chain sound). If a caller chased every proposal, from a tunnel into the earth to a ladder into the sky, the demands of those unwilling to respond would never end.

This does not deny the signs with which Allah supported the Prophet. It explains that his truthfulness does not depend on satisfying demands that change whenever an answer is given. A stubborn opponent can follow every answer with a fresh request until the caller spends a lifetime pursuing objections and neglects the central duty of conveying the message. They said, “If only he was given the like of what was given to Moses before[21].” (28:48), then exposed their position with “We will never believe in this Qur’an or in any [Scripture] before it.” (34:31). The form of the sign was not what prevented them from believing.

Understanding glad tidings and warning makes clear that the duty is to explain revelation plainly. The Quran that supplies the proof is not material to be changed to suit opponents’ desires. This duty preserves the direction of the call while its message remains universal.

Verse 29

They say, “When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?”

After the discussion of the true Provider, the falsehood of idolatry and the universality of the message, their objection returns to the timing of judgment. Asking about the promise reveals what lies behind much of the argument. These people did not simply want further explanation. They disliked the prospect of their actions ending in a reckoning that would require them to change.

Surah al-Qiyamah presents the objection to resurrection, then reveals this motive: Does man think that We cannot reassemble his bones? In fact, We are able to restore even his very fingertips. But man desires to persist in sin[2], and asks [mockingly], “When is the Day of Resurrection?” (75:3–6). It begins with disbelief that bones will be gathered, then explains that the wish to persist in wrongdoing lies behind the question about the Day of Resurrection. Someone rejecting the message may therefore use an objection about the Book or the possibility of resurrection as a screen for the wish to continue following desire. When evidence answers it, another objection takes its place, because acknowledging accountability is what they are avoiding.

One application is the person who calls divine legislation outdated or unsuitable as a way of turning away from submission itself. The problem is then not merely a phrase that needs explaining, but a willingness either to accept responsibility or reject it. Here in the surah, their question is an act of rejection and denial after the truth has been explained. Those unwilling to let revelation guide their actions ask about the promise that they will answer for what they do. The lesson lies in the motive the passage brings to light. Someone who asks in order to learn has a different purpose. We should not attribute that same hidden motive to them.

Verse 30

Say, “There is an appointed Day for you that you can neither delay for a while, nor can you bring it forward.”

The answer establishes that they have an appointment they can neither advance nor delay. Fulfillment of the promise does not depend on their demanding it sooner, nor can they ask for more time once it arrives. After the explanation already given, the address moves beyond repeatedly debating whether resurrection exists to what will happen to them there. Their day comes at its appointed time, and each soul faces the consequences of its choices.

This resembles someone insisting that no examination awaits them, only to be answered with what they will find on the day of the examination and the consequences of failing to prepare. The speaker does not accept the denial, but carries the discussion from an abstract claim to its practical outcome. That is why the surah follows the fixed appointment with a scene of wrongdoers held before their Lord and blaming one another. What they dismissed while secure in this world becomes a situation they cannot get past. What they demanded be hastened cannot be repelled when it arrives. Once this settles in the heart, the useful question becomes how to prepare for what is coming, now that the appointment has been shown to belong entirely to Allah.

Verse 31

Those who disbelieve say, “We will never believe in this Qur’an or in any [Scripture] before it.” If only you could see when the wrongdoers would be made to stand before their Lord, throwing blame at one another! Those who were oppressed will say to those who were arrogant, “Had it not been for you, we would have been believers.”

The deniers revealed their underlying position by announcing that they would believe neither in the Quran nor in the scriptures preceding it. They had previously demanded, “Bring us a different Qur’an, or make changes in it[19].” (10:15). They wanted a message that would not bind them to the oneness of Allah and accountability they disliked. The Messenger was commanded to answer, “It is not for me to change it on my own accord; I only follow what is revealed to me.” (10:15). The Messenger is a servant commanded to convey the message, without the right to create a religion suited to people’s desires. When alteration was refused, it became clear that asking for a book like Musa’s was not readiness to accept revelation. They ended by rejecting the Quran and every preceding scripture.

One interpretation identifies the speakers here as the arrogant chiefs. This connects their words with their earlier efforts to oppose Allah’s signs and their later boasting of wealth and children. An opponent may possess money and resources yet fear someone lacking those things because they recognize the influence of the Quran that person carries. Their efforts therefore turn toward cutting people off from their source of guidance.

One way of diverting them is weakening their connection with Arabic until they hear the Quran without understanding it. The gap may widen until its wording and explanations become difficult, or knowledge of it may be pushed to the margins of education. Someone can then attain a high standing in worldly sciences while being unable to recite al-Fatihah properly. The problem is not an inability to learn: they have demonstrated the ability to acquire what they considered important. It is turning away from knowledge needed for their religion.

A sound relationship with the Quran gives people a standard by which to weigh the objections presented to them. They recognize when a statement contradicts revelation and are not deceived merely by the speaker’s fame or the reach of the platform. Among the passages providing this standard are “Whoever does not judge according to what Allah has revealed, it is they who are the disbelievers.” (5:44), “Whoever does not judge according to what Allah has revealed, it is they who are the wrongdoers.” (5:45), and “Whoever does not judge according to what Allah has revealed, it is they who are the rebellious.” (5:47), as well as “The Jews and the Christians will never be pleased with you until you follow their religion.” (2:120). Returning to what Allah revealed keeps foundational principles clear and does not substitute people’s approval for truth. Those seeking to divert the community from guidance try to distance it from the Book that has not been altered.

The context then moves from the arrogant person saying “We will never believe” to that person standing before the Lord: “If only you could see when the wrongdoers would be made to stand before their Lord.” In Arabic, the result of the conditional is left unstated, allowing the heart to imagine the terrible sight one would witness. The person who detained others, restricted them and prevented believers from worshipping is now detained and questioned: “and stop them, for they are to be questioned.” (37:24). This reassures the oppressed. Acts they could not prevent are not lost, and the tyrant does not remain in command when Allah gathers creation.

The wrongdoers here include both the arrogant and those who willingly followed them. The leader wronged others; the follower wronged themselves by consenting to obedience in falsehood. Conversations between leaders and followers recur in the Quran because some people have the capacity to lead and others choose to follow. Leadership can serve guidance or misguidance. Of Pharaoh and his people Allah says, “We made them leaders[17] calling to the Fire” (28:41). A small group may direct a great multitude through its money and platforms, but following it does not remove the duty to consider the evidence.

In al-Baqarah, the language emphasizes following: “[Consider] when those who were followed will disown their followers when they see the punishment, and all ties between them will be cut off.” (2:166). This draws attention to the danger of imitation replacing proof. Here the repeated descriptions of arrogance and being treated as weak expose the connection between the leaders’ power and wealth and submission to them. The expression “throwing blame at one another” pictures mutual accusations, with bonds of shared advantage turned into hostility. Ibrahim said something similar: “You have taken idols other than Allah as a means of love among yourselves in the life of this world, but on the Day of Resurrection you will disown and curse one another.” (29:25).

They had cooperated for gain. A follower might harm truth and its people or circulate an objection for payment. Now comes the hour when they speak after long silence, but the time for action has passed. Arrogance seeks superiority over people and feeds upon the submission of those content to be led. The people portrayed as weak in this scene accepted being treated as people unable to say no to falsehood; they were not deprived of every capacity to choose. Both sides’ perception was distorted: the arrogant person saw themselves above everyone, and the follower saw themselves as too lowly to question or object.

The ugliness of the first attitude is brought closer by “Do not turn your face away from people [in contempt][7]” (31:18). The Arabic image likens the proud turning away to a condition that twists a camel’s neck. The posture in which the arrogant person takes pride signals a defect rather than perfection. Opposite it is the follower who grows accustomed to silence about injustice until inward rejection disappears: they see a weak person harmed or a theft committed and pass on as though it has nothing to do with them. Each acceptance and silence makes the oppressor bolder.

The words “Had it not been for you, we would have been believers.” show how deeply the leaders’ intimidating presence had taken hold. The Arabic conditional links the absence of faith to those people’s presence, as though their very existence were an obstacle, even before the followers expressly describe the plotting in the dialogue that follows. One application is to examine the excuses placed after one’s own if only: work, children, temptations or circumstances. A person may turn them into a barrier to night prayer, spending and giving, imagining what they would do if the obstacles disappeared instead of asking what they can do alongside them.

Al-Sarakhsi dictated portions of al-Mabsut while imprisoned. His biography relates that he was in a well, with students above writing down his dictation; loss of freedom did not end his benefit to others (al-Jawahir al-Mudiyyah, 3:78; Egyptian Dar al-Ifta, biography of al-Sarakhsi). Ibn al-Qayyim wrote portions of Zad al-Ma‘ad while traveling away from his books (the author’s introduction). Ibn Taymiyyah wrote on meanings of the Quran during imprisonment (Ibn ‘Abd al-Hadi, al-‘Uqud al-Durriyyah, account of his writings). An obstacle may change the means of giving without extinguishing the will to give.

The paths of conveying the message also varied under opposition: the Prophet ﷺ called people in Makkah, went to al-Ta’if and migrated to Madinah; his Companions migrated to Abyssinia. Closing one route did not end the message, and effort did not stop with methods already tried. In the account of al-Hudaybiyyah he expressed his resolve to continue “till I get killed”—giving his life for his cause (al-Bukhari, 2731–2732).

Behind every instance of steadfastness lies a path of striving that someone admiring the result alone may not see. A person may wish for a martyr’s rank and glad tidings, or for people to speak of a good ending, while being unwilling to tread the path to what they desire. Allah says, “You did wish for martyrdom[62] before facing it, and now you have seen it with your own eyes.” (3:143). The verse thus turns the heart from making responsibility depend on someone else’s presence to sincere choice and giving, before the day comes when a person regrets whom they followed.

Verse 32

Those who were arrogant will say to those who were oppressed, “Was it we who prevented you from guidance after it came to you? Rather, you yourselves were wicked.”

The arrogant refuse to carry the responsibility of their followers alone. They confront them with a question: did we prevent you from guidance after it reached you? You had a will that accepted our invitation and found in following us something suited to your desires and interests. Hence their words, “Rather, you yourselves were wicked.” Reporting their answer does not acquit their plotting; the next verse will expose it. It shows that the existence of someone making falsehood attractive does not erase the choice of the person accepting it.

Beside these people were others who heard the invitation to truth, responded and endured harm. Different responses under partly shared circumstances expose the weakness of the sweeping excuse that the leaders’ mere presence prevented faith. The words “after it came to you” make the matter more serious. Guidance was not simply an account hidden from them. It reached them, and they chose otherwise.

When faith enters the heart, it possesses a strength beyond comparison with an opponent’s money. In Heraclius’s conversation with Abu Sufyan, he asked whether the Prophet’s followers were the distinguished people or the weak. He was told that the weak followed him, their numbers increased, and none who entered the religion renounced it out of displeasure. Heraclius explained that this is the nature of faith when its sweetness enters hearts (al-Bukhari, 7).

Two ways of obstructing guidance become clear: preventing guidance from reaching people, and preventing people from reaching guidance. The first targets those carrying it. Allah says, “And [remember] when the disbelievers plotted against you[18], to take you as captive, kill you or expel you.” (8:30). The first Arabic verb here means to imprison; imprisonment, killing and expulsion are named. The second keeps the audience occupied with desires, noise and objections that obstruct hearing and the heart, as in their words, “Do not listen to this Qur’an, but make noise [when it is recited], so that you may prevail.” (41:26). They try to prevent attentive, receptive listening because they do not control a heart that has turned to Allah.

One interpretation of “and Allah will never give the disbelievers a way to [overcome] the believers.” (4:141) concerns such authority over hearts. The hearts of Allah’s servants are in His hand. The Prophet ﷺ said that they are between two of the Most Merciful’s fingers, and He turns them as He wills (Muslim, 2655). The arrogant have no independent power to remove guidance from a heart holding firmly to it.

This returns to the earlier discussion of Iblis. Allah denied him compelling authority; he will tell his followers that he called them and they answered. Similarly, a follower cannot make Satan’s temptation or an arrogant person’s invitation an excuse erasing responsibility. “We did misguide you, for we ourselves were misguided” (37:32) pictures the misleader meeting those who accepted the misguidance. Each bears responsibility for what they did, despite their different positions of strength and weakness.

Verse 33

Those who were oppressed will say to those who were arrogant, “Rather, [it was your] conspiracy of night and day when you were ordering us to disbelieve in Allah and to set up rivals to Him.” They will hide their remorse when they see the punishment, and We will put shackles around the necks of those who disbelieved. Will they be recompensed except for what they used to do?

The followers answer by exposing what the arrogant tried to conceal. Your invitation was no passing remark: behind it lay “conspiracy of night and day,” relentless planning and spending on what diverted people from guidance. Some followers had seen the process from within, knowing what was planned and spent, yet remaining silent through fear or benefiting from payment. The leaders are not innocent of misleading, as their answer suggests; nor are those who responded innocent of choosing, as their complaint suggests. Both wrongs are exposed in the exchange of blame.

The plotting grows through platforms and means that spread objections or stir desires, making both night and day a continuous period of work. This awakens believers to the effort actually required. A person may seek promotion at work in order to have more rest, while leaders of falsehood increase their expenditure and planning as their influence grows. Iblis said, “Grant me respite until the Day they are resurrected.” (7:14). He did not intend a quiet interval, but an extension of his work of temptation.

His involvement with human beings begins at birth. The hadith describes his touching the newborn, excepting Maryam and her son. Abu Hurayrah then cited Maryam’s mother’s prayer, “and I seek refuge with You for her and her offspring from Satan[17], the accursed.” (3:36; al-Bukhari, 3431). This is an enmity extending across time, finding an opening in every condition. The verse states the purpose organizing the work: “when you were ordering us to disbelieve in Allah and to set up rivals to Him.” Rivals are those people set alongside Allah in what belongs to Him alone. The plotting reaches the corruption of belief in Allah’s oneness, rather than stopping at an isolated sin detached from knowing the Lord. Something similar appears in what Allah relates of the jinn after they recognized the truth: “and that the fool among us [i.e., Satan] used to say outrageous things about Allah[2]” (72:4). They recognized the falsehood of what they had been receiving about Him.

Among the openings for temptation described in hadith is resentful objection to the decree after an event. If-only complaints open the way for Satan’s work (Muslim, 2664). Instead of learning from experience, a person moves into resentment undermining submission. Another is the temptation Satan seeks when a woman goes out. In a report al-Tirmidhi judged hasan, the Prophet ﷺ said, “The woman is Awrah, so when she goes out, the Shaitan seeks to tempt her.” (al-Tirmidhi, 1173). He looks for situations in which he can find an opening to make temptation attractive. Markets are another example. Salman warned against prolonging one’s time in them, describing them as Satan’s battlefield, where he raises his banner (Muslim, 2451). These different openings show that he does not use a single approach for everyone or leave a stage of life without temptation.

One way belief in Allah’s oneness may be corrupted in children is through stories repeatedly presenting characters as controlling the wind, provision or other affairs of creation, and then portraying them as multiple gods. The child grows up among competing supposed lords rather than with a heart devoted to Allah. Being young does not mean that what is received leaves no effect; repeatedly presented meanings help shape understanding. This also includes taking Allah’s names and attributes as titles for fictional figures whose acts are made to rival His. Those raising a child must attend to what reaches the heart, rather than treating such material as entertainment without meaning. Other forms suit the interests of adults, adolescents, men and women, while sharing the attempt to divert them from revelation.

This is the significance of “conspiracy of night and day.” They did not stop at declaring “We will never believe in this Qur’an” and then rest. They worked to turn others away too. Those sincere in saying that the Quran guides should not merely praise it and then stop teaching and conveying it. The Quran makes clear that those serving falsehood know weariness as well: “If you are suffering harm, they too are suffering harm just like you; but you hope from Allah what they do not hope.” (4:104). Hardship is not confined to believers, but they carry a hope opponents do not, with purpose and reward helping them to endure and give.

The dispute ends with what lies beyond words: “They will hide their remorse when they see the punishment.” Followers regret those they obeyed and the adviser they ignored; leaders regret what arrogance led them to do. One explanation is that the arrogant hide their remorse to preserve an appearance of composure before their followers. Another is that the horror overwhelms them so completely that they cannot express what is within. Concealing remorse here does not prevent its appearance in another situation: successive states each reveal an aspect of the terror.

Once they see the punishment, accusations, curses and attempts to escape responsibility do not help. Then come the words, “and We will put shackles around the necks of those who disbelieved.” These are restraints around the neck by which people are led. One reflection on the image is the suitability of this recompense for someone who willingly allowed their mind to be led in this world, following without examination or questioning despite clear evidence against the falsehood. Following Musaylimah’s false claim despite the Arabs’ linguistic knowledge and powers of discernment is an example of abandoning judgment. The obstacle was not always lack of the necessary capacity, but acceptance of subservience. This correspondence does not restrict the punishment to followers: both leaders and those responding to them fall within the description of disbelief.

The verse closes by returning the matter to justice: “Will they be recompensed except for what they used to do?” What they see is the consequence of their deeds, not an injustice inflicted on them or a dispute they can settle by transferring their entire burden to someone else.

Verse 34

We never sent a warner to a town except that its affluent ones said, “We disbelieve in what you have been sent with.”

After the hostility between the arrogant and the weak is exposed in the Hereafter, the context returns to the origin of their relationship in this world. Those corrupted by luxury led the rejection of the messengers. Many of the weak followed their lead in rejecting, as they followed them in other affairs. The answer here is therefore spoken by the affluent, showing who led the struggle and who went after them.

The surah emphasizes arrogance and being treated as weak, whereas the scene in al-Baqarah says, “when those who were followed will disown their followers” (2:166). The chosen description here exposes arrogance’s role in creating such dependence. The formulation “We never sent a warner to a town except that its affluent ones said” (34:34) reveals a pattern repeated in the missions of the prophets. People whose luxurious position depends on superiority fear that the message will alter their established influence and oblige them to justice, equal rights, zakah and the other duties Allah has imposed on wealth. They see people’s guidance as a threat to their position.

This description does not include every rich person. It concerns those whom blessing has driven into arrogance toward others. The Arabic form recalls someone who has been given luxury: the gift was bestowed, and Allah provided its abundance. Gratitude was due, but they made it a reason to rebel against the Giver. Luxury is also explained here through the image of swelling with blessing and using it to assert superiority, converging with the arrogance of the preceding scene. One proposed derivation connects the word with a protuberance in the upper lip. On that view, the image of swelling helps convey the wealthy person’s feeling of elevation. Some linguists, however, object to this derivation.

Because these people possess money and the means of reaching an audience, they can spend on distorting the message and spreading objections to it. Allah says, “The disbelievers spend their wealth to prevent [people] from the way of Allah. They will continue to spend it, then it will be a source of regret for them, then they will be defeated” (8:36). Heavy expenditure against truth does not prove a strong argument. They may spend their money and reap only regret. Their attacks on the Messenger do not remove Allah’s honor to him: “and raised high your fame for you[3]” (94:4).

The words “its affluent ones” indicate that every society has its influential people. The Arabic term translated as a town means a settled community, not only a village in a modern administrative classification. A similar meaning appears in “Thus We have placed in every city the most wicked ones to conspire in it” (6:123). They seek the centers of power: decision-making, markets people need, and interests on which their affairs depend. Examples include control of gold or iron markets, taking advantage of people unfamiliar with prices, or an influential person controlling the completion of people’s transactions.

In contrast, the words locating the message “to a town” draw attention to its entering a community’s life. A caller brings revelation to people where they live and deal with one another, instead of remaining distant from their needs. The phrase “what you have been sent with” reinforces this. The message accompanies the messengers in their call, confronting wrongs in markets, gatherings and the rest of life. Just as the plotting of night and day extends through society’s different groups, the call needs to reach those groups too.

Mentioning a “warner” suits people whom blessing has distracted and whose rejection has become severe. They need someone to awaken them to its consequences. After the repeated commands to speak in argument with the polytheists, their general answer is “We disbelieve in what you have been sent with.” They reject the whole message without offering an argument refuting its meaning. A similar arrogance appeared among Salih’s people: Those who were arrogant said, “As for us, we surely disbelieve in what you believe.” (7:76). It was as though the weak accepting the truth became, for the arrogant, a further reason to resist it.

Verse 35

They[20] said, “We have greater wealth and more children, and we will not be punished”.

The affluent did more than announce their disbelief. They put forward a misleading argument that would keep the weak under their authority: we have more wealth and children, so we will not be punished. Seeing leaders disown their followers in the Hereafter could shake a follower's confidence in them; the arrogant therefore need something to renew that attachment. Their strength depends on their supporters. Pharaoh exercised his violence through his soldiers; if they deserted him, the creature's weakness would be exposed. Allah's dominion, however, depends on no one's assistance. His alone is the answer when the call is made: “To whom does the dominion belong this Day[4]? To Allah, the One, the Subjugator!” (Ghāfir 40:16).

The affluent began their argument with something people could see—the abundance of their possessions—then built on it a conclusion that did not follow: abundant gifts must mean that they are more deserving, closer to Allah, and safe from punishment. This is the flaw. A premise may be true while the conclusion is false. Superiority in strength, industry, or wealth does not make someone's beliefs sounder than those of a person with less. Worldly progress does not answer the questions of Allah's oneness and the Hereafter. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, expressed the powerful influence of wealth in this regard: “Every Ummah has a trial, and the trial of my Ummah is wealth.” (Al-Tirmidhī 2336).

The Qur'an overturns this measure: As for man, when he is tested by his Lord by honoring him and bestowing blessings upon him, he says, “My Lord has [deservedly] honored me[5]!” Then: But when he is tested by restricting his provision, he says, “My Lord has [undeservedly] humiliated me!” Both assumptions are followed by “No indeed[6]!” (Al-Fajr 89:15–17). Receiving is a test, and restriction is a test. Yet the person seizes on being honored and loses sight of being tested, imagining that a blessing certifies his deservingness and that its loss humiliates him. If he remembered that blessing tests him, he would give thanks; if he remembered that hardship tests him, he would be patient. He would not make someone's outward circumstances a verdict on their standing before Allah.

Notice how ‘Ād answered their messengers' warning: “Who is more powerful than us?” (Fuṣṣilat 41:15). What does bodily strength have to do with the truth of their response to revelation? Similarly, when the verses were recited to the polytheists, they asked, “Which of the two parties is better in terms of dwellings and superior in social gatherings?” (Maryam 19:73). The answer came: How many generations We have destroyed before them who were superior in terms of wealth and outward appearance! Earlier people had finer furnishings and appearances, yet their adornment did not prevent their destruction. Qārūn, deluded by his wealth, met this end: “Then We caused the earth to swallow him and his house.” (Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81).

This measure may slip into a person's heart until he assumes that anyone afflicted must have committed some enormous sin. In the report about Ayyūb, peace be upon him, his affliction lasted eighteen years. People near and far withdrew, while two of his brothers continued visiting him morning and evening. One said that Ayyūb must have committed a sin unlike anyone else's, taking the duration of the affliction and the delay in its removal as his evidence. When Ayyūb heard this, he did not accept the assumption. He recalled his reverence for Allah: when he heard two men disputing and mentioning Allah, he would return and make expiation on their behalf, unwilling that Allah should be mentioned except truthfully (Ibn Ḥibbān 2898). The prolonged affliction did not prove what his companion imagined. Ayyūb's heart retained that profound reverence for his Lord's name.

Attachment to this measure may begin with small things. A child sees that money obtains what he wants, while its absence prevents some of his wishes. This association may grow until he imagines that all happiness lies in money. Unless this love is disciplined, it spreads to his assessment of actions and ideas: whatever earns money is good, while whatever costs him profit is rejected, even if it is one of Allah's rulings. He may defend interest or unlawful work because it brings him income. The Prophet described growing greed: “If Adam's son had a valley full of gold, he would like to have two valleys” (Al-Bukhārī 6439).

When money becomes the measure of religion, this description applies: There are some people who worship Allah on the edge [of faith][6]: if something good comes to them, they are content; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they turn back[7] (Al-Ḥajj 22:11). Ibn ‘Abbās explained this through a man who came to Madinah: if his wife bore a boy and his mares produced offspring, he called it a good religion; otherwise, he called it a bad religion (Al-Bukhārī 4742). He did not make acceptance of truth the foundation governing his life. He made religion depend on the benefit he expected from it.

This may extend to loving the wealthy for their wealth and despising the poor for their poverty, until a person becomes a slave of the dinar. The Prophet said, “Wretched is the slave of the dinar!” (Al-Bukhārī 2887, with similar wording). A person may even fight for money despite the obvious danger. In the report about the Euphrates uncovering a mountain of gold, people fight over it and ninety-nine out of every hundred are killed. Each thinks that perhaps he will be the one who survives (Muslim 2894). He accepts this risk of destruction in pursuit of money, yet may plead a minor difficulty when called to worship or to give for Allah. This reveals what has taken possession of his heart.

It is then unsurprising that an affluent leader resists a call threatening his leadership. The problem runs deeper than attachment to the shape of an idol: it is servitude to the position he has built. Allah says: Seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, prayer is burdensome except for the humble, (Al-Baqarah 2:45). One explanation takes this as a call to the leaders of the People of the Book to seek help through patience and prayer in abandoning what prevents them from following the Messenger. Patience here has also been explained as fasting, which trains the self to relinquish its desires. Practicing the abandonment of something permissible can help it give up what is forbidden. The lecturer also discusses the Arabic wording with reference to the surrender of leadership and superiority that submission would require of them. The published English wording “prayer is burdensome” expressly identifies prayer; that broader interpretation remains commentary on the Arabic.

Thus the first followers of messengers were generally among the weak. Noah's people said, “we do not see those who follow you except the lowest among us apparently.” (Hūd 11:27). This also appeared in Heraclius's questioning of Abū Sufyān about the Prophet's followers (Al-Bukhārī 7). Wealth does not prevent a person from believing, nor is he required to destroy his possessions. What obstructs him is allowing wealth and leadership to govern whether he accepts truth.

The affluent made two claims: they had received abundant gifts, and they believed themselves safe from punishment in the Hereafter. An argument like theirs can lead people astray and keep them under the sway of those who uphold falsehood. Its flaws therefore need explaining, even though it may be right to walk away from a quarrel pursued for its own sake. The answer begins with the source of provision, then explains what determines nearness to God and recompense, and finally shows what comes of using wealth to obey Him or to turn people away from revelation.

Verse 36

Say, “My Lord extends provision to whom He wills or restricts it, but most people do not know”.

The first correction to the affluent people's argument is that their wealth is provision Allah has enlarged, not a power independent of His will. The Messenger says “My Lord”: the Lord whom I worship and submit to is the One who gave you these things, and He can withhold what you boast of. Abundant gifts do not mean an entitlement to salvation. Allah may expand provision as a test, then take it away in a moment.

A particularly immediate disclosure of human need is: Say, “What do you think, if your water were to sink deep into the earth, who can bring you flowing water?” (Al-Mulk 67:30). If the water sustaining life sank away, wealth could not replace Allah's power to bring it forth. The believer told the owner of the two gardens: it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden, and send upon your garden a thunderbolt from the heaven, turning it into a barren land. Then: Or its water may sink, so you will never be able to get it back.” (Al-Kahf 18:40–41). This does not deny the work and effort of those who possess wealth. They worked and pursued the means, but Allah created those means and enabled their results. His gifts cannot reasonably be made an argument for disobeying His command.

Ease may gradually draw someone who has chosen rejection toward destruction: But as for the one who is miserly and deems himself self-sufficient, and denies the best reward, We will make easy for him the way of perdition. (Al-Layl 92:8–10). The verse also alerts the believer that worldly causes do not cease to operate simply because beliefs differ. Whoever places a sound seed in suitable soil and waters it has taken a means of germination, whether Muslim or non-Muslim. A believer should not sit idle expecting the fruit of work, then be surprised that someone else obtains it through effort.

Nevertheless, causes offer no guarantee independent of Allah's will. Two people doing similar work need not receive equal provision: “Whoever desires this fleeting life[16], We hasten therein whatever We will to whoever We please.” (Al-Isrā’ 17:18). The gift depends on what Allah wills and whom He chooses. Taking the means therefore operates together with His will and wisdom; neither is understood in isolation from the other.

The believer is accordingly commanded to strive and prepare: “Prepare against them whatever force[35] you can” (Al-Anfāl 8:60). He is reminded that his opponent also endures hardship: “If you are suffering harm, they too are suffering harm just like you; but you hope from Allah what they do not hope.” (Al-Nisā’ 4:104). Effort is required, provision comes from Allah, and differences in provision do not certify the soundness of belief. Most people, however, stop at the wealth or poverty they see without understanding the reality of the test. The recipient becomes deluded by his gifts, while the deprived person grieves as though a smaller share proved his low standing.

Verse 37

It is not your wealth or your children that bring you closer to Us; but those who believe and do righteous deeds, it is they who will have a multiple reward for what they did, and they will be safe in the lofty mansions.

After explaining that abundant wealth comes from Allah's expansion of provision, the passage overturns the claims of nearness and salvation built on it. Wealth and children do not bring their owners near to Allah merely by existing or being numerous. “bring you closer to Us” directs attention to the goal a person should seek through everything he owns: nearness to his Lord, rather than simply surpassing others in worldly possessions.

Angels, prophets, and righteous people seek the closest stations to Him. Those whom the polytheists worship besides Allah themselves seek His pleasure and nearness: “Those whom they call upon seek means of nearness to their Lord, each trying to become closer” (Al-Isrā’ 17:57). They have been explained as angels; another explanation identifies jinn who accepted Islam while their people continued worshipping them. Either way, a worshipper cannot make worship of another needy servant like himself his path to Allah. Nor can he make wealth a means of nearness except in the way Allah loves.

If material superiority proved a religion true, the polytheists' victory at Uḥud would have been an argument for them. It was not. Defeat may befall those who hold the truth through wisdom and testing, while abundance may draw the people of falsehood gradually toward destruction. The exception then opens the door to action: “but those who believe and do righteous deeds”. The wealthy are not barred from nearness. Genuine faith and the use of their gifts in righteous action bring them close. Actions suited to this context include spending wealth on good and educating children, raising them in obedience and service to the religion, so that blessings do not remain mere material for boasting.

The Prophet explained that those with much wealth will have little on the Day of Resurrection, except those who give to their front, right, left, and back (Al-Bukhārī 6443). The many directions suggest the many avenues of giving, from meeting the needs of the poor to supporting the pursuit of knowledge and the religion. A person should not simply accumulate wealth and assume that owning it proves his honor. Hence: “it is they who will have a multiple reward for what they did”. Wealth used for good opens many doors to good deeds.

The poor Companions complained that the wealthy had surpassed them through acts they could not afford. The Prophet taught them to glorify Allah, praise Him, and declare His greatness. When the wealthy did the same, he said, “This is Allah's Grace which He gives to whom He wishes.” (Muslim 595). Differences in the ability to give are no occasion to object to Allah; they are a favor to be employed in His obedience.

Safety in the lofty dwellings is a fitting reward for their attachment to their Lord. Someone who relies on wealth alone fears losing the source of his security by spending it. Someone who trusts Allah's promise gains hope and reassurance through giving, knowing that what he has sent ahead will not be lost. The address thus refutes the polytheists' argument and steadies the believer who might be affected by their boasting. Having little should not deprive him of trust in Allah, and having much should not prevent him from giving.

Verse 38

But those who strive against Our verses[21], seeking to discredit them, it is they who will be summoned to the punishment.

A person cannot consistently claim that his wealth brings him near to Allah while spending it to obstruct His verses. This verse therefore stands opposite the description of those who believe and act righteously. It establishes the measure by which wealth's effect is known: what does its owner use it for? The former made it a path to obedience and received multiplied reward and safety; the latter used it to fight guidance and will be brought to punishment. Wealth is neither an absolute good nor an absolute evil. Its effect emerges through its use.

The Prophet's report provides a similar key: “Strange are the ways of a believer for there is good in every affair of his”. If something pleasant happens, he gives thanks, and that is good for him; if hardship strikes, he is patient, and that is good for him (Muslim 2999). A blessing's occurrence alone does not establish that it is good for its recipient: does he respond with gratitude or ingratitude? Nor must hardship be bad for him if he receives it patiently.

Even deliverance may itself be a test. One explanation of “That was a great trial from your Lord.” (Al-A‘rāf 7:141) includes Allah's rescue of the Children of Israel from Pharaoh, testing how they would act after the blessing. Having crossed the sea, they saw people devoted to idols and said, “O Moses, make for us a god just as they have gods.” (Al-A‘rāf 7:138). Deliverance did not prevent them from responding badly to their Benefactor. They had already been told: “It may be that your Lord will destroy your enemy and make you successors in the land to see how you will do.” (Al-A‘rāf 7:129). What matters is how a person acts after receiving gifts and power.

Here the Arabic present tense underlying “strive” portrays renewed effort: whenever they acquire money or influence, they employ it to obstruct. This continues their plotting night and day, and corresponds to the earlier description: “But those who strive against Our verses[3], seeking to discredit them” (Saba 34:5). “against Our verses[21]” draws attention to their targeting the details and rulings of revelation. Their objection is not confined to religion as a general label; it extends from Allah's oneness to acts of worship and prophetic practices.

The Arabic word mu‘ājizīn [seeking to discredit them] conveys their imagined ability to elude Allah or prevent His command from taking effect. A reading without the alif and with a doubled jīm, مُعَجِّزين, is also transmitted (Ibn al-Jazarī, Al-Nashr fī al-Qirā’āt al-‘Ashr, the readings of Sūrat al-Ḥajj). It has been explained as attempting to discourage believers or make the call appear powerless. Their imagined escape is answered by “summoned to the punishment”: they will be seized and brought to it, beyond the help of their devices and numerous supporters. The jinn acknowledged: We have realized that we can never escape Allah on earth, nor can we ever escape Him by fleeing. (Al-Jinn 72:12). Allah also says: Do the evildoers think that they can escape Us? How evil is their judgment! (Al-‘Ankabūt 29:4). Someone who spends Allah's gifts resisting His verses has not made blessing an argument for his salvation. He has made its misuse a cause of his misery.

Verse 39

Say, “My Lord extends provision to whom He wills of His slaves or restricts it. Whatever you spend in charity, He will compensate for it, for He is the Best of Providers.”

The command to proclaim that provision lies in Allah's hands returns. Repeating it after the explanation of recompense adds clarification. One understanding is that the first verse confronted the polytheists' delusion over abundant gifts, while this address turns toward believers, steadying them and preparing them to give. This suits the addition “of His slaves”, honoring servitude to Him, and an added Arabic pronoun referring to the person whose provision is restricted. The selected English clause is “or restricts it”; its “it” refers to provision, so it is not an English personal-pronoun counterpart. The believer does not view expansion and restriction as someone cut off from his Lord would. He remembers His care in both. Abundance may assist obedience; restriction may be a mercy protecting him from temptation or raising him through patience. One reflection on the added Arabic pronoun is that restriction may be for his sake and benefit, even if he dislikes it at first.

The damage to the ship appeared harmful before its protective purpose emerged. The account of the boy likewise disclosed mercy toward his parents. Al-Khiḍr said of the ship: “I wanted to make it defective because there was a king ahead of them who seized every [good] ship by force.” Of the boy: “his parents were believers, and we feared that he would overburden them with his rebellion and disbelief[38].” (Al-Kahf 18:79–80). What initially saddens a believer need not be devoid of mercy toward him. In the sacred report, Allah describes His servant drawing near through voluntary acts until He loves him, then promises to give when he asks and protect him when he seeks refuge. Yet the death the believer dislikes still comes. The report describes the believer's dislike of death and Allah's dislike of causing him distress (Al-Bukhārī 6502). Care does not mean preventing everything he dislikes; he trusts his Lord in both giving and withholding.

Another understanding distinguishes between provision varying from person to person in the first verse and one person's changing circumstances in the second. Someone whose provision was expanded may later experience restriction, and someone in straitened circumstances may receive abundance. On this reading, the repetition warns the polytheist against being deceived by a condition he cannot preserve, and urges the believer to give while able, before a time when he wishes to give but cannot.

Both understandings meet in freeing the heart from servitude to wealth. The affluent person's ambition is to say, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers.” (Al-Kahf 18:34), or to become consumed by competition for more. The believer asks how to make his possessions a means of nearness to Allah. This extends to ordinary spending on his family when he seeks reward for it: the Prophet counted it as charity (Al-Bukhārī 5351). He also explained that spending in pursuit of Allah's pleasure is rewarded, even the morsel a man puts into his wife's mouth (Al-Bukhārī 56). Giving should not be narrowed to a large sum or a single avenue of good.

The general wording “Whatever you spend in charity” includes the small amount a person can afford. Some poor believers exerted themselves to work and earn something to give, yet still suffered the hypocrites' mockery. Of them the Qur'an says: Those who disparage the believers who give charity voluntarily and ridicule those who find nothing to give but their own toil[63]. Allah will ridicule them, and for them there will be a painful punishment. (Al-Tawbah 9:79). What people regard as little may be great because of the sincerity and effort behind it.

The promise is then assigned to Allah: “He will compensate for it”, reassuring the person who fears loss. Compensation need not be of the same kind as the gift or appear as a larger sum of money. It may take the form of blessing, health, protection from affliction, or a higher standing with Allah. What is required is trust in His promise, rather than demanding a form of compensation chosen by the servant. Someone who stops at outward arithmetic may think that charity reduces wealth while interest increases it. Yet Allah says: Allah destroys usury and nourishes charities, and Allah does not like any ungrateful sinner. (Al-Baqarah 2:276). The growth of charity and destruction of interest are realities that cannot be grasped merely by looking at an immediate figure. This reveals the limitation of those described as follows: They only know the apparent life of this world, but they are heedless of the Hereafter. (Al-Rūm 30:7). They know apparent increase and decrease, overlooking what follows their deeds with Allah.

“He is the Best of Providers” invites reassurance in the wisdom of the One promising compensation. A child who trusts a truthful father's promise of a good gift feels reassured before knowing its details—and to Allah belongs the highest example. How much more so with the promise of the Lord who knows the need and gives well? Giving has greater worth when supporters are few and need is intense: “Those of you who spent and fought before the conquest [of Makkah and those who did not] are not equal. They are much greater in rank than those who spent and fought afterward[9].” (Al-Ḥadīd 57:10). This meaning also appears in Waraqah ibn Nawfal's wish to be young and strong when the Prophet's people drove him out, so that he could support him (Al-Bukhārī 3). Revelation thus transforms wealth from material for boasting into a means of servitude to Allah. It then returns the reader to the gathering, to see the end of what people clung to and the deeds that remain for them.

Verse 40

On the Day when He gathers them all together, then He will say to the angels, “Was it you that they used to worship?”

The passage moves from people's delusion over their possessions to the Day when all are gathered. Neither the arrogant leader nor the weak follower will be absent. The earlier scene disclosed leaders disowning their followers; this one discloses the disavowal of those whom the polytheists worshipped in hope of mediation and intercession. A worshipper cannot escape by transferring responsibility to a master he obeyed or a creature he took besides Allah.

Allah asks the angels, in these people's hearing, whether they used to worship them. The question seeks no knowledge hidden from Him, exalted is He. It exposes the truth and establishes the case, so that the polytheists hear those whom they claimed were close to Allah reject what they did. It resembles His question to Jesus, peace be upon him: “did you tell people, ‘Take me and my mother as gods besides Allah?’” and his answer: “Glory be to You! It is not for me to say what I have no right.” (Al-Mā’idah 5:116).

Such transitions keep the sūrah's argument connected to the Hereafter. This is not a contest won in debate and over when the gathering disperses. It is a choice whose consequences each person will meet when Allah gathers him with those he followed and worshipped.

Verse 41

They will say, “Glory be to You! You are our Protector, not they. Rather they used to worship the jinn[22]; most of them believed in them.”

The angels begin by declaring Allah's perfection and affirming that their allegiance belongs to Him alone: “You are our Protector, not they.” They neither approve the polytheists' worship nor take them as allies. Instead, they proclaim their need of their Lord and servitude to Him. A subtle feature of their answer is that they do not attribute worship to themselves even by denying it. Rather than saying that these people did not worship them, they first declare Allah's perfection and affirm His protection, then explain the people's actual deviation.

“Rather they used to worship the jinn[22]” has two explanations. According to one, they actually worshipped jinn, then, on seeing the angels' honored position and the fate of the jinn whom the polytheists had obeyed, claimed to have worshipped angels. The angels expose that claim as a lie. According to another, the jinn made worship of angels and idols attractive and urged them toward it. Following and obeying them in associating partners with Allah was therefore, in reality, worship of the jinn, whatever other names they gave their objects of worship.

Some polytheists gave idols feminine names; some imagined that angels inhabited them, making their worship a path to angelic mediation with Allah. Behind this lay the pursuit of worldly benefits—a hoped-for child or victory—or the assumption that an object of worship would intercede and relieve them of the demands of obedience and repentance.

One reflection here concerns the way a sense of weakness fosters dependence. A person may seek a power to support him against fear: the weak attach themselves to the arrogant, who attach themselves to wealth and supporters; another seeks refuge in jinn, as people entering a valley used to seek protection from its chief among the jinn. All this exposes the creature's need, however strong he appears. The same meaning can appear in someone who imitates the dominant merely through fascination and a sense of defeat, adopting things he would not find good if he considered them independently.

The angels, despite the abilities Allah has granted them, say, “You are our Protector”, showing the proper place of refuge. The sūrah brings a heart scattered among these powers back to a strong connection with its Lord and His revelation, seeking His protection and guidance instead of making its need for strength a doorway to worship of others.

Verse 42

So Today none of you has the power to benefit or harm one another. And We will say to the wrongdoers, “Taste the punishment of the Fire which you used to deny!”

The relationships that deceived them collapse that Day. No master benefits a follower; no object of worship can ward off punishment from its worshipper; no one can independently harm another as they imagined. After the angels are questioned and declare their disavowal, those who did wrong are told to taste the punishment of the Fire they denied.

This ending exposes what lay behind their devices. For these people, worship of intermediaries was a way of evading acknowledgment of an accounting that required submission. A person wanted to satisfy his desires, then ask an object of worship or an ally taken besides Allah to release him from the consequences of his actions, instead of repenting to his Lord and following His command. When hope in those objects of worship fails, the reality he tried to evade remains: a Fire that denial and imagined mediation cannot avert. The problem is more than the intercessors' inability to serve him. The very path he chose was wrongdoing and denial, now disclosed when no opportunity to repair it remains.

Verse 43

When Our clear verses are recited to them, they say, “This is nothing but a man who wishes to turn you away from what your forefathers used to worship.” And they say, “This is nothing but a fabricated lie.” And the disbelievers say about the Truth when it comes to them, “This is nothing but clear magic,”

After the sūrah has shown Allah's dominion and knowledge, what He subjected to David and Solomon, His removal of Saba's blessings, the dialogue between followers and leaders, and the futility of being deluded by wealth, the verses come as “clear verses”. Truth has become manifest, yet the obstinate answer with three lines of attack. The first targets the Messenger himself: a man seeking to turn them away from their forefathers' religion. The second targets what he brings: a fabricated lie. The third targets its evident effect: clear magic.

The repeated “they say” separates the claims and highlights the attention given to each. Their objection is no passing remark: they attack the person calling them, then his teaching, then its effect on hearts. The word “wishes” accuses his intention. They claim that he seeks leadership that would displace their forefathers' leadership. Someone consumed by self-interest may struggle to understand an action done for Allah's sake, searching behind every call for money or influence.

They then stir loyalty to inherited custom: “what your forefathers used to worship.” This diverts the listener from considering the evidence to fearing betrayal of the group and its ancestors. Such loyalty had manifest power, until the command came: “remember Allah as you used to remember your forefathers[99], or even with greater remembrance.” (Al-Baqarah 2:200). A similar tactic accuses someone speaking truth of opposing the homeland or people's traditions, alienating listeners before they hear his explanation.

The intensifying accusations suggest the influential leaders' fear that revelation will affect the weak and free them from their control. Someone whose followers begin leaving may exaggerate his attacks to regain their confidence. He may devote an independent statement or an entire presentation to maligning each aspect of the call. The multiplicity of attacks therefore serves a purpose.

They call the Qur'an a “fabricated lie”, combining an accusation of falsehood with the claim that it was invented. They move between charges: sometimes he supposedly composed it himself, sometimes he sought a teacher's assistance: “This [Qur’an] is nothing but a lie which he has fabricated, with the help of other people.” (Al-Furqān 25:4). They seek no description established by evidence, only something to turn listeners away, however their claims shift and contradict one another. All three statements use restrictive Arabic constructions, presenting falsehood as a certainty admitting no other possibility.

Yet the problem of its effect remains. Why would someone leave wealth, family, and standing, enduring harm for this Qur'an, if it were as they claimed? Why are hearts drawn together to hear it? Abū Bakr's story gives a clear example. He prayed and recited in his courtyard, often weeping. Polytheist women and children gathered, amazed at his recitation. This alarmed Quraysh's nobles, who demanded that he not recite publicly where people would be affected (Al-Bukhārī 3905). No speech directed at them was required: the recitation itself troubled them. They therefore called the truth's effect magic, offering an explanation intended to block any response to it.

There are two understandings of the explicit description “the disbelievers” here. It may emphasize their covering up truth after its arrival and disclosure, setting disbelief against revelation's clarity. Alternatively, it may refer to the leaders of disbelief telling the weak that this is obvious magic, seeking to detach them from the call's influence. “clear magic” adds to their show of confidence, as though its falsity were too obvious for discussion, although it is the verses that are clear. This way of resisting messengers recurs: Did they suggest this to one another[17]? Rather they are transgressing people. (Al-Dhāriyāt 51:53). Similar transgression produces similar speech; methods of obstruction resemble one another even when their presentation changes.

Verse 44

even though We did not give them any scriptures to study, nor did We send them any warner before you.

On what basis did they assert that this revelation was fabricated? They had been given no scriptures to study in which they could find it contradicting revelation they knew. Nor had a warner come to them before him whose teaching they could invoke to reject his message. This understanding strips their claim of the appearance of knowledge. They reject the Book through desire and imitation, not an informed comparison.

The books that came from Allah call to His oneness and the removal of injustice. Thus fair-minded people among the People of the Book recognized the source of this light on hearing the Qur'an. The Negus recognized it, declaring that what he heard from the Qur'an and what Moses brought emerged from a single niche of light. Their agreement pointed him to the common source of revelation. ‘Abdullāh ibn Salām accepted Islam. Heraclius recognized prophetic characteristics that disclosed the Prophet's truthfulness, although he did not follow him (Aḥmad 22498; Al-Bukhārī 3329, 7). Knowledge grounded in consideration of revelation is not equivalent to claims repeated by a group without knowledge.

Another understanding sees the verse as a reminder of an honor they should have welcomed. A Book to study and a Messenger to warn them had come after they had possessed neither. They should have embraced this knowledge instead of casting it aside. Being unlettered in contrast to the People of the Book, and the need some Arabs felt for information about the unseen, led them to ask others about creation, death, and what lies beyond sense perception. Many accounts reached them in this way. Once revelation clarifying truth had arrived, what deprivation it would be to abandon Allah's honor and remain captive to imitation of their forefathers. Both understandings invalidate the charge of fabrication: they had no basis for it, and making it squandered their opportunity for guidance and knowledge.

Verse 45

Those who came before them also rejected [the truth], while these [Makkans] have not been given even one-tenth of what We had given those [earlier nations], yet they rejected My messengers; how severe was My response!

Quraysh are not the first to reject, and their strength cannot protect them from what befell earlier peoples. The passage therefore returns them to the history of deniers. There are two explanations of the pronouns in “have not been given even one-tenth of what We had given”. According to one, Quraysh had not attained a tenth of earlier peoples' strength and power. Many nations had larger bodies, greater dominion, cultivated the earth, and carved through mountains, yet their strength availed them nothing when they rejected the messengers. The One who destroyed them cannot be thwarted by the weaker Quraysh.

According to the other explanation, those earlier peoples had not received a tenth of the clarity of the signs given to these people. The Qur'an came as an enduring, clear proof. How great was the revelation given to the Arabs, addressing them in their own language and establishing the evidence against them. Explaining this sign's standing, the Prophet said that every prophet had been given signs through which people believed, while what he had been given was revelation from Allah; he therefore hoped to have the most followers on the Day of Resurrection (Al-Bukhārī 4981). On this understanding the warning is stronger: if Allah seized those who rejected messengers despite the evidence they received, what of those who turn away after these tremendous clear signs? Greater clarity increases the responsibility of those who deliberately reject it.

‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr said that three groups suffer the severest punishment: the hypocrites, those among the people of the table spread who disbelieved, and Pharaoh's people (Al-Ṭabarī, Jāmi‘ al-Bayān, 9:132). The lesson appears in Pharaoh's case: We showed Pharaoh all Our signs, but he rejected them and refused to believe. (Ṭā Hā 20:56). It also appears in the warning accompanying the table spread: “but if anyone among you disbelieves afterward, I will subject him to a punishment such as I have never inflicted upon anyone in the worlds.” (Al-Mā’idah 5:115). Likewise, someone who hears revelation and knows the Messenger's guidance, then conceals disbelief while displaying its opposite, cannot make proximity to the places of clarification an excuse.

The verse ends by asking, in the lecturer's explanation, how His denunciation of them proved to be. The Arabic form nakīr [My response!] omits the possessive yā’, indicated by the kasrah beneath the final rā’. Allah's denunciation is not like a human objection that can do no more than protest. It appears through punishment that shatters the strength of those who turn away, leaving their former sources of delusion useless. History here warns those repeating the deniers' path; it is not an account detached from the present.

Verse 46

Say [O Prophet], “I only advise you to do one thing: stand up for Allah, in pairs and individually, then reflect. There is no madness in your companion[23]; he is but a warner to you ahead of a severe punishment.”

After extended argument and varied explanations, a single exhortation gathers the path toward accepting truth: rise for Allah, in pairs or individually, then reflect. The language of exhortation preserves the stance of compassionate advice. Despite their rejection, the Messenger still wants their salvation. He leaves them something clear to take away and consider for themselves.

This continues the fairness of the earlier address: “And surely either we or you[18] are rightly guided or clearly misguided.” (Saba 34:24). Displays of power and repeatedly heard distortions may overwhelm a person. He needs an address opening the door to consideration and drawing him out of the noise obscuring the evidence. The first step is to rise: to begin a serious search, rather than surrender to confusion or merely repeat the group's words. Someone may hear conflicting opinions, tire of people's disagreements, and say that he no longer trusts anyone. That weariness does not release him from seeking guidance; his own destiny before Allah is at stake.

Searching does not mean selecting the views that suit his desires or secure profit and comfort, then calling this a search for truth. Hence the qualification that it be for Allah: His pleasure is the aim, rather than conformity, social standing, or satisfying someone whose blame he fears. Salmān's journey exemplifies such seeking. He left the familiar to discover the religion, traveling in search of those who could guide him through Syria, Mosul, Nisibis, and Amorium. He followed the knowledge he found until his search brought him to Arabia, then to the Prophet in Madinah (Aḥmad 23737). Sincere desire does not remain an idle wish; it moves a person to act and bear the cost.

Meeting in pairs or reflecting alone provides an atmosphere conducive to thought. A single shouting person can spoil a noisy gathering, turning evidence into a surging argument in which no one listens. Someone may fear being the first to challenge the prevailing opinion, rejecting publicly what he would accept if he considered it alone. For example, a gathering may rush to ridicule an unfamiliar proposal, such as educating a child at home, before examining its feasibility and what suits each situation. The lesson lies in judgment being issued under group influence before the proposal itself is weighed.

Fear of blame can become more severe. Some people in pre-Islamic ignorance responded to imagined disgrace even by burying their daughters alive, allowing society's judgment to overpower compassion and love. The same underlying influence appears in a young man advised to pray among his friends. He resists because his heart is occupied with what they will say and the companionship he may lose. When a loving adviser speaks to him alone, he listens, softens, and accepts. Solitude, or a conversation between two sincere, sensible seekers of guidance, reduces the influence that had prevented him from hearing.

This affliction is not confined to those distant from religion. A student of knowledge may devote himself to a discipline that raises him in his group's eyes, neglecting what he or the people he serves need more, because he cannot bear to rank below his peers. Rising for Allah examines intention in both situations and returns the question to what pleases Him.

After sincere seeking and the quieting of outside pressures comes reflection on their companion. They have known this man for years, knowing his truthfulness, trustworthiness, and sound judgment. He is no untested stranger. People still had deposits entrusted to him at the time of his migration (Al-Bayhaqī 12823), and they accepted his arbitration when they disagreed over placing the Black Stone (Al-Ṭayālisī 115). How does this knowledge accord with calling him mad? Why would he endure harm and danger, leaving his former ease, to call them to something for which he asks no money? He also brings a Qur'an challenging them to produce its like, and they find nothing to overturn its proof.

Al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah's report shows the Qur'an having an evident effect that was then rejected under social pressure. The Prophet recited to him, and his heart softened. When Abū Jahl heard, he pressured him to make a statement pleasing to his people. Al-Walīd acknowledged the distinction of what he had heard, describing its sweetness and beauty, yet eventually called it magic (Al-Ḥākim, Al-Mustadrak, 2:550). The problem was not that his heart felt no effect, but that concern for his people's judgment returned.

The exhortation ends with the Messenger's true function: “he is but a warner to you ahead of a severe punishment.” He is neither mad nor seeking benefit from them. He warns of an approaching consequence that should awaken reflection, so that dispersing from the gathering does not end their consideration of what they heard.

Verse 47

Say, “If I had ever asked you for a reward, it is yours. My reward is only with Allah, and He is Witness over all things.”

The denial of any demand for payment makes the grounds for reflection clearer. The one warning them does not turn his call into a source of their money or seek to enjoy the fruit of their giving. One explanation of the statement about any requested reward being theirs is that he has asked for none at all; even supposing he had asked for something, it would be returned to them. This accommodating form of address removes the suspicion of private interest.

Another explanation concerns the zakāh, charity, and spending to which he calls them: their benefit returns to the givers, rather than serving as payment he enjoys. A further explanation takes the meaning more broadly: the benefit of the call and Qur'an belongs to them, for they are guided and saved by following it, while he seeks recompense for conveying it only from Allah. These explanations converge on the sincerity of the message: their acceptance does not supply some benefit he pursues at their expense.

It closes with “He is Witness over all things.” Allah knows what the Messenger says and intends. No truth or lie is hidden from Him, and He could expose a claim contrary to reality if the Messenger were fabricating against Him. His testimony to His Messenger includes the support He manifests, the elevation of his renown, and His protection until he conveys the message. Considering their companion's character, the absence of the payment they accuse him of seeking, and Allah's support leaves them to confront the evidence itself, rather than evade it through an unproved allegation about his intention.

Verse 48

Say, “My Lord hurls the Truth [against falsehood], and He is the All-Knower of the unseen.”

The truth the Lord hurls is the revelation brought by the Messenger, the foundation for confronting falsehood and establishing proof against it. Its strength is not measured by the weakness that may befall its bearer or a debater's lack of eloquence. A speaker may be weak while the Qur'an's proof remains standing. People's fortunes in battle and practical action may change without a false belief becoming true. We must distinguish the clarity of the proof from the victories or defeats of its adherents. Material strength cannot replace evidence, and a lack of that strength cannot invalidate it.

“hurls the Truth” depicts revelation's force in striking at falsehood's foundation and removing it: “Rather, We hurl the truth at falsehood, and it crushes it[10], so it vanishes.” (Al-Anbiyā’ 21:18). The Arabic image is of a blow to the head, leaving falsehood unable to stand on its proof. The Qur'an is not a collection of verbal subtleties admired by listeners, general ethics detached from Allah's oneness, or habits practiced without regard to the truth it establishes and the association of partners it overturns. It addresses the heart of the conflict between guidance and error. This is an ancient enmity, an aspect of which appears in the account of Iblīs circling Adam after his creation (Muslim 2611). Revelation came to make humanity aware of its enemy and the path of escape from his misguidance.

Evidence may become clear and still be denied by someone governed by something other than the search for truth, as with al-Walīd, who recognized the Qur'an's effect before turning to a statement that would please his people. Hearing this proof is so significant that Allah commanded protection for a polytheist seeking refuge until he hears it, followed by safe escort: “If any of the polytheists asks you for protection, give it to him, so that he may hear the Word of Allah[9], then escort him to his place of safety” (Al-Tawbah 9:6). He is to have the opportunity to know truth, without fear preventing him from hearing revelation, so that his choice rests on evidence: “so that those who chose to perish would perish after seeing clear evidence, and those who chose to survive would survive after seeing clear evidence.” (Al-Anfāl 8:42).

“My Lord” recalls the care given to the Messenger carrying this proof. His steadfastness does not rest on a strength independent of his Lord's care. The ending clarifies further: the One sending truth is “the All-Knower of the unseen”, in contrast to the deniers later described as “making far-fetched conjectures about the unseen[27]” (Saba 34:53). The images contrast the one hurling, what is hurled, and the extent of knowledge behind it. Here the Lord sends truth with complete knowledge; there human beings launch claims without evidence, possessing no book they study as their basis. They resemble a marksman far from the target, shutting his eyes and firing. Distance and lack of sight together expose his claim's unsoundness, however confidently he speaks.

This difference affects the conceptions directing people's lives, including ideas about politics, money, and human relationships: socialism, capitalism, liberalism, and secularism when presented as comprehensive accounts of life. A human conception may be right on a particular question or agree with a sound ruling without its overall foundations constituting guidance. Nor should every particular accepted by someone opposing revelation be rejected. We examine the foundations that determine humanity's purpose, object of worship, and measure of judgment, referring particulars to established truth.

Guidance frees the heart from servitude to creatures, introduces it to its Lord and destiny, and gives life a direction beyond immediate benefit. The All-Knower of the unseen knows the consequences of acting on revelation or abandoning it, and what people will need in their future circumstances. His guidance is not equivalent to a human experiment whose limitations may emerge only after people suffer its consequences. Looking at people's present state, someone may find it implausible that this revelation can benefit them until the Hour, or that the weak people among whom the call appeared in Makkah could carry this religion to the world. What he dismisses through limited knowledge is not hidden from the Lord who sent it and knows its outcomes.

A person may trust his familiar, limited knowledge, then doubt the guidance's suitability for circumstances he has not yet witnessed. He needs to remember the vast knowledge of its Sender. Hidden and manifest are alike in Allah's knowledge: It is the same [to Him] whether one of you speaks secretly or in public, and whether one is hidden in the darkness of the night or walks about openly in the daylight. (Al-Ra‘d 13:10). His knowledge cannot be measured against His creatures': “There is nothing like unto Him[8], and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing.” (Al-Shūrā 42:11). Trust in revelation comes from knowing the Lord who sent it and the comprehensiveness of His knowledge, rather than imagining that humanity has comprehended every aspect of its wisdom.

Verse 49

Say, “The Truth has come, and falsehood can neither initiate nor restore anything[24].”

After explaining that the Lord hurls truth comes the announcement: “The Truth has come”. Its appearance exposes falsehood's inability: And say, “The truth has come and falsehood has perished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to perish[73].” (Al-Isrā’ 17:81). Those who hold truth do not wait for falsehood voluntarily to vacate its place before beginning to convey and act upon it. The Children of Israel's words illustrate the error of such waiting: “we will never enter it until they leave it.” (Al-Mā’idah 5:22). They made the inhabitants' departure a condition of their entry, as though they wanted to attain the goal after the very obstacle they were charged to confront had disappeared. Reform likewise requires bringing truth forth and upholding it, rather than merely waiting for falsehood to retreat.

“falsehood can neither initiate nor restore anything[24]” has distinct explanations. According to one, falsehood can achieve nothing against the proof that has arrived: its inability is exposed and its power to oppose the proof exhausted. According to another, it cannot originate creation or restore it after death. How can something possessing neither power be worshipped and made a rival to the Creator, exalted is He?

One reflection is that truth establishes a community and guides it to what gives it life, restoring the means of rising when it falls and returns to truth. Falsehood gives it no enduring guidance by which to order this world and the Hereafter, nor can it repair the corruption arising from departure from truth. This does not deny the existence of materially powerful states outside Islam. Establishing a state through force and wealth is one matter; guiding a community to a life whose purpose, ethics, and civilization are sound is broader.

Two considerations prevent outward non-Muslim strength from becoming a delusion. First, the justice, removal of oppression, and mutual sincere counsel found among some of them belong to meanings brought by the prophets. Not everything they adopt and gain strength through is false, even if they do not attribute it to revelation. Second, relative superiority does not certify the soundness of every aspect of the superior party's condition. Muslims' backwardness may become so severe that those above them appear to have attained perfection despite their moral and social defects.

Imagine two men at different levels underground. The lower sees the other as higher, but relative height does not mean he has reached the surface. The comparison may reveal how far the first has sunk without proving that the second has attained the goal. Imitating the outward form of strength therefore cannot replace consideration of truth's measure. Pride in revelation does not release a person from acting on it and taking the means of strength it commands.

Verse 50

Say, “If I go astray, the detriment of my straying is only upon myself; but if I am guided, it is because of what my Lord reveals to me. He is indeed All-Hearing, Ever Near.”

This sequence ends with the fairness in argument with which it began. The Messenger supposes, for the sake of addressing their allegation, that he were astray, explaining that its harm would return to himself. Error originates in the self when it abandons guidance. The conditional statement acknowledges no actual error on his part and expresses no doubt in the revelation he brings. It accommodates their argument to return them to the evidence and cut off the excuse of accusing his intention.

He then attributes guidance entirely to his Lord: “it is because of what my Lord reveals to me.” If the Messenger, the most truthful and soundest in judgment, explains that his guidance comes through this revelation, how can anyone below him dispense with it through reason, experience, or numerous supporters? What he brings is not the product of a device for gaining influence. It is guidance from the Lord who hears His servants' words and knows their need.

“All-Hearing, Ever Near” reassures the one conveying the message. Allah hears what he says and what is said to him. He knows his need and the need of those he calls to guidance. This confrontation is never beyond His knowledge and care.

Verse 51

If only you could see when they are stricken with terror, but there will be no escape, and they will be seized from a nearby place[25].

After five successive commands to say, opening doors of fairness and reflection to those turning away, comes a scene of terror, keeping the warning of consequences present: if only you could see them terrified and finding no escape. The timing of this terror has several explanations. It may refer to what struck them at Badr when they saw the Muslim forces and angels fighting alongside them. Another explanation places it at death, when they see the angels and what awaits them: “If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and their backs” (Al-Anfāl 8:50). A third identifies the terror of resurrection, when what they denied appears and the Fire is brought. The Prophet reported that Hell will be brought that Day with seventy thousand reins, each pulled by seventy thousand angels (Muslim 2842).

Each explanation discloses a moment when the boldness of denial turns into fear and a person tries to flee what he sees. The answer is “there will be no escape”. They cannot outstrip Allah's power or thwart Him by flight. Being “seized from a nearby place[25]” overturns the notion that a location could protect them from Him. Wherever they are, seizure reaches them, whether the scene is understood as worldly punishment, death, or resurrection. Nearness here conveys the encompassing reach of His power and the ease of taking them, after they imagined their strength placed them beyond punishment.

Verse 52

They will say, “Now we believe in it,” but how could they attain faith from such a distant place[26],

Then they say, “Now we believe in it”. The verses had been presented, Allah's dominion and knowledge explained, and the invitation to consider His Messenger's truthfulness offered, but they turned away. Now acknowledgment comes with terror, after faith had been offered when they could choose and act.

The Arabic tanāwush [attain faith] means reaching to take something. Its underlying image is of extending one's hand to something close enough to grasp; the related word munāwashah likewise conveys engagement at close range. Now, however, they seek to grasp what has become remote: faith that benefits its holder, after the opportunity for its acceptance has passed. The question exposes the impossibility of attaining it at that time. It was at hand and they refused it; then they wished to return when the time for making amends was over.

The distance is more than ground to be crossed on foot. It is the distance between two conditions: faith freely chosen when revelation calls, and acknowledgment forced by seeing what can no longer be resisted. The image of a hand stretching out but failing to reach conveys their grief over an opportunity once close, squandered, and sought again too late.

Verse 53

while they disbelieved in it before, making far-fetched conjectures about the unseen[27]?

Their belated request does not follow an unawareness in which clarification never reached them. They disbelieved earlier while it was offered to them, then went beyond rejection to assail truth and its people with claims of which they had no knowledge. They called revelation falsehood and magic, attacked their companion, and delivered their judgments as though they knew what lay behind them, while remaining far from evidence.

“making far-fetched conjectures about the unseen[27]” renews the contrast with: “My Lord hurls the Truth [against falsehood], and He is the All-Knower of the unseen.” (Saba 34:48). The Sender of truth knows the unseen; they throw conjectures at what they do not know. Their inability to reach faith is no injustice to them. They distanced themselves when its path was near and chose to turn others from it, until what they had confidently rejected became something they saw, unable to return to the time when they could choose it.

Verse 54

A barrier will be placed between them and what they desire[28], as was done with the likes of them before, for they were indeed in a disquieting doubt.

The sūrah ends with a barrier disclosing the fate of their attachments: “A barrier will be placed between them and what they desire[28]”. One explanation is that they are prevented from enjoying the wealth, children, and worldly pleasures they loved. The ending thus returns to their boast: “We have greater wealth and more children, and we will not be punished” (Saba 34:35). Their possessions fed their delusion, yet at death they can neither carry them away nor remain among them. Imagine someone attached to wealth reaching for it but being stopped, or hoping for further enjoyment of his children after his time among them has ended. This exposes the inability of what he made his source of strength and security.

Another explanation identifies the barrier as preventing the repentance and faith they desire after their time has passed. They regret every attack on religion and every stand against truth, but a belated wish cannot restore the time for action. Their punishment even reaches a point at which they are denied excuses: nor will they be allowed to make excuses. (Al-Mursalāt 77:36). They are told: Allah will say, “Stay therein despised and do not speak to Me[48]. (Al-Mu’minūn 23:108). Their grief concerns more than lost enjoyment: salvation had been near, and they abandoned it.

The same befell earlier deniers like them. Their counterparts are those who shared their path of disbelief and rejection; the recompense recurred because its cause recurred. The ending then describes a condition entrenched within them: “they were indeed in a disquieting doubt.” Their uncertainty did not move them to rise sincerely for Allah and seek evidence. Whenever revelation nearly awakened their hearts, they returned to fear for their standing and to hesitation, until their lives ended while they resisted what called them to salvation.

The ending thus connects with the exhortation to rise for Allah in pairs or individually. Sincere reflection helps a person escape the group's control; continually calculating its approval after truth is clear may imprison him until his final moment. Abū Ṭālib's death illustrates this. The Prophet invited him to declare that there is no deity but Allah, a statement with which he could plead for him before Allah. Abū Jahl and ‘Abdullāh ibn Abī Umayyah kept asking whether he would abandon ‘Abd al-Muṭṭalib's religion. His final words to them affirmed that he remained upon it (Al-Bukhārī 1360). Loyalty to inherited custom kept pressing him at a moment when the decision could bear no delay.

Al-Walīd's earlier story likewise showed him affected by the Qur'an before returning to calculations about his people's position and what would satisfy them. Of him it was said: For he reflected and plotted [against the Qur’an][6]. (Al-Muddaththir 74:18). The thinking condemned here is calculation used to repel a truth that had become clear, rather than the sincere inquiry commanded in verse 46. There is no contradiction between the invitation to reflect and the warning against hesitating to obey. A servant needs to struggle against what prevents him from following guidance and respond promptly once he recognizes it, before what he can choose today becomes something he desires tomorrow but is barred from attaining.

‘Umar’s wish is a Companion’s statement, not a saying attributed to the Prophet. In the assessment accompanying Faḍā’il al-Ṣaḥābah 1280, Shu‘ayb al-Arna’ūṭ describes its transmitters as reliable except Abū Ṣakhr, whose report is acceptable when corroborated. Commentary on verse 10

Al-Tirmidhī 3076 gives the account in which David’s sixty years become one hundred through Adam’s gift of forty. Al-Tirmidhī calls it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; Darussalam grades it ḥasan. A sentence in the separate published English explanation instead says that David’s lifespan was forty years and became sixty. This differs from the numbered narration’s sixty years plus forty.

Published explanation on Allah’s knowledge and the angels’ records:
Allah, may He be glorified, is All-Knowing of what happened, what will happen, and what did not happen if it happened how it would be. He knows what He wrote for him and what increase He would grant him later. The angels have no knowledge except what Allah informs them. Allah knows things before they happen and after they happen. For this reason, scholars said that erasing and affirming occur in the sheets of the angels; but knowledge of Allah, Glorified and Exalted, never changes, nor does He become aware of anything He does not know before. Therefore, there is no erasing or affirmation in His knowledge.

Commentary on verse 12

The published English verse 34:12 says “molten brass”; the Arabic lecture explains the metal as copper. Brass and copper are not being identified as the same substance.

Commentary on verse 12

Abū Dāwūd 3462 combines ‘īnah transactions, attachment to cattle and cultivation, and abandonment of jihad in its warning. Al-Albānī grades that numbered report ṣaḥīḥ; a separate Arabic source presentation labels it ḥasan without naming its evaluator. The warning is not being turned into an unconditional prohibition of agriculture.

Explanation of the Arabic commentary:
In an ‘īnah sale, the seller gives the buyer time to pay, then immediately buys back the same item for less, paying cash. The buyer now has cash in hand and owes the seller a larger sum. The Arabic commentary condemns this use of two sales to get around the prohibition on interest. It explains the image of a farmer following cattle at the plow as a warning about becoming so absorbed in earning a living that one loses sight of the Hereafter and neglects jihad, whether by giving of one’s wealth, taking part in person, or speaking out. The report warns that the resulting humiliation lasts until the people return to practicing their religion.

The people of Makkah faced that same outcome if they received the signs they demanded and still refused to believe. Despite the denial and abuse he had endured, the Prophet ﷺ continued to call them to Allah. He hoped that Allah would guide them or that some of their descendants would worship Him.

Commentary on verse 31

Al-Sarakhsī’s prison dictation and the well account appear in biographical accounts; completion of Al-Mabsūṭ continued afterward. The biographical reference is Al-Jawāhir al-Muḍiyyah 3:78; it does not imply that all of Al-Mabsūṭ was composed in the well. Ibn al-Qayyim’s statement in the introductory fascicle, printed page 38, concerns words written while traveling with a needed reference unavailable. Ibn ‘Abd al-Hādī describes Ibn Taymiyyah’s Qur’anic explanations in confinement; this does not establish a complete commentary on every verse.

Commentary on verse 33

Al-Tirmidhī 1173 uses the technical word ‘awrah, something requiring covering because its exposure is shameful. Its English wording about Satan seeking to tempt her is an interpretation of istashrafahā; explanations include making her attractive to men and seeking temptation of her and through her. Al-Tirmidhī calls the report ḥasan gharīb; Darussalam grades it weak; a separate Arabic source presentation labels it ṣaḥīḥ without naming its evaluator. These assessments remain separate. Salmān’s marketplace comparison (Muslim 2451) is a Companion’s statement, rendered from Arabic with its battlefield and banner imagery.

Explanation of the Arabic commentary:
The Arabic commentary discourages a woman from appearing before a man who is neither her husband nor a relative she can never marry. In its reading, she is forbidden to make a display of her adornment before such a man. When she goes out, it says, Satan hopes to tempt her and stirs others to desire her. While she is in the privacy of her home, he has no such expectation. The commentary also records a figurative reading in which Satan here means a corrupt person who watches her.

Commentary on verse 35

The brief dinar saying is attributed to Al-Bukhārī 2887 with similar wording. The longer English witness includes garment descriptions that differ from Al-Bukhārī’s wording. The Ayyūb report is Ibn Ḥibbān 2898 in the consulted numbering (2887 in another edition): Al-Albānī calls it sound in Al-Ṣaḥīḥah 17, while Shu‘ayb al-Arna’ūṭ assesses its chain as meeting Muslim’s conditions. These are attributed assessments.

Published explanation of the longer report witness:
In this Hadīth, the Messenger of Allah (may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him) says that there are people who are fully overwhelmed by the worldly life, for which they pay their utmost attention. Such people will be led to destruction and loss. They are characterized by their deep greed for the worldly life; if one of them is given of its pleasures, he is pleased, and if not, he is displeased. There are other people, however, who aim to please Allah and seek the Hereafter. They have no desire for prestige or fame. They only seek to obey Allah and His Messenger. Such people do not care about how they look, they are satisfied with the place they are placed in, they are insignificant in the eyes of people, and they are distant from the people of authority. If one of them seeks permission to enter upon a person of power, he is not granted permission, and if he intercedes, he is not given such a privilege. However, he is destined to Paradise, which is definitely the most excellent reward.

The explanation concerns the longer witness, distinct from the brief dinar saying attributed to Al-Bukhārī 2887. In that longer report, the English places the servant in the vanguard, whereas the Arabic describes the rear.

Commentary on verse 39

The sacred report in Al-Bukhārī 6502 is summarized in Arabic-derived meaning where it describes Allah’s dislike of causing the believer distress. This is distinct from the consulted English wording about disappointing him. The reflection concerns an added Arabic pronoun referring to the person. The selected English wording “or restricts it” uses “it” for provision; it does not translate that personal pronoun with an isolated English counterpart. The reflection remains commentary on the Arabic.

Commentary on verse 40

The embedded Qur’anic wording and its published notes express the selected translator’s interpretation. Where the lecturer discusses further readings or explanations—such as 34:18, 34:23, 34:45, 34:51, and 34:54—those remain commentary rather than alterations to the verse or its source notes.

Commentary on verse 44

In the complete Aḥmad 22498 account, the Negus compares what he hears with what Moses brought; the exchange about Jesus occurs the next day. The accompanying assessment attributed to Shu‘ayb al-Arna’ūṭ calls the chain strong. ‘Abdullāh ibn Salām’s conversion and Heraclius’s recognition are separate Al-Bukhārī reports; no conversion of Heraclius is asserted.

Commentary on verse 45

The statement about three groups receiving severe punishment is an account from the Companion ‘Abdullāh ibn ‘Amr, not a prophetic saying. In the discussion accompanying the two reports on Al-Ṭabarī’s printed 9:132, Aḥmad Shākir records Sulaymān al-Taymī’s weakening of Abū al-Mughīrah al-Qawwās and Ibn Ma‘īn’s judgment that he was reliable. These differing narrator assessments are retained rather than replaced with a blanket verdict.

Commentary on verse 46

The complete Salmān account is Aḥmad 23737 in the consulted numbering; its accompanying assessment attributed to Shu‘ayb al-Arna’ūṭ is ḥasan. The deposit account is the compiler’s expressly abbreviated entry in Al-Bayhaqī, Al-Sunan al-Kubrā 12823, Al-Turkī edition 13:92, with an accompanying ḥasan-chain assessment attributed to Al-Albānī. The Black Stone report is Al-Ṭayālisī 115, 1:108 in the consulted edition, with a ḥasan assessment attributed to Shu‘ayb al-Arna’ūṭ. Numbering varies between editions.

Commentary on verse 46

For al-Walīd’s report, Al-Ḥākim calls the connected chain sound according to Al-Bukhārī’s conditions, whereas Muqbil al-Wādi‘ī criticizes the connected Ma‘mar route as anomalous. Al-Bayhaqī’s connected and disconnected parallels and comments are distinct witnesses. The account is not presented as having an uncontested assessment.
