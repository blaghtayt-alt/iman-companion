Extended reflection

Surat al-Kahf — Reflection

Verses 1–110

Verse 1

All praise be to Allah Who has sent down upon His slave the Book, and has not allowed any crookedness therein[1],

Surah al-Kahf is a Makkan surah, one of the venerable early surahs whose value the Companions recognized from an early time. Its structure moves between contrasting forms of trial and action: young men with no resources of strength beyond faith withdraw to a cave; a believer speaks with a man of wealth and influence; Moses, peace be upon him, makes a long journey to learn what he did not know; then Dhul-Qarnayn, who possesses extensive resources, uses them to call people to Allah, uphold justice, and resist corruption. The surah can therefore be read as weekly training in steadfastness through weakness, action, learning, and power, while the standard remains the same: revelation, sound guidance, and seeing worldly adornment for what it is. The themes of adornment and sound guidance recur because trials often work by making falsehood attractive or magnifying outward appearances, whereas the Quran gives the believer insight to see the outcome before it becomes apparent to everyone. The prophetic texts strongly connect this surah with protection from trials. Among the best known is: “Whoever memorizes the first ten verses of Surat al-Kahf will be protected from the Antichrist.” [Muslim 809]. There are narrations concerning its beginning and others concerning its end. The Antichrist, or Dajjal, represents the extreme reversal of what the senses perceive: the hadiths describe him displaying what appears to people as water and fire, contrary to their reality. Thus the surah’s protection against trial is not a matter of words detached from their meanings; it nurtures light, tranquility, and the ability to distinguish reality from appearance. Reports from the Companions and prophetic hadiths also relate light to reciting al-Kahf on Friday, although hadith scholars differ over some of their chains and wordings. The central lesson remains undisputed: return regularly to this surah to strengthen the heart against trials whose forms keep changing. The surah begins with praise for the greatest blessing a person needs amid trials: revelation. Someone may possess intelligence, experience, and abundant information, yet still see things wrongly. A trial does not always appear ugly; it often comes attractively presented, making the temporary seem permanent, falsehood seem true, and harm seem beneficial. Allah’s sending down the Book to His slave therefore calls for the fullest praise. Despite his exalted rank, the Messenger ﷺ is described here through “His slave.” This honors him and refutes any exaggeration that raises him above the station of a servant. It also prepares the context for the warning against those who said, “Allah has begotten a son[3].” The statement that He “has not allowed any crookedness therein[1]” has been explained as the absence of contradiction or defect, or of any deviation from the truth. These explanations share one meaning: revelation is the upright standard against which human ideas and judgments are assessed. Human reasoning is not discarded altogether, but human speech must not become the judge over Allah’s speech. Here the light of al-Kahf begins: before entering its accounts of trial, take the standard by which you will recognize trial. One distinctive feature of the surah is that its four accounts, with these details, are not brought together elsewhere in the Quran. Even Moses’s journey with Khidr does not appear in the lengthy accounts of Moses in other surahs. Al-Kahf is also one of five surahs that open with praise: al-Fatihah, al-An‘am, al-Kahf, Saba, and Fatir. Here praise is connected especially with the blessing of the Quran’s revelation. After witnessing weakness and persecution, wealth that tempts, knowledge whose possessor cannot encompass everything, and power that requires gratitude, the reader returns to the opening and praises Allah for a Book that guides him before trial overtakes him. The surah’s reported merits and meanings bring together three needs of the person journeying toward Allah: light to distinguish the way, tranquility to remain firm upon it, and protection from trial. These merits are not detached from reflection on its message. One may also observe a movement from the young men’s vulnerability to Dhul-Qarnayn’s extensive resources, with calling to Allah in the dialogue with the garden owner and learning in Moses’s journey in between. This is a possible reflective reading of the surah’s structure, not a definitive explanation of the order of its accounts. It suggests that worship and action need not await a time of power: every condition offers a door to obedience.

Verse 2

an upright Book, to warn of severe punishment from Him[2], and to give glad tidings to the believers who do righteous deeds, that they will have a good reward,

The Book from which crookedness is denied is described as “an upright Book.” The Arabic word qayyiman has been explained as upright, as attending to the welfare of creation, or as overseeing and standing in authority over the earlier scriptures. These meanings support a picture of revelation that is not merely correct in itself, but also puts human understanding and conduct right. Two purposes then appear, without which calling people to Allah cannot remain sound: “to warn” and “to give glad tidings.” The Quran does not become cold intellectual culture, nor emotional speech that offers promises without accountability. It warns of Allah’s severe punishment and gives good news to the believer who translates faith into righteous action. A subtle feature of the surah’s structure is that someone who preserves this balance is better equipped to face the Dajjal and worldly trials. The greatest trials turn standards upside down, whereas the upright Book restores things to their true names and worth. Because a human being is more than pure intellect, the Quran does not stop at proof and correction: it combines warning with good news. Fear of the outcome and hope in Allah’s mercy stir the will to act, while a person may know the argument yet remain captive to desire. This is one aspect of the wisdom in the surah’s succession of promises and warnings: receiving revelation in a way that changes one’s life requires insight of the heart alongside clarity of evidence.

Verse 3

wherein they will abide forever;

The “good reward” is not a passing recompense like the things that disappear in this world. It is an enduring abode, “wherein they will abide forever”. From the beginning, the surah trains the heart to compare outcomes: the earth’s adornment will become barren ground; the owner of the two gardens will lose what he thought could never perish; and even the great barrier will be leveled when Allah’s promise comes to pass. What is with Allah, however, remains. This meaning is completed toward the end of the surah with the gardens of Paradise and “never desiring to leave.” Whoever knows that the path ends in an eternal stay will not make temporary hardship, poverty, or isolation an excuse to abandon the truth, nor take temporary comfort as proof that his course is successful.

Verse 4

and to warn those who say, “Allah has begotten a son[3].”

After the good news comes a specific warning against those who say, “Allah has begotten a son[3].” Placing this warning after describing the Prophet ﷺ as “His slave” brings every created being, however high in rank, back to the reality of servanthood. Various claims attributing offspring to Allah fall under this heading. The problem is not so much the name of one particular group as a corruption in the very foundation of knowing Allah. One connection suggested by the surah’s structure is that the Dajjal’s trial at the end of time reaches the extreme of reversing realities. Jesus, peace be upon him, then descends and kills the Dajjal, in one of the greatest scenes exposing falsehood. The Messiah is Allah’s servant and messenger, neither a god nor the son of a god. Whatever extraordinary powers the Dajjal makes people imagine he possesses, he remains only a powerless servant. From its beginning, the surah addresses both how we understand the Lord and how we understand the means and resources at people’s disposal.

Verse 5

They have no knowledge of it, nor did their forefathers[4]. What a monstrous word that comes out of their mouths! They say nothing but lies.

“They have no knowledge of it, nor did their forefathers[4].” This dismantles the appeal to inherited belief when it is detached from evidence: passing a claim from one generation to another does not make it true. Then comes the emphatic statement, “What a monstrous word.” The surah teaches that a word is not merely a small sound; it can carry an entire conception of Allah and reshape the feelings and convictions of generations. Thus the true words of revelation confront words of falsehood throughout the surah: the claim that Allah has offspring, the garden owner’s speech, the arguments of those who deny, and Dhul-Qarnayn’s words. The believer neither dismisses the importance of words nor repeats inherited claims without knowledge, for the gravest fabrication is to attribute to Allah what He has not said. The context places the trial of a false word before its discussion of earthly adornment. A single expression may create a false conception of Allah that many people inherit. It is no less dangerous than a desire that deceives the eye, and its effects may reach further. Without identifying an occasion of revelation or limiting the verse to a single meaning, one may see a connection between the claim that Allah has offspring, the trial of the false messiah, and the descent of Jesus, peace be upon him, which exposes that falsehood. A false word may confuse people, before the truth of servanthood and Allah’s oneness dismantles that confusion.

Verse 6

Perhaps you will grieve yourself to death [O Prophet] over them[5] if they do not believe in this message[6].

The Prophet’s ﷺ grief over those who turned away reached the intensity pictured in the verse: “Perhaps you will grieve yourself to death [O Prophet] over them[5].” Compassion is required of someone calling people to Allah, but consuming oneself is not worship. Some people reject the message not because the evidence has failed to reach them, but because layers of desire and doubts have made them unwilling to listen. Hence the warning concerning the Dajjal: “Let him who hears of the Dajjal (Antichrist) go far from him.” A man may approach him thinking himself a believer, then follow him because of the doubts he arouses [Abu Dawud]. Drawing closer and arguing more is not the answer to every situation. Nor should one spend a lifetime’s energy on every person who turns away, while another heart is seeking guidance. True compassion combines making every effort to convey the message with accepting that opening hearts is in Allah’s hands. Calling people to Allah must not become an emotional dependence on an outcome the caller cannot control.

Verse 7

We have made all that is on earth as an adornment for it, so that We may test them as to which of them is best in deeds.

“We have made all that is on earth as an adornment for it, so that We may test them as to which of them is best in deeds.” This establishes a central principle of the surah: adornment is a test, not a certificate of worth. Wealth, beauty, strength, followers, tools, and status may all be blessings used for Allah’s sake, or they may become a screen that blinds the heart. The verse says “best in deeds,” rather than most numerous in deeds. The surah will show that the best action changes with a person’s ability and situation. For the people of the Cave, it was to flee to preserve their religion; for the believer beside the garden owner, to speak with him and offer sincere advice; for Moses, to travel in pursuit of knowledge; and for Dhul-Qarnayn, to use strength and resources to uphold justice. Sound guidance does not mean everyone repeating the same action. It means each person recognizing what Allah requires of him in his own situation, then doing it well. The recurring mention of adornment, alongside the theme of sound guidance, makes this a question of choosing rightly, rather than merely appearing detached from worldly things. A believer may reject harmful adornment in one situation, then use wealth, knowledge, and strength themselves in obedience in another. Having few resources is not, by itself, what protects against being captivated. Protection lies in the heart possessing a standard that reveals the worth of a means and the purpose it serves.

Verse 8

And We will surely turn all that is on it into a barren land[7].

After presenting adornment in its most attractive form, the surah cuts through attachment to it by showing its end: “And We will surely turn all that is on it into a barren land[7].” This is the lens of sound guidance that the surah offers: seeing a thing together with its outcome before it falls apart before people’s eyes. A corresponding meaning appears in al-Hujurat: “Allah has endeared faith to you and made it pleasing to your hearts” and “Such are the rightly guided” [49:7]. True adornment is what Allah makes beautiful in the heart, not everything that glitters before the eye. The prominence of sound guidance in the young men’s account suits the severity of their trial and their few resources: the greater the pressure of circumstances, the more firmly grounded the believer’s insight must be. The “barren land” will return on a smaller scale in the account of the two gardens, when the flourishing garden becomes “a barren land.” The garden’s fate is a small picture of the fate of all earthly adornment. Discussion of the Dajjal gives a tangible illustration of deceptive appearances. Just as iron or copper can be coated with a golden color so that someone sees gold where there is none, falsehood may be presented as truth and fire as salvation. Strong eyesight alone does not save a person from this deception. He needs the light of revelation to distinguish a thing from its manufactured appearance, and to keep away from situations of trial in which he fears his own weakness.

Verse 9

Do you think that the people of the Cave and the Inscription were of Our only wondrous signs[8]?

Before recounting the young men’s story in detail, the surah asks: “Do you think that the people of the Cave and the Inscription were of Our only wondrous signs[8]?” Their story is indeed remarkable, but it is not Allah’s most remarkable sign. Excessive pursuit of extraordinary events can hide the signs repeated before a person every day: a plant growing, an embryo developing, the sun rising, and a heart beating. “Do you think” calls attention to an assumption that needs correcting: do not make your faith dependent on an exceptional event before recognizing Allah’s power. There are many interpretations of al-Raqim, rendered here as “the Inscription”: a tablet bearing their names, a book, or a place. What al-Raqim denotes remains uncertain, and the lesson of the account reaches beyond that question. What is most remarkable is not the place’s name or the young men’s number, but Allah guiding young people amid trial and protecting them through means they had never imagined. One interpretation takes al-Raqim to be something written, drawing a linguistic comparison with “a clearly inscribed Record” and “a record inscribed” [83:9,20]. Those who hold this interpretation then differ: did it record the young men’s names and story, or was it a document preserved with them from earlier books of guidance? Some who develop this interpretation picture righteous companions preserving their faith, with something remaining of revelation, in a time of persecution. This is a possibility within that interpretation. It establishes no particular historical report about them. A comparison with the creation of Jesus, peace be upon him, helps explain the warning against becoming captive to the extraordinary. Someone may be so astonished by a birth without a father that he attributes to the created being what belongs only to Allah, while overlooking that the creation of a human being from a father and mother, and the creation of Adam without either, are signs of the same Lord. It is a mistake for wonder at a sign to turn into exaggeration concerning the created being in whom it appeared.

Verse 10

When the youths took refuge in the Cave, and said, “Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself, and show us the right way in our ordeal[9].”

“When the youths took refuge in the Cave” brings together words that seem at odds. Taking refuge suggests rest and shelter; youths possess strength; and a cave is a forbidding place. Yet their real refuge was not the rock, but their address: “Our Lord.” In the hadith about the three men who entered the mosque, the Prophet ﷺ said: “One of them sought refuge with Allah, so Allah gave him refuge.” [Bukhari and Muslim]. For someone who seeks refuge with Allah, Allah may provide safety in a place that others fear. Describing them as youths highlights that young people, despite their intense exposure to adornment and social pressure, may attain faith strong enough to leave everything familiar for Allah’s sake. Their prayer combined two distinct needs: “mercy” encompassing their fear, loneliness, and pain, and “the right way,” guiding them to the right decision within a complex religious trial. They did not ask merely to escape; they asked for an escape that would please Allah. Hence their words, “grant us mercy from Yourself”: a special mercy that prepares for them what ordinary means cannot. A reported linguistic explanation of rushd, connected here with “the right way,” combines uprightness with firmness and steadfastness upon it, rather than merely recognizing what is right for a passing moment. Their need for sound guidance was therefore a need for a right decision and the resolve to preserve it under threat. They were not simply asking Allah for information about a way to safety, but to prepare a way upon which they could remain steadfast.

Verse 11

Then We caused them to fall into a deep sleep in the cave for many years.

Allah answered their fear in a way they had not expected: “Then We caused them to fall into a deep sleep in the cave for many years.” No thunderbolt struck the king, and no army came to carry them to a safe country. Sleep itself became one of Allah’s forces. This is an important principle in understanding His power: do not restrict deliverance to the scenario you have imagined. Allah returned the infant Moses to his mother through a simple means: “We had already forbidden for him all wet nurses[8]” [28:12]. Likewise, relief may open through a small detail that draws little attention. In recent crises, the world has seen how a tiny, invisible cause can bring cities, airports, and entire businesses to a halt. If a small created thing can have such an effect, a person should not confine the Creator’s power to the limits of his imagination. Your duty is to use the means available to you, then leave Allah to choose the form of protection.

Verse 12

Then We awakened them so that We may see which of the two parties would be more precise in calculating the length of their stay.

“Then We awakened them so that We may see which of the two parties would be more precise in calculating the length of their stay.” The account moves from protection to revealing the limits of human perception. The Arabic ahsa, connected here with “more precise in calculating,” has been discussed linguistically: is it a verb meaning to record or calculate accurately, or a comparative form? What matters here is that the discussion itself serves the meaning. People try to reconstruct and calculate the past, but complete certainty belongs to Allah. Some commentators suggest that discovering the length of their stay would enable them to recognize how greatly they had been protected and give thanks. Many acts of divine kindness occur in our lives, and only years later do we discover how much had been arranged for us behind the scenes: a marriage, a job, an encounter, harm averted, or a path closed so that another might open. A person sees only part of the story while living through it. Allah knows its beginning, its end, and everything between.

Verse 13

We relate to you their story in truth. They were youths who believed in their Lord, and We gave them more guidance.

“We relate to you their story in truth.” After the legends surrounding their account, this returns its narration to Allah as its source. The verse then sums up the truth about them that deserves to be remembered: “They were youths who believed in their Lord, and We gave them more guidance.” They began with the faith they were able to put into practice, and Allah gave them guidance suited to the next stage. In a sacred hadith, the Prophet ﷺ relates Allah’s words: “If the slave draws closer to Me by a hand-span, I shall draw closer to him by a cubit” [Bukhari and Muslim]. You do not have to see the map of the next ten years before taking your first step toward the truth. They had enough guidance to believe; Allah then increased their insight until they could leave family, comfort, and country for the cave. Many people postpone action because they want guarantees about every detail of the future. The Quran’s approach is to act on the duty you know, and receive the guidance you need when you come to its door. Another perspective opened by the account is that the young men’s visible heroism is not its only focus. The verses trace Allah’s actions toward them: He increased their guidance, strengthened their hearts, caused them to sleep, turned them over, made the sun play a part in their protection, then awakened them and made their case known. Read from this perspective, the account introduces the servant to his Lord, who arranges for him what he cannot arrange for himself. It is more than a story of brave youths accomplishing something extraordinary through their own strength. Reading note. The report about drawing near to Allah is authentic. Its sound narrations vary in the wording of the condition and the preposition. Those differences do not weaken the report.

Verse 14

We strengthened their hearts when they stood up and proclaimed, “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and earth. We will never call upon any god other than Him, or we would be uttering an outrageous lie[10].”

“We strengthened their hearts when they stood up.” The strengthening came at the moment of standing. They did not wait for fear to disappear before acting: they stood up, and firmness came from Allah. Steadfastness is not the absence of natural fear; it is Allah preventing fear from driving the heart to compromise. Their opening declaration was: “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and earth.” The One who preserves the heavens and earth will not abandon a small group who have gone forth for His sake. A recurring subtlety is that the two Arabic forms rabbana and rabbukum keep returning in different crises: “Our Lord” in their prayer and declaration of faith [18:10, 14], and “Your Lord” as they remind one another [18:19]. Whenever circumstances become unsettled, they seem to bring themselves back to the Lord. They then say: “We will never call upon any god other than Him, or we would be uttering an outrageous lie[10].” For them, associating partners with Allah is a grave transgression far beyond the bounds of truth, rather than merely a difference of opinion. Sound guidance means keeping the standard of Allah’s oneness clear even when society becomes accustomed to falsehood and presents it as normal.

Verse 15

These people of ours have taken besides Him other gods. Why do they not bring clear proof about them? Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates lies against Allah?

After declaring their belief, they moved to the evidence: “Why do they not bring clear proof about them?” Their lack of numbers and authority did not prevent them from possessing the stronger question: where is the proof? Social power does not create evidence, and large numbers of followers do not make a fabrication about Allah true. They concluded: “Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates lies against Allah?” The gravest wrong is to attribute to Allah what He has not said. This verse trains the believer not to let the intimidating weight of his surroundings prevent him from asking for evidence, and not to compensate for a lack of resources with harshness or insults. Evidence is itself a source of strength.

Verse 16

When you have distanced yourselves from them and what they worship other than Allah, take refuge in the cave; your Lord will extend His mercy to you and make for you an easy way out of your ordeal.”

When remaining among their people threatened the foundation of their religion, they said to one another, “take refuge in the cave.” The idea of taking refuge returns, carrying a sense of rest within a place whose outward appearance is forbidding. Then comes their good expectation of Allah: “your Lord will extend His mercy to you and make for you an easy way out of your ordeal.” Trusting in His mercy means trusting that He will care for His servant, whatever form relief takes and whenever it comes. Through mercy, a cramped cave may become more spacious than a palace that corrupts one’s religion. A supplication may be answered through an immediate gift, harm being averted, or a reward stored for later. By His command Allah made the fire cool and safe for Abraham. The Prophet ﷺ told Abu Bakr in the cave, “Do not grieve; Allah is with us.” [9:40]. An ordeal may also continue for a long time, as it did for Job, peace be upon him. The believer trusts in Allah’s care in each of these circumstances.

Verse 17

You would have seen the sun when it rose, it would incline away from their Cave to the right; and when it set, it would turn away from them to the left[11], while they were in its open space. That was one of the signs of Allah. Whoever Allah guides is rightly guided, and whoever He causes to stray, you will never find for him a protecting guide.

The verse describes meticulous care extending even to the sun’s movement around the place where they slept: it would “incline away from their Cave to the right” and “turn away from them to the left[11].” One interpretation is that the cave’s orientation was naturally suited to keeping the sun from harming them; another is that the sun’s movement itself was specially directed away from them. Here the Quran allows you to recognize Allah’s care either in choosing a suitable means or in changing how that means operates. Both interpretations return you to “That was one of the signs of Allah.” Within a vast universe, and beside a sun with which a small patch of cave can scarcely be compared, care appears directed toward this little group. An individual is not lost to Allah amid the multitude of creation. The verse then concludes: “Whoever Allah guides is rightly guided.” A protected place is a blessing, but the greater blessing is the heart remaining firm upon the way.

Verse 18

You would have thought they were awake, although they were asleep. We turned them over on their right and left sides, when their dog stretched its forelegs at the entrance. If you had seen them, you would have fled away from them, filled with horror[12].

They were asleep, yet “You would have thought they were awake.” Allah turned them onto their right and left sides, their dog was at the entrance, and He made the sight of them inspire such dread that others kept away. It has been said that turning them over preserved their bodies, and that their sleeping posture made anyone who saw them fearful. When they awoke, they thought they had slept for a day or part of a day. What is clearer is that their protection did not depend upon their strength. They did what they could when they went forth; Allah then guarded them while they were unaware. The One who protected them while they were unconscious can protect His servant while he obeys Him, and place in the hearts of those who would harm him a fear that he could not produce for himself.

Verse 19

Similarly, We awakened them so that they might question one another. One of them said, “How long have you been here?” They said, “We have been here for a day or part of a day.” They said, “Your Lord knows best how long you have been here. Send one of you with these silver coins of yours to the city, let him see which food is purest and bring you some provision from it. And let him be cautious and let no one know about you.

When Allah awakened them, they differed over the length of their stay. Then they cut short the discussion, useful or otherwise, by saying, “Your Lord knows best how long you have been here.” They moved on to a practical need: food. This is a skill of sound judgment; not every disagreement deserves to consume one’s time. They then said, “Send one of you.” The group acts without inflating everyone’s role: one person suited to the task goes, and that is enough. The mention of “these silver coins of yours” shows that they did not understand reliance on Allah to mean abandoning practical means. They carried money and used it. Concerning “which food is purest,” one interpretation is the tastiest food, and another is the most lawful. If lawfulness is intended, hardship does not remove the need to ask what Allah permits. If the tastiest is intended, a crisis does not require the believer to become a bundle of panic unable to appreciate anything beautiful in life. They then instructed him: “let him be cautious.” Having a sound aim does not remove the need for wisdom in one’s approach. One person’s rashness can spoil an entire group’s work if he speaks without considering the circumstances, or shares a clip or a piece of information while imagining that he is serving the truth.

Verse 20

For if they find out about you, they will stone you or force you back to their religion, and then you will never succeed.”

Their fear was realistic: if their people discovered them, they might stone them or return them to their religion. Faith does not make a person naive when assessing danger. These young men were recipients of remarkable divine favor, yet they still planned for secrecy and a careful approach. Their priorities were clear, however: bodily harm was less serious than losing their religion. Hence their words about returning to the worship of others besides Allah: “and then you will never succeed.” Success here does not mean escaping prison or stoning, but leaving this world with one’s religion intact. This standard prevents a believer from selling his faith for safety, only to discover that his body survived while he lost himself.

Verse 21

This is how We made their case known so that the people might know that the promise of Allah is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. When they were disputing among themselves[13] concerning their matter [of resurrection], they said, “Build a structure over them. Their Lord knows best about them.” Those who prevailed in their matter said, “We will surely build over them a place of worship.”

Allah made their case known after centuries “so that the people might know that the promise of Allah is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour.” The account itself became a tangible demonstration of the possibility of resurrection, just as Allah revived the man after a hundred years [2:259] and showed Abraham how He gives life to the dead [2:260]. A person may know Allah’s power through revelation, then Allah gives him a sign through which he witnesses the effect of that meaning. Yet after the sign became apparent, a new deviation emerged: people disputed what to build over them, and those who prevailed concluded, “We will surely build over them a place of worship.” The purpose was to establish resurrection; the sign must not be consumed by venerating the place and the graves. The Prophet ﷺ explicitly warned: “May the curse of Allah be upon the Jews and Christians; they took the graves of their prophets as mosques.” [Bukhari and Muslim]. Love for righteous people must not turn into building a place of worship over their graves, or people will move away from the righteous person’s message toward his person and location. A subtle observation about the account’s course is that the Quran preserved the memory of people who refused to support wrongdoing and withdrew from it, while it did not list the names of those involved in the changes that occurred in society during their long sleep. Accounts of reform include heroes whose actions bring neither fame nor a visible effect that can be measured within their lifetimes. Their stands are nevertheless not lost with Allah. The enduring lesson is that what matters is not remaining at the forefront of history, but one’s faith and sincerity.

Verse 22

Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” – merely guessing at the unseen. And others will say, “They were seven, their dog was the eighth.” Say, “My Lord knows best their number; no one knows about them except a few[14]. So do not argue about them except in a brief manner, nor ask anyone about them.”

After some people lost sight of the lesson by building over graves, others lost it by counting the youths: three, five, or seven? The verse describes two proposals as “merely guessing at the unseen,” evoking someone throwing a stone into darkness in the hope of hitting a target. Some commentators considered that the mention of “They were seven, their dog was the eighth” without repeating the description of guessing gives that view stronger support. Others discussed the Arabic conjunction wāw, meaning and, before the reference to the eighth. This has been called the waw of eight, a proposed counting rule that most grammarians did not accept. What remains central is the Quran’s own statement: “My Lord knows best their number; no one knows about them except a few[14].” From this comes the rule: “So do not argue about them except in a brief manner.” There are matters for which people possess no certain evidence. Lifetimes should not be consumed by arguments about the unseen built on uncertain premises. Knowledge does not mean answering every question; it includes knowing where to stop.

Verse 23

And never say about anything, “I will surely do this tomorrow[15],”

After the lesson about a past we do not possess comes the proper attitude toward a future we do not possess either: the warning against declaring, “I will surely do this tomorrow[15]”, without making it subject to Allah’s will. An account relates that the Prophet ﷺ promised his questioners an answer the next day without adding the condition of Allah’s will, and revelation was delayed. Hadith scholars have raised questions about some chains of this account of the occasion of revelation. The meaning indicated by the text, however, is established: a servant should not speak about the future as though he guarantees what will occur. You plan, promise, and work, but Allah’s will remains above your plan. Saying insha Allah, “if Allah wills,” is not a way to evade responsibility or cover laziness. It is a declaration of servanthood at the very heart of taking practical means.

Verse 24

without adding, “if Allah wills.” And remember your Lord if you forget, and say, “My Lord will hopefully guide me to something closer to what is right[16].”

The instruction is to add “if Allah wills,” followed by “And remember your Lord if you forget.” One interpretation is that if you forget to add the condition, mention Allah when you remember. Another is broader: remembrance of Allah is itself a remedy for forgetfulness, for at the root of forgetfulness is the heart momentarily losing touch with its dependence upon Him. Both memory and resolve depend on Allah’s help. Then comes the prayer: “My Lord will hopefully guide me to something closer to what is right[16].” The Arabic demonstrative hādhā, meaning this, has several proposed referents here; the published English does not give it a separate word within the phrase “something closer to what is right[16]”. One is guidance to something more sound than the matter forgotten, with Allah providing something better in its place. Another is a sign clearer than the account of the youths in establishing prophethood. A third is guidance higher than theirs. These interpretations share a lesson: sound guidance is not a certificate obtained once and for all. Whenever you recognize your shortcomings, ask Allah for guidance closer to what is right. The heart needs that same help to stand firm. Fear does not leave at one’s own command. Of the youths, Allah says, “We strengthened their hearts.”

Verse 25

They remained in their cave for three hundred years, plus nine[17].

The Quran states the length of their stay: “three hundred years, plus nine[17].” One interpretation is that the three hundred are solar years, with the additional nine accounting for the difference in the lunar calendar. Another holds that the sentence reports a circulating claim, followed by “Allah knows best how long they remained.” Many commentators, however, favored understanding the duration as information from Allah. Even concerning the first interpretation, some people versed in calculation have discussed the exactness of a strictly literal conversion; Arabic allows approximation in large numbers. However the number is understood, the youths’ sense of time was limited. They thought they had stayed for a day or part of a day, although centuries had passed. Our perception of time is limited. Allah alone knows the reality of the duration and all that happened within it.

Verse 26

Say, “Allah knows best how long they remained. To Him belongs [the knowledge of] the unseen of the heavens and earth. How well He sees and how well He hears[18]! They have no protector besides Him, and He shares His command with none.”

“Allah knows best how long they remained.” The account closes with the same reference to which the youths themselves turned: “Your Lord knows best how long you have been here.” To Him belongs knowledge of the unseen of the heavens and earth. “How well He sees and how well He hears[18]!” He saw their departure, heard their words, and knew their sleeping and awakening when they were hidden from every human being. A hero forgotten by history books is therefore not lost with Allah. Among the accounts told about them are things that are sound and things that are not; certainty, however, comes from revelation. Then: “They have no protector besides Him, and He shares His command with none.” No protector preserves anyone independently of Allah, and no one shares the Lord’s judgment. From this point, the surah turns to three practical foundations after the youths’ account: attachment to revelation, patience alongside the people of faith, and declaring the truth. These are, as it were, the means of deliverance from trial after we have seen their living example. The details of a sacrifice may remain hidden from an entire society, just as the youths’ story remained hidden for years. Being hidden from people’s eyes does not place it beyond Allah’s knowledge. This closing therefore reminds someone enduring patiently on the path of truth that his prayer, dependence upon his Lord, and relationship with the unseen are not marginal matters beside the noise of present circumstances. They are the source of his steadfastness when no witness sees what he has given and no one appreciates its effect.

Verse 27

Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord. None can change His Words, and you will never find any refuge except with Him[19].

“Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord.” This is the first instruction given to the believer after the account of trial: return to revelation. Recitation includes reading and following. The Quran is not a text to cite after forming one’s standards elsewhere; it is what forms the standard. This does not prevent studying human fields of knowledge, but it prevents making them judges over revelation. “None can change His Words” includes the firmness of His information, promise, and law. No one can escape what Allah has decreed, and what has befallen you could not have missed you. Concerning “you will never find any refuge except with Him[19],” one interpretation is that no refuge can be found apart from Allah. Another connects the meaning with revelation: in matters of the unseen and guidance, no alternative source of knowledge can take the place of Allah’s Book. Someone who leaves the unchanging words and seeks security in shifting assumptions will find himself without a true cave when trial comes. A relationship with the Quran requires time devoted to it, like every relationship intended to endure and grow. One lesson awakened by regularly reading al-Kahf on Friday is not to let a week pass seeking solutions to one’s crises everywhere while abandoning the Book already at hand. A quick passage over the letters is not enough. Allah’s words, “so that you may recite it to people at a slower pace” [17:106], teach a deliberate way of receiving revelation that allows its meaning to change thought and choice.

Verse 28

Be patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face [pleasure]. Do not turn your eyes away from them, desiring the adornments of the life of this world. And do not obey one whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desires and whose affairs [deeds] are at loss.

The second foundation after revelation is companionship: “Be patient with those who call upon their Lord.” The instruction is more than not expelling them: it requires keeping oneself patiently in their company. The soul may incline toward gatherings of status and wealth and grow weary of gatherings of the weak. Accounts of the occasion of revelation and parallel meanings relate to the words in al-An‘am, “Do not send away [O Prophet] those who invoke their Lord” [6:52], when some leading men of Quraysh wanted the poor believers removed. The standard for companionship is “seeking His Face [pleasure],” not a group’s name or a teacher’s fame. If the companionship itself becomes religiously corrupt, its label alone no longer makes it sound. In the hadith: “Verily, the example of the righteous companion and the evil companion is like that of the carrier of the musk and the blower of the bellows.” [Bukhari and Muslim]. Spending time together transmits interests, language, and standards. Then comes: “Do not turn your eyes away from them, desiring the adornments of the life of this world.” The eye sometimes moves ahead of the heart and ranks the person of wealth above the person of faith. Concerning a poor man whom people disregarded, the Prophet ﷺ said: “This one (the second) is better than an earthful of the other (the first).” [Bukhari]. On the opposite side: “And do not obey one whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desires.” The heart does not remain without a leader: when it leaves remembrance, desire enters. Repeated looking has effects beyond the eye. The movement may begin when the eye turns toward a person of standing; the body then follows to his gathering, and the heart gradually changes. Language, terms, and interests shift until wealth becomes the standard for giving or withholding precedence. Hence the command to remain patiently among believers, rather than merely acknowledging their virtue from afar. An account concerning some weak early believers being given precedence over people of status points back to the standard of earlier faith and sincerity, rather than influence. The gathering a person chooses shapes what he loves, what he fears, and what he sees as success.

Verse 29

Say, “The truth is from your Lord. Whoever wills may believe, and whoever wills may disbelieve.” We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire which will encompass them like the walls. If they cry for relief, they will be relieved with water like boiling oil that will scald the faces. What a terrible drink, and what a terrible resting place!

“The truth is from your Lord.” This is the third foundation. After a Book that sets one’s vision right and companions who help one remain firm, the believer can speak the truth without making the approval of the wealthy or the pressure of the crowd his standard. “Whoever wills may believe, and whoever wills may disbelieve.” One interpretation emphasizes the reality of choice; another understands it as a threat. The context makes clear that the two paths are not being treated as equal, because the Fire is mentioned immediately afterward. The Fire “will encompass them like the walls.” This is a painful counterpart for someone who leaves the cave in which Allah extended mercy and chooses a different enclosure. If they cry for relief, they are given water like boiling oil that scalds faces. The wording “they will be relieved” is used although the response itself is punishment, as though the language overturns the imagined rescue they sought. Then “what a terrible resting place!” directly contrasts with “make for you an easy way out of your ordeal” [18:16]. Someone who abandons the ease of obedience in pursuit of immediate comfort ends in the worst resting place. Ibn Kathir explains that the wrongdoers in this passage are those who disbelieve in Allah, His Messenger, and His Book. The enclosure of the Fire calls to mind persecutors who surround believers and make them live in fear. The cry for relief answered only with torment recalls someone who heard an oppressed person call and failed to help. The comparison warns against oppression and neglect. Commentators have explained murtafaq, connected here with “resting place,” through resting or reclining; other explanations relate it to ease or gathering together. The interpretations vary, while the close contrast remains with the ease the youths sought, and then with the believers’ pleasant resting place.

Verse 30

Those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will not let the reward of those who do good deeds to go to waste.

On the other side are “Those who believe and do righteous deeds”, assured that their reward will not be lost. The emphatic Arabic expression of divine assurance, connected here with “We will not let the reward,” stands as though answering everyone who fears that his life has been wasted because he has seen no worldly fruit from his work. In the reckoning of history, the people of the Cave might appear to be young men who disappeared and slept, founded no state, and did not change the system with their own hands. Nevertheless, fleeing to preserve their religion was the best action in their situation. The worth of work is not measured by fame, by how many people know its doer’s name, or by the speed of results. History books preserve the names of many soldiers, donors, and teachers imperfectly or not at all, but Allah lets none of their work be lost when it is righteous and well done. Society may change during a period in which a reformer never witnesses the fruit of his stand. The work of someone who remained firm against wrongdoing and did not participate in it should not be belittled because he did not personally lead the historical transformation. The contrast between the youths and Dhul-Qarnayn gives two different forms of heroism their place: refusing a trial when one lacks the means to remove it, and using resources to remove wrongdoing when the ability becomes available. Either may be an instance of doing well, according to the circumstances.

Verse 31

It is they who will have Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow. They will be adorned therein with golden bracelets and will wear green garments of fine silk and brocade, reclining on adorned couches. What an excellent reward, and what a pleasant resting place!

The verse details the reward that will not be lost: Gardens of Eternity with rivers flowing beneath them, ornaments, green clothing, reclining, and rest. The Arabic ʿadn, rendered in “Gardens of Eternity,” carries the meaning of residence and settled permanence. This suits those who left a spacious home and lived temporarily in a cave for Allah’s sake. Some accounts relate that the youths left fine clothing and an easy life to preserve their faith. Their reward is a purer, lasting comfort and adornment that will never harm them. The same word then returns: “What an excellent reward, and what a pleasant resting place!” In verse 16, they asked Allah to prepare ease for them in the cave; in verse 29, the Fire was the terrible resting place; here is the pleasant eternal resting place.

Verse 32

Give them an example of two men[20]: to one of them We gave two gardens of grapevines and surrounded them with palm trees, and placed between them crops[21].

After commanding patience alongside poor believers and forbidding turning beyond them in pursuit of worldly adornment, the Quran gives the example of two men. One was given two gardens of grapevines, surrounded with palms, with crops placed between them. One explanation for presenting the example is that it addresses the leading men of Quraysh who were deceived by wealth and followers and demanded preference over the poor. Another interpretation understands it as an actual account rather than only a constructed example. What matters is that the Quran places before the person of status a picture of the end awaiting someone who makes wealth the standard of superiority. Notice the sequence of actions before the garden owner speaks: “We gave,” “surrounded them,” and “placed between them crops[21].” The account first returns the blessing to Allah, before we hear its possessor attribute strength to himself. The garden’s arrangement is itself precise: grapes within, palms around, and crops between the two gardens. There is abundance, beauty, and good use of space, but all of this remains a gift, rather than a self-sufficient possession.

Verse 33

Each garden yielded its produce and did not fall short in the least, and We caused a stream to flow through them.

“Each garden yielded its produce and did not fall short in the least.” There was no deficiency to justify resentment. Every tree fulfilled its task; the Arabic wording describing the gardens’ not falling short uses a root also associated with wrongdoing. The Quran will describe their owner as “having wronged himself[22].” It is a striking contrast: the nonhuman world fulfills its function, while the human being’s standards become distorted despite the completeness of the blessing. Then: “We caused a stream to flow through them.” Allah had even eased the burden of irrigation for him. In its descriptions of cultivation, the Quran repeatedly attributes water and growth to Allah: “How We pour down rainwater in torrents”, “and cause the soil to split open [for sprouts]”, and “and cause grains to grow in it” [80:25–27]. A believer does not see the fruit and forget the immense network of blessings that preceded it: water, soil, sunlight, bodily ability, and a mind that plans. Any link could break if Allah willed.

Verse 34

And he had abundant fruit. He said to his companion, as he was conversing with him, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers.”

“And he had abundant fruit.” One interpretation takes this as the fruit of the two gardens themselves. Another understands him to have possessed further wealth as well, such as gold and silver. On either reading, his abundance was great. His words then revealed what it had done to his heart: “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers.” The first prominent word is “I,” followed by the comparison with the other man. The blessing moved from a reason for gratitude to a means of defining himself and belittling someone else. This resembles Iblis’s statement, “I am better than him” [7:12], and the Children of Israel’s objection concerning Saul: “we are more deserving of kingship than him” [2:247]. Today, the same attitude may take the form of follower counts, qualifications, numbers of books, or projects, even in religious work. A number is not corrupting in itself; the danger is making having more the measure of worth. The words “as he was conversing with him” keep the door to dialogue open: the force with which he asserts falsehood does not prevent the believer from addressing him with evidence while the opportunity remains.

Wealth is therefore not evil in itself. It may, however, change from a blessing into a trial that corrupts its possessor’s heart and drives him toward pride, heedlessness, and transgression.

It may even be part of Allah’s mercy toward His servant to withhold worldly things that He knows could corrupt him. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Indeed Allah protects His believing servant from the world while loving him as you protect your sick from food and drink because you fear it for them.” [Reported by Ahmad; graded authentic by al-Albani in Sahih al-Jami‘, 1814]. Just as a patient may be kept from something he craves out of concern for him, a servant may be denied something he loves as a mercy to him, even when he does not perceive the good in it.

Allah’s words illuminate this meaning: “If Allah were to give abundant provision to [all] His slaves, they would transgress on earth; but He sends down what He wills in due measure. Indeed, He is All-Aware and All-Seeing of His slaves.” [42:27]. Reflect on the verse’s closing, “All-Aware and All-Seeing.” He, glorified is He, knows His servants’ hearts best and knows what will set them right. Wealth may be better for one and poverty for another. At one time He may open a door of provision for a servant, and at another restrict it, for a wisdom He knows. Not everything we wish for is good for us, and not everything withheld from us is harmful to us.

More dangerous still, abundant wealth and blessings can sometimes be a form of gradual drawing toward punishment. A person is given access to the worldly things he desires, becomes increasingly attached to them and absorbed in accumulating them, and grows heedless of Allah, until he imagines that the continuation of blessings proves the soundness of his condition. Allah says: “When they forgot the reminder that they were given[13], We opened for them the doors of everything – until when they rejoiced in pride for what they were given, We seized them by surprise, so they fell into utter despair.” [6:44]. Notice how the opening of the doors of blessing came after they turned away from the reminder. Such abundance was not evidence of Allah’s pleasure with them, but a gradual drawing toward punishment of which they were unaware.

Allah also says: “Do they think that what We provide them of wealth and children means that We hasten to them all that is good? No, but they do not perceive it[25].” [23:55–56]. Someone may imagine that continuing and increasing wealth proves that he is on the right path, while in fact he becomes more heedless and distant from Allah. Not every wealthy person is being drawn toward punishment in this way. The danger lies in the blessing distracting him from its Giver, and increasing his transgression instead of his gratitude.

Wealth and children themselves may become a means of punishment, as Allah says concerning the hypocrites: “So do not let their wealth or their children impress you; Allah only intends to punish them through these things in the life of this world and to let their souls depart while they are still disbelievers.” [9:55]. For some people, wealth becomes a source of misery. They are consumed by acquiring and protecting it, live in fear for it, and grieve because of it, until it takes possession of their hearts and makes them forget the Hereafter. Thus the very thing a person sees as his greatest source of happiness may become a cause of his suffering.

Verse 35

He entered his garden, having wronged himself[22], and said, “I do not think that this will ever perish,

He entered his garden at the height of its beauty, “having wronged himself[22].” It has been said that he was taking his companion around it like someone presenting a visual spectacle to put pressure on the poor man. The image itself may become an illusory argument when the real argument is weak. This recalls Korah coming out “before his people in all his glamor” [28:79]. The eye may be captivated before the mind asks whether the path is truly sound. He then said, “I do not think that this will ever perish”. One explanation for the singular “his garden,” although he owned two, is that the gardens were connected. Al-Zamakhshari offered another subtle interpretation: this was his only garden, which he had made his entire world, with no share for him in the Garden of the Hereafter. Whichever explanation is taken, his clearest sickness is the illusion of permanence. It is the same illusion through which Satan approaches: “shall I show you the tree of immortality and a kingdom that will never decay?” [20:120]. Transgression likes to imagine that the system it has built will endure.

Verse 36

nor do I think that the Hour will ever come. Even if I am brought back to my Lord, I will surely find something better than this[23].”

His pride did not stop at expecting the garden to remain. It extended to the Hour: “nor do I think that the Hour will ever come.” He then devised another form of assurance for his beliefs: if there is a return to Allah, he will find something better than the garden. This is among the most dangerous ways of reading a blessing: taking material success as Allah’s certification that one’s belief is correct and that one will always deserve more. Elsewhere the Quran dismantles this illusion: a person may be tested through honour and blessings, then say, “My Lord has [deservedly] honored me[5]!” The answer follows: “No indeed[6]!” [89:15–17]. One interpretation takes the garden owner’s words as contradictory mockery: he doubts the Hereafter, then says that if it exists he will be better off. Another understands him as genuinely deceived by the reasoning that the One who gave him things here will also give him things there. Either way, when wealth becomes a person’s explanation of his relationship with Allah, it may produce a belief tailored to desire instead of making its possessor submit to revelation.

Verse 37

His companion said, while conversing with him, “Do you disbelieve in He Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then fashioned you into a well-proportioned man[24]?

His believing companion did not merely tell him that he was arrogant. He brought him back to his origins, asking how he could disbelieve in the One Who “created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then fashioned you into a well-proportioned man[24]”. The Quran chooses from the stages of creation what suits the sickness being treated. Before someone boasting of having more, it places beginnings over which he had no control: dust, then a sperm-drop, then Allah fashioning him into a man capable of speaking and comparing. Remembering the origin of creation is therefore more than a fact in biology; it is a remedy for pride. Companionship did not prevent frank advice. When his words reached disbelief, his companion asked, “Do you disbelieve,” then grounded the explanation in Allah’s oneness and creation instead of turning the conversation into an exchange of insults.

Verse 38

As for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I do not associate anyone with my Lord.

After answering falsehood, the believer declared his own standard: “As for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I do not associate anyone with my Lord.” In a time of doubts, the person holding to truth cannot remain only on the defensive. He needs to state what he believes and reveal the source of his worth. Poverty did not make him borrow the rich man’s definition of success. The other man can boast, “I am greater than you in wealth,” while he says, “He is Allah, my Lord.” One educational lesson here is that a dialogue about belief does more than dismantle the opponent’s words. It also reaffirms the identity of the person holding to truth, so that he does not win the argument but lose his heart by longing for what his opponent possesses.

Verse 39

If only you had said, when you entered your garden, ‘This is by Allah’s Will; there is no power except with Allah.’ Even though you see me as inferior to you in wealth and children,

He then moved from the root of disbelief to addressing the moment of entering the garden: “If only you had said, when you entered your garden, ‘This is by Allah’s Will; there is no power except with Allah.’” “This is by Allah’s Will” returns the blessing’s existence to His will, while “there is no power except with Allah” returns the ability to acquire and preserve it to its source. These words are not a charm for guarding possessions; they correct their owner’s vision. He did work, but his work itself never lay outside Allah’s giving. This meaning recurs in many remembrances that teach dependence at the height of ability: saying “Glory be to Him Who has subjected this for us, for we could not have done it by ourselves[7].” [43:13] when mounting a means of transport, praising Allah after food, and asking Him not to leave us to ourselves. The heart’s most dangerous moment is not only a time of helplessness. It can also be when a plan succeeds and a person feels that he has mastered the means.

Verse 40

it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden, and send upon your garden a thunderbolt from the heaven, turning it into a barren land.

The believer answers the comparison of wealth and children without a sense of inferiority: “Even though you see me as inferior to you in wealth and children”, followed by “it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden”. The present situation is not an eternal destiny. Allah, who gave to one man, can give to the other or take away from the first. This recalls the statement concerning the Romans after their defeat: “but they will gain victory after their defeat” and “at the victory granted by Allah[4]” [30:3–5]. Standards of power that seem settled may be reversed. One interpretation takes “something better than your garden” as something in this world, and another as something in the Hereafter. Both challenge the confinement of goodness to the form the rich man presently sees. Then comes “send upon your garden a thunderbolt from the heaven.” The Arabic husban, connected here with “a thunderbolt,” has been explained as a thunderbolt or a punishment sent down, or as a measured, allotted recompense. The range of interpretations itself reminds you that blessings may disappear through more avenues than their possessor can count.

Verse 41

Or its water may sink, so you will never be able to get it back.”

The collapse of a blessing may come without a visible thunderbolt. It is enough that “its water may sink, so you will never be able to get it back.” The Arabic ghawran, connected with “its water may sink,” is a verbal noun used emphatically, as though the water had become sheer, inaccessible depth. The appearance of the land and trees may remain, yet a single hidden cause disappears and the entire system collapses. This trains the heart not to be deceived by the visible surface of practical means. Projects, homes, and bodies depend upon thousands of conditions that their owners do not control. The “barren land” in the previous verse returns us to the surah’s opening: “We will surely turn all that is on it into a barren land[7].” What happens to a small garden is a model of the fate of all earthly adornment.

Verse 42

Eventually, all his produce was destroyed and he started wringing his hands [in dismay] over what he had spent on it, as it had collapsed on its trellises, and he said, “Oh, would that I had not associated anyone with my Lord!”

The transformation came suddenly: “all his produce was destroyed.” The passive construction heightens the terror of being overwhelmed: the harm came from a direction he could not repel. The same Arabic term that had expressed the abundance of his blessings in “And he had abundant fruit” now expresses his loss. He began “wringing his hands [in dismay]” over what he had spent, while the garden “had collapsed on its trellises,” a Quranic expression of complete ruin. Finally he said: “Oh, would that I had not associated anyone with my Lord!” He saw the truth after the adornment fell away; the purpose of Surah al-Kahf is to enable you to see it before the fall. The garden owner’s loss warns against placing all one’s security in wealth, a person, a job, or a platform. When that support collapses, a person’s inner world may collapse with it. External circumstances change, but people cannot take away the inner garden that comes from knowing Allah. This evokes Ibn Taymiyyah’s well-known saying about his garden being within his chest, reported by Ibn al-Qayyim in a discussion that also describes his steadfastness during imprisonment. Sound guidance means holding worldly means in your hand, rather than making your identity depend upon them.

Verse 43

He had none to support him against Allah, nor could he support himself.

The other support of which he had boasted collapsed: “He had none to support him against Allah, nor could he support himself.” Being “superior in followers” did not prevent the disaster, nor could he save himself. A community and its supporters are valuable means of getting work done. Dhul-Qarnayn himself will work with soldiers and a community. They remain means. Ultimate security comes from Allah. When Allah’s command comes, neither the group independently possesses the power to help nor the individual possesses self-sufficiency. This moment reveals who regarded a practical means as a door opened by Allah, and who regarded it as a substitute for Allah.

Verse 44

In that situation[25], the authority only rests with Allah, the True God. He is best in reward and best in outcome.

“In that situation[25], the authority only rests with Allah, the True God.” This is a particularly rich ending to the account. In Arabic, the word connected here with “the authority” has been read as both al-walayah and al-wilayah. It has been explained as support, as allegiance and returning to Him, or as sovereignty and dominion. The time indicated by “In that situation[25]” has been understood as the garden’s collapse or as the Hereafter. The meanings converge: when the powers that veiled the heart fall away, it becomes apparent that Allah truly is the helper, protector, and owner. His ownership did not begin at the collapse; rather, the illusion of independence disappeared. “He is best in reward and best in outcome.” This returns the standard to the end, rather than the present scene. The wealthy man looked better off in the immediate picture, and the believer had less wealth. What matters, however, is who will have the favorable outcome.

Verse 45

Give them the example of the life of this world: it is like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky, but soon they turn into chaff scattered by the winds. Allah has full power over all things.

After the account of the two gardens, the Quran makes the principle general: “Give them the example of the life of this world: it is like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky, but soon they turn into chaff scattered by the winds.” What is striking is that the stage of beautiful green abundance is not described at length here, as it is elsewhere, such as in Yunus: “when the earth is in its splendor and its fairest appearance” [10:24]. It is as though someone looking through the lens of al-Kahf sees directly from the first greenery to the dry chaff, without becoming lost in the stage of enchantment. The chaff is dry fragments scattered by the wind: the extreme weakness of what was alive and vigorous only a little earlier. “Allah has full power over all things.” His power is apparent not only in bringing things into existence, but also in transformation, removal, and recreation.

Verse 46

Wealth and children are the adornments of the life of this world, but the lasting righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward and better in hope.

“Wealth and children are the adornments of the life of this world” brings together the two chief sources of outward strength in the garden owner’s account: money and human support. Both are blessings, and both are adornments without permanence of their own. The hadith states: “The dead person is followed by three: His family, his wealth and his deeds. Then two of them return: His family and his wealth, and there remain only his deeds.” [Bukhari and Muslim]. The call is not to abandon wealth and children, but to turn adornment into something lasting by using it in obedience. Concerning “the lasting righteous deeds,” one general interpretation includes all righteous deeds. Some transmitted explanations particularly mention the five daily prayers; others mention glorifying Allah, praising Him, declaring His oneness and greatness, and similar acts of remembrance. The more likely understanding is that these are examples included within the expression, rather than an exhaustive definition. A passing thing acquires lasting worth when connected to a righteous deed done for Allah.

Verse 47

And [beware of] the Day when We will move the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare[26]; We will gather them all and not leave behind anyone of them.

After explaining what remains and what perishes comes a scene that removes one of the greatest symbols of stability: “the Day when We will move the mountains away.” A mountain, ordinarily a symbol of firmness, moves at Allah’s command, and the earth appears laid bare. “laid bare[26]” has been explained as level, with no one able to conceal himself upon it, or as bringing forth what lies within it. On either interpretation, the scene cuts off every worldly refuge. Then: “We will gather them all and not leave behind anyone of them.” Those whose bodies fell apart, those who drowned, and those who burned will all be gathered. The authentic hadith relates the man who, out of fear of Allah, told his sons to burn his body and scatter half over the land and half over the sea. Allah gathered him, questioned him, and forgave him because of his fear [Bukhari and Muslim]. The One who gathered that man’s scattered particles is not unable to gather all creation.

Verse 48

They will be presented before your Lord in rows, “Now you have come to Us as We created you the first time[27], although you claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.”

“They will be presented before your Lord in rows.” One interpretation takes this presentation to include all creation; another understands the context as referring particularly to those who denied, in light of what follows. In either case, the moment of presentation strips away all the layers people have constructed: wealth, lineage, followers, and soldiers. “Now you have come to Us as We created you the first time[27].” The Sunnah relates that people will be gathered “barefooted, naked, and uncircumcised” [Bukhari and Muslim]. The rich man who said, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers” stands alone without the things upon which he relied. Those who denied the appointment are then confronted: “although you claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.” What had been a thought pushed into the future has become a present line before the Lord.

Verse 49

And the record [of deeds] will be placed before them, and you will see the wicked scared of what it contains. They will say, “Woe to us! What is this book that leaves nothing, small or big, except that it has recorded it?” They will find all what they did before them, and your Lord does not wrong anyone.

“And the record [of deeds] will be placed before them.” Here the final answer sheet is disclosed, and “the wicked,” named explicitly rather than referred to by a pronoun, see what their wrongdoing has produced. Before the details are read, they are “scared of what it contains,” because a person knows much of what he has sent ahead even if he tries to forget it. Then comes the shock: “What is this book that leaves nothing, small or big, except that it has recorded it?” One explanation for mentioning the small before the great is that recording minute details causes greater astonishment. Another is that minor sins, when treated lightly, lead to major ones. Some transmitted explanations illustrate the small things with smiling or laughing during a sin. The record does not merely count the deed’s heading, but also the approval and participation accompanying it. Then the verse brings “what they did” before them. Allah needs no witness to remind Him; the deed itself is present. Alongside this precise reckoning comes “your Lord does not wrong anyone.” The terror is of being called to account, not of injustice from the One who calls to account. Among the transmitted explanations for placing the small before the great is that the soul is surprised to find details it had belittled recorded, including delight in a sin or laughter accompanying it. It has also been said that taking minor sins lightly can become a path along which a person gradually moves into major sins. The scene of the record thus seems to return to him the course of his choices, not only the headings of his actions, until he sees how what he considered fleeting accumulated.

Verse 50

And [remember] when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all prostrated except Iblīs, who was one of the jinn, but he disobeyed the command of his Lord. Will you then take him and his progeny as protectors instead of Me[28], even though they are your enemies[29]? What a terrible exchange for the wrongdoers!

After the scene of reckoning, the surah suddenly returns to the first example of arrogance: Iblis. “so they all prostrated except Iblīs,” but he “disobeyed the command of his Lord.” The root meaning of fisq, connected here with disobedience, is going outside, as though the sin took him beyond the bounds of obedience. The verse says that he “was one of the jinn.” It then rebukes those who choose him and his offspring as allies despite their enmity: “Will you then take him and his progeny as protectors instead of Me[28], even though they are your enemies[29]?” Iblis did not create you, provide for you, or possess a garden or deliverance to give you. Yet a person obeys him in opposition to the Lord who gave him everything. This is wrongdoing in its deepest sense: placing love, obedience, and allegiance where they do not belong. The connection with the garden owner is apparent. The arrogant assertion of self is ancient; whoever takes Iblis as a protector repeats his reasoning, even if dressed in wealth, an idea, or fame. The reminder of Iblis after the record that counted every deed is like a return to the beginning of the path. Iblis was deceived by himself and despised the one before whom Allah commanded him to prostrate. Many people deceived by wealth and lineage then followed his same course when they despised the people of faith. Another connection between the two scenes has been suggested: the human being whom Allah honored chose to follow a contemptible enemy. Choosing such corrupt allegiance ended in humiliation at the reckoning, rather than the dignity he had hoped for.

Verse 51

I did not call them to witness the creation of the heavens and earth, nor even the creation of their own selves, and I would not take those who misguide others as helpers[30].

“I did not call them to witness the creation of the heavens and earth, nor even the creation of their own selves” dismantles the claim to authority of those who mislead and are taken as references in place of revelation. They did not witness the beginning and do not possess comprehensive knowledge of the universe’s origin, or even of themselves. There are therefore questions about the origins of creation, language, and history for which reason alone cannot independently provide a certain answer, because it was not present. It needs to include revelation’s testimony in building knowledge, rather than arguing through a method that excludes the only source that witnessed the truth. Then: “I would not take those who misguide others as helpers[30].” Allah has no need of them as assistants. All the more should a believer avoid making someone who leads him away from Allah the pillar of his religious work. The purpose of the believer’s work governs how he uses human tools. He does not entrust them to people who undermine the foundation of that work.

Verse 52

And [beware of] the Day when He will say, “Call upon those whom you claimed to be My partners.” So they will call them, but they will not respond to them[31], and We will make them share the same doom.

On the Day of Resurrection, they are told: “Call upon those whom you claimed to be My partners.” The time has come to test the support to which the heart attached itself in this world. “So they will call them, but they will not respond to them[31].” This is more than a delayed response; the belief itself was an illusion. The Arabic mubiqan, connected in the published translation with “the same doom,” has been explained as destruction, or as a valley or barrier of punishment between the worshiper and what he worshiped. Relationships built upon associating partners with Allah end in separation and enmity, rather than companionship and support. This is the other side of righteous companionship in verse 28: someone who chooses a protector by a standard other than Allah’s will discover the worth of that choice when the real need comes.

Verse 53

The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and they will find no escape from it.

“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it.” The Arabic verb zannu, rendered here as “realize,” means certainty in this context, because the Fire has become visible rather than a report of the unseen. Then: “they will find no escape from it.” The ways of postponement available in this world have ended: no argument, change of subject, protector, or wealth. The surah that teaches from its beginning how to see the outcome before it happens now places someone who failed to do so before that outcome as a visible reality. The difference between the believer and the wrongdoer is not that one sees the Fire and the other does not; both will see it. The believer, however, accepted the report before seeing it and changed his path.

Verse 54

We have surely diversified in this Qur’an every kind of example for people[32], but man is the most quarrelsome of all beings[33].

“We have surely diversified in this Qur’an every kind of example for people[32].” The variety of accounts, images, and arguments is not needless repetition. It is a remedy whose Creator knows where to place it in the heart: an example addresses the mind, a scene awakens fear, an account opens hope, and a ruling regulates conduct. Yet “man is the most quarrelsome of all beings[33].” The problem may lie in how the mind is being used, rather than a lack of evidence. The hadith relates that the Prophet ﷺ went to Ali and Fatimah one night to encourage them to pray. When divine decree was mentioned in the response, he left, striking his thigh and saying, “man is the most quarrelsome of all beings[33].” [Bukhari]. A true statement may sometimes be used to evade what is required. The Quran should satisfy the heart so that it need not become addicted to every argument. Not every objection deserves to become a lifetime’s project. A person may argue against verses of warning because he wants only the Quran’s reassurance and separates it from fear. Yet the varied warnings, like the varied promises, address a human nature for which abstract conviction is not enough to abandon what it desires. Remembering the danger of sin and its consequences may directly deter it, just as explaining the end of a destructive path helps a person keep away before experiencing it.

Verse 55

Nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking forgiveness from their Lord except that the fate of the earlier nations should come to them, or that the punishment should come before their eyes.

After this variety of signs, the question arises: what prevented people from believing and seeking forgiveness? It was not insufficient explanation. Rather, some seem to want nothing short of “the fate of the earlier nations” coming upon them, or punishment confronting them directly. One interpretation is that they demand to see an example of the punishments that befell those before them. This resembles the obstinate statement: “O Allah, if this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the heaven” [8:32]. Demanding a tangible sign may look like seeking evidence, but when sufficient evidence has already come, it may be obstinacy rather than a search for guidance. For the wording rendered “the punishment should come before their eyes,” al-Tabari records two readings. In qubulan, both qāf and bāʾ take a short u sound. This reading refers to different kinds of punishment. In qibalan, qāf takes a short i and bāʾ a short a. This reading refers to seeing the punishment face to face. Al-Tabari also records other explanations. The message is one: do not make reaching the brink of punishment your condition for submitting to the truth. One explanation of the verse’s construction is that the real obstacle is the obstinate people’s insistence on demanding the tangible signs and punishments that befell earlier peoples. It is as though a demand for the earlier nations’ fate is understood in the wording after sufficient guidance has already arrived; it does not mean that punishment’s arrival prevented faith before it occurred.

Verse 56

We do not send the messengers except as bearers of glad tidings and warners. But those who disbelieve seek to undermine the truth with false arguments, and they take My verses and warnings in ridicule.

The messengers’ task is defined: they are sent as “bearers of glad tidings and warners.” They are not required to meet every proposal an obstinate person devises or stage a display suited to his tastes. The verse then describes a kind of argument: “those who disbelieve seek to undermine the truth with false arguments.” The Arabic root behind “undermine” carries a sense of slipping and falling. Sometimes the aim is not to build a coherent alternative theory, but to make the truth appear unstable in people’s hearts. This may happen through repeated attacks on a trusted foundation, such as the Sunnah, scholars, or revelation, without offering a method to replace it. The situation then develops into “they take My verses and warnings in ridicule.” Mocking death, the Fire, and the message is not a neutral joke. It may be a psychological means of stripping something of its seriousness so that the heart can turn away from it without pain.

Verse 57

Who could be more wrong than one who is reminded of the verses of his Lord, but he turns away from them and forgets what his hands have sent forth? We have placed veils on their hearts so that they cannot understand it, and deafness in their ears. Even if you call them to guidance, they will never be guided.

“Who could be more wrong than one who is reminded of the verses of his Lord, but he turns away from them.” The Arabic connective suggests how quickly some hearts move from being reminded to turning away, before giving themselves a sincere opportunity to understand. Then he “forgets what his hands have sent forth”: he forgets his sin and occupies himself with arguing about the evidence. As the choice is repeated, punishment follows: coverings over hearts and heaviness in ears. The veil is not evidence that the verses are unclear; it may result from accumulated earlier rejection. Hence the statement, “Even if you call them to guidance, they will never be guided” concerns those in whom this characteristic has become entrenched, rather than passing judgment on every individual disbeliever. People who once fought against Islam have entered it. The fear here is that a temporary stance of rejection may turn into a settled disposition of the heart that has no wish to listen at all.

Verse 58

But your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Lord of Mercy. If He were to take them to task for what they did, He would have hastened their punishment. Yet there is an appointed time for them from which they will find no escape.

After all the preceding disbelief, argument, and ridicule comes the surprising statement: “But your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Lord of Mercy.” Delayed punishment is neither inability nor acceptance of falsehood, but a door of mercy through which a person is given time to return. Elsewhere: “Your Lord is the possessor of infinite mercy, but His punishment cannot be averted from the wicked people.” [6:147]. Mercy and power do not contradict one another. “If He were to take them to task for what they did, He would have hastened their punishment.” If people immediately received everything they deserved, they would perish. The respite, however, has an end: “Yet there is an appointed time for them from which they will find no escape.” Do not treat a long period of faults remaining concealed as proof of safety. Make it an opportunity to repent.

Verse 59

Those are the towns We destroyed when they persisted in wrongdoing, and We set an appointed time for their destruction.

The towns Allah destroyed stand as witnesses to the preceding verse: “Those are the towns We destroyed when they persisted in wrongdoing, and We set an appointed time for their destruction.” Respite was not neglect; wrongdoing had a known term. One interpretation sees an allusion to Quraysh and the appointment of Badr. The meaning remains broader: Allah’s established way does not change merely because people do not see it carried out every day. Here a stage of the surah focused on the lens of sound guidance ends. Revelation varies its examples, the universe is full of signs, and the respite itself is a sign of mercy. For someone who continues demanding a new sign after that, the problem may lie not in the eye, but in the covering created by desire and argument.

Verse 60

And [remember] when Moses said to his servant, “I will not give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, or I travel for ages.”

The journey of knowledge begins with exceptional resolve: “I will not give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, or I travel for ages.” The Sunnah supplies the background. Moses, peace be upon him, was asked about the most knowledgeable person, and his answer identified himself. Allah admonished him for not attributing knowledge to Him and told him of a servant possessing knowledge Moses did not have. Moses asked, “O my Lord! How can I meet him?” [Bukhari 3401]. He did not become angry because someone else knew what he did not; he immediately turned the information into a journey of learning. This is a sign of a scholar devoted to Allah. The account does not mean that Khidr was superior to Moses in every respect. A person’s particular knowledge in one field does not place him above a prophet of outstanding resolve. If that knowledge had been something Moses required to convey his revealed law, Allah would have given it to him. A student therefore learns not to make general praise or his specialization a prison that prevents him from asking a colleague to teach him. Moses was accompanied by Joshua son of Nun, as he is commonly identified in the hadith: a young man suited to the journey who would later become a leader of the Children of Israel. A young learner serving at the beginning of the road must not be belittled. “I will not give up” also treats a sickness of the age of speed. Someone seeking firm knowledge cannot build himself through scattered clips alone. He must endure a long book, memorization, repetition, and years of travel along the path. Moses identified the destination to his companion and was prepared to travel “for ages.” Great knowledge requires sustained endurance. The hadith’s background also shows that the question about the most knowledgeable person came after Moses had admonished his people. A word of praise or a question voiced before someone of knowledge may test how he views what Allah has given him. Likewise, excessive praise of scholars and callers may tempt some of them without the person offering praise realizing it. Choosing the right companion is part of sound judgment: Moses chose a youth able to endure the nature of this journey and told him from the outset that the road was long. Not every righteous companion is suited to every undertaking. A companion in seeking knowledge needs different preparation from a companion serving in another field. Joshua begins the scene as a learner and attendant, then later becomes a figure of importance among the Children of Israel. An educator should not underestimate a young student’s role; he may carry a great trust after him.

Verse 61

But when they reached the junction of the two seas, they forgot their fish, which made its way into the sea, slipping away.

“But when they reached the junction of the two seas, they forgot their fish, which made its way into the sea, slipping away.” Although the youth was directly responsible for carrying the fish, the verse’s wording attributes forgetting to them both. Some commentators observe that a leader retains a share of follow-up responsibility even after delegating a task; delegation does not end responsibility. Losing the fish was the sign of the meeting. Then something extraordinary happened: the fish took its path into the sea, and the hadith indicates that the water became like a tunnel or an arch around it [Bukhari 3401]. Allah, who had previously parted the sea for Moses, could hold water around a fish to teach His prophet a new field of knowledge. A subtle point is that Moses had actually reached the desired place, then passed beyond it because the sign had not come to his attention. A person may reach the place of an opportunity without recognizing it at the time. However sincere his intention, he remains in need of Allah bringing it to his attention.

Verse 62

When they traveled further, he said to his servant, “Bring us our morning meal; this journey has truly exhausted us.”

After they passed beyond the place, Moses said: “Bring us our morning meal; this journey has truly exhausted us.” The hadith relates that this fatigue did not affect Moses until he had passed the place where he was commanded to seek Khidr [Bukhari 3401]. This is a subtle observation: some exhaustion may result from going beyond one’s appointed task, or taking upon oneself a form of religious effort that Allah has not required. Exhaustion in a good undertaking does not by itself prove that Allah wants it from you in that particular form. Sometimes we need to ask whether this really is our task, whether we have exceeded our ability, and whether we have understood Allah’s established way along the path. Distinguishing required striving from exhaustion produced by misunderstanding is part of sound guidance.

Verse 63

He said, “You remember when we rested by the rock, I forgot the fish; it was none who made me forget to mention it except Satan, and it made its way into the sea amazingly.”

The young man recalled what had happened by the rock: “I forgot the fish; it was none who made me forget to mention it except Satan.” The remarkable thing is that the event itself was extraordinary: a fish, dead or being carried with them, made its way into the sea, yet it was forgotten. This reveals Satan’s eagerness to obstruct a righteous meeting or a path of knowledge that will benefit the community. His work is not confined to direct sins; it also includes forgetfulness, distraction, and delaying a meeting that would give rise to sound guidance. Hence seeking refuge is prescribed when reading the Quran: “When you recite the Qur’an, seek refuge with Allah from the accursed Satan.” [16:98]. Forgetfulness recurs in this account until it becomes an educational theme: a person needs renewed reminders. One wisdom in regularly reading al-Kahf is that new preoccupations may cover the light learned the previous week. Some specialists in recitation have paused over the heavier pronunciation of the Arabic pronoun at a few places in Hafs’s reading, including within the expression rendered “made me forget to mention it” here. They have found a suggestive connection with the difficulty of remembering. This is a subtle observation about sound, not a definitive interpretation. The central lesson is not to trust memory alone in a time of trials. Keep a regular devotional practice, with reminders and companions who bring you back to what you have learned. Tend to your heart before the shock so that you can act on what you remember when it comes. As the report says, “Indeed, patience is at the first shock.” [Bukhari and Muslim].

Verse 64

Moses said, “That is exactly what we were looking for.” So they turned back, retracing their footsteps.

Moses immediately said: “That is exactly what we were looking for.” Then: “So they turned back, retracing their footsteps.” He did not begin a prolonged inquiry into why his companion forgot, who was responsible, or how they had lost time. As soon as the point of error became clear, they returned to put it right. This is the mindset of someone whose purpose matters more than winning a discussion over blame. The Sunnah relates that when the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions slept through the dawn prayer during a journey until the sun had risen, they moved from the place and then prayed. They dealt with the situation instead of allowing the incident to become an exhausting exchange of reproaches [Muslim]. Admitting a mistake is required, but once it is recognized, returning to the path takes priority. “they turned back” conveys a prompt response. Sound guidance does not mean never making a mistake; it means not allowing pride to prevent you from retracing your steps when you recognize the right place.

Verse 65

There they found one of Our slaves[34] upon whom We bestowed Our mercy[35] and We taught him from Our Own knowledge[36].

“There they found one of Our slaves[34] upon whom We bestowed Our mercy[35] and We taught him from Our Own knowledge[36].” Joshua recedes from the scene without a crisis; his role in this stage has ended. Part of maturity in relationships is allowing a person’s task or a period of companionship to end without conflict or a sense of betrayal. Allah then describes Khidr, as he is commonly named in the Sunnah, first of all through his servanthood. He combines for him “Our mercy[35]” and “Our Own knowledge[36],” placing mercy first. Knowledge through which some secrets of divine decrees are disclosed cannot be understood apart from Allah’s mercy. Scholars have differed at length over whether Khidr was a prophet or a righteous servant given special knowledge. On either view, this account gives no one grounds to claim knowledge of the unseen or an exemption from the revealed law. Moses himself did not accept what appeared wrong until it became clear that these actions were commanded by Allah. The purpose is to show wisdom behind reality that we do not encompass, not to open the door for people to claim knowledge of the unseen. If Moses witnessed actions whose wisdom he could not understand from their outward appearance, placing mercy before knowledge prepares the reader not to judge every unknown divine decree from its first scene. This also connects with the earlier delay of punishment for the obstinate. A believer may see wrongdoing and a respite that troubles him, without encompassing everything to which events will lead. The believer must object to wrongdoing according to the evidence before him, knowing that Allah sees beyond the event. Recognising the limits of his knowledge helps him submit to Allah while carrying out his duty.

Verse 66

Moses said to him, “May I follow you so that you may teach me some knowledge that you have been taught?”

Moses said to Khidr, “May I follow you so that you may teach me some knowledge that you have been taught?” Here is an honoured prophet making himself a follower in a particular field of knowledge, asking courteously rather than speaking as though he were entitled to it. He also traces knowledge back to Allah: “that you have been taught”. What a teacher possesses is first a gift, before it is something he can claim and boast about. More importantly, Moses did not ask for rare information for its own sake. Alongside the request for “some knowledge that you have been taught”, the Arabic word rushdan means right guidance: beneficial knowledge leads to sounder action and deeper worship. This meets the first command of revelation, “Read in the name of your Lord”, and the end of Surat al-‘Alaq, “prostrate and draw near [to Allah]”. Knowledge that does not bring us closer to Allah calls for examination. The words “so that you may teach me” set both the purpose and the condition of the journey. Moses follows Khidr to learn this particular knowledge from him. His agreement to follow extends to that purpose. It is not unconditional submission to a person.

Verse 67

He said, “You will never be able to have patience with me.”

Khidr’s answer offered neither a sales pitch nor flattery for the student: “You will never be able to have patience with me.” A trustworthy teacher does not present the path as easy simply to keep a student. He explains its cost before the student commits. Despite his determination to travel “for ages”, Moses would now face another kind of patience. Enduring travel is different from enduring the sight of something that outwardly conflicts with what you know of justice. Wisdom includes understanding the kind of difficulty ahead: some fields of learning will unsettle your understanding; efforts to call people to Allah may bear fruit slowly; attempts to guide and nurture others may fail. Not every difficulty is evidence that the path itself is false.

Verse 68

How can you have patience with that which you have no knowledge about?”

Khidr explained why: “How can you have patience with that which you have no knowledge about?” Much of a person’s pain over Allah’s decree comes from seeing a single scene without seeing the whole story. He knows about the hole in the boat but not the king who seizes boats; he sees the boy’s death but not what the boy’s future would have brought upon his parents; he sees a wall being repaired but not the two orphans’ treasure. The concealment of the unseen is itself a mercy. If a person knew every loss, illness and death awaiting him before it came, he might be unable to go about ordinary life. Moses will object to what he sees as injustice. Reason stays alert to what is visible while learning the limits of its knowledge of Allah’s actions. Wisdom may be present even when you cannot see it.

Verse 69

Moses said, “You will find me patient, if Allah wills; and I will not disobey any of your orders.”

Moses said, “You will find me patient, if Allah wills; and I will not disobey any of your orders.” He combined firm determination with complete dependence on Allah. He did not rely on himself and claim that he would certainly be patient, nor did he use “if Allah wills” as an excuse for a weak commitment. He made a real promise, then referred his ability to fulfil it to Allah’s will. This continues the lesson earlier in the surah about making our plans subject to Allah’s will. We need this balance in learning and in long projects: a clear commitment, a schedule and discipline, together with continual prayer that Allah will not leave us to ourselves.

Verse 70

He said, “Then if you follow me, do not ask me about anything until I mention it to you.”

Khidr laid down the rule for their companionship: “Then if you follow me, do not ask me about anything until I mention it to you.” A clear agreement before work begins prevents many clashes. The teacher knows that the nature of the lesson will provoke Moses’s questions, so he explains when questions may be asked. This does not make silence an absolute virtue in every form of teaching. It is a condition of this particular experience, whose explanation will come at the end. Part of understanding an educational relationship is knowing when to ask and when to wait for the picture to take shape, without forcing the teacher to rearrange the course whenever an unfinished part makes the student uneasy. In return, the teacher must state the rules openly, rather than punish a student for expectations he never explained.

Verse 71

So they both set out, until when they boarded a ship, he made a hole in it. Moses said, “Did you make a hole in it to drown its people? You have done something terrible!”

They boarded a ship. The authentic report relates that its crew recognised Khidr and gave the two of them passage without payment; then Khidr made a hole in it [al-Bukhari: 3401]. This makes Moses’s objection all the more understandable: these people had treated them well, and now their ship was being damaged! He said, “Did you make a hole in it to drown its people?” His objection named the ship’s people. The author contrasts this with a hypothetical concern expressed in the first-person plural—that we might drown. Moses’s concern was for the ship’s people, rather than merely for himself and his companion. His objection arose from an instinct for justice and mercy, not self-interest. Moses’ moral judgment had value on the evidence before him, even though his knowledge was incomplete. He saw the hole in the ship. He could not see the king ahead. That gap shows why the meaning of a particular decree cannot always be read from one moment’s outward appearance. Moses was right to object according to the knowledge available to him, while Khidr was carrying out a command Allah had made known to him. The ending will show that, once the information was complete, these two perspectives did not contradict one another.

Verse 72

He said, “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?”

Khidr said, “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?” He did not offer an early explanation simply to end the tension, because their agreement was that the explanation would come in its own time. Some teachers undermine a course because they cannot bear a student’s discomfort: they reveal the conclusions before the student has experienced the question. Khidr kept to the plan. For Moses, this was a moment of discovering that sincere intention and determination do not guarantee flawless performance. A single experience can reveal something about ourselves that we had not known before.

Verse 73

Moses said, “Do not take me to task for what I forgot, and do not make it too difficult for me to follow you.”

Moses did not launch into a lengthy defence of himself. He said, “Do not take me to task for what I forgot, and do not make it too difficult for me to follow you.” His apology was clear: he had forgotten, and he wanted to continue. Maturity does not mean never making a mistake in a relationship. It means acknowledging a breach without dressing it up and asking for a fair chance to carry on. Nor did the teacher turn the first breach into expulsion; there remained room for human error. This balance protects relationships from two extremes: leniency that erases boundaries, and harshness that treats the first slip as the end of every companionship.

Verse 74

Then they proceeded until they met a boy, and the man killed him. Moses said, “Did you kill an innocent soul who killed none? You have done something monstrous!”

The second scene was incomparably more severe: “they met a boy, and the man killed him”. Reports differ in describing how the killing took place, so there is no need to insist on a detail that has not been established. The point is that, in Moses’s eyes, an innocent soul had been killed without having killed anyone. This time he described the act as “something monstrous”, stronger than “something terrible” at the ship. A hole can be repaired; in ordinary human experience, death cannot be reversed. Among the accounts related are descriptions of a good-looking boy at play, in whom his parents delighted. If established, this picture makes the question still more painful: how could mercy lie behind such a loss? Here the story brings faith in Allah’s decree to one of its hardest points. Reason is commanded to think and learn, yet it too is Allah’s servant; it must not turn its own inability to perceive wisdom into a judgment against Allah’s wisdom. Moses objected to the wrong he saw. His response to the boy’s killing showed that his moral sense was alive, even though this particular action, commanded by Allah, rested on knowledge we do not possess. There is an immense difference between submitting to Allah’s decree and using that decree to justify human oppression.

Verse 75

The man said, “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?”

The second reminder was stronger: “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?” After the repeated breach, the Arabic adds laka, meaning to you, an explicit address to Moses. The published English renders the dialogue here and in verse 72 in the same words, so the added emphasis is a feature of the Arabic expression. Relationships have a memory: repetition naturally changes the level of a warning. Khidr still did not explain, because their agreement remained in force, but he brought Moses face to face with the fact that his promise was harder to fulfil than he had expected. On the path of learning, a person may understand a rule’s value in theory yet need to encounter its demands more than once before appreciating its practical weight.

Verse 76

Moses said, “If I ask you about anything after this, do not keep me in your company, for then you would be excused concerning me.”

Here Moses set a limit for himself: “If I ask you about anything after this, do not keep me in your company, for then you would be excused concerning me.” It is generous to recognise when a relationship is becoming draining for both sides, and to acknowledge that the other person would be justified in ending it. Not every path suits every person, and not every teacher or method suits every student. Discovering that a particular path is not yours may be sound judgment rather than failure. In the hadith, the Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, wished that Moses had remained patient so that Allah would tell us more of their story [reported by al-Bukhari and Muslim]. The lesson also teaches the value of sustained patience: beyond the next obstacle there might have been another wonder we had not yet seen. But events unfolded as decreed, and the journey would end without a quarrel. A great relationship can fulfil its purpose and then be brought courteously to a close.

Verse 77

Then they went on until they came to the people of a town[37]. They asked its people for food, but they refused to offer them hospitality. They found there a wall that was about to collapse, but he repaired it. Moses said, “If you wished, you could have taken some payment for it.”

They entered a town: “They asked its people for food, but they refused to offer them hospitality.” Asking for hospitality was not humiliation; guests and travellers have rights in Islamic teaching. The expression suggests that they asked the people of the whole town and found no generosity. Despite hunger and fatigue, they found a wall about to collapse, and Khidr repaired it. Reports and interpretations differ over how he did this: whether he passed his hand over it or rebuilt it through hard work. The meaning does not depend on that detail. What matters is the strangeness of the situation: people withheld food, yet Khidr offered them a service without payment. Moses then said, “If you wished, you could have taken some payment for it.” This question seems much milder than the objection to the killing, but it was the third question, after which Moses had bound himself to ending the companionship. Sometimes the problem is not the size of the final breach, but repeatedly departing from an agreed condition.

Verse 78

The man said, “This is the parting of ways between me and you. I will inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.

Khidr said, “This is the parting of ways between me and you.” The two men parted without a quarrel or attempts to discredit one another. He then said, “I will inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.” Part of honouring a companionship is explaining why it is ending, rather than leaving the other person struggling with bitter guesses. This moment resembles what everyone wishes for concerning their own allotted trials. If every calamity ended with a written explanation of the illness, the loss and all the rest, the heart would find relief. Yet such full disclosure is generally deferred to the Hereafter. Khidr’s story opens only a small window, showing us that mercy may lie behind what we cannot understand today. We are not asked to invent an explanation for every calamity. We are asked to trust that our ignorance of the explanation does not rule out wisdom.

Verse 79

“As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people who worked at sea. I wanted to make it defective because there was a king ahead of them who seized every [good] ship by force.

He began with the event easiest to understand once its background was revealed: “As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people who worked at sea. I wanted to make it defective because there was a king ahead of them who seized every [good] ship by force.” An explanatory reported reading makes explicit that the ships seized were sound ones; the English translation marks this clarification with the brackets in “[good]”. That reported reading remains distinct from the Quranic text. The hole was an immediate worldly defect that would preserve a greater worldly benefit later. The ship’s people may have wept over the damage and feared for their trade and reputation. Then, when the usurping king arrived, they could discover that the defect that had made them weep had saved their very livelihood. The ship was a worldly means of livelihood. Limited damage to it prevented it from being seized altogether. Weighing benefits and harms in this way requires knowledge and devotion to Allah. Preserving faith comes first. An appeal to benefit cannot justify undermining it. Likewise, a loss you dislike in your wealth, health or plans may be a means by which Allah averts a greater harm you cannot see.

Verse 80

“As for the boy, his parents were believers, and we feared that he would overburden them with his rebellion and disbelief[38].

“As for the boy, his parents were believers, and we feared that he would overburden them with his rebellion and disbelief[38].” Here the benefit rises from protecting wealth to protecting faith. The concern was not that he would cost his parents more money or trouble them with ordinary misbehaviour. It was that their love for him would draw them into what would burden their faith. The pain was therefore greater than the damage to the ship: when what is being preserved is more precious, the trial may outwardly be more painful. This verse does not permit any person to kill on the basis of a prediction about the future. Khidr acted under a particular command from Allah, as he would explain in verse 82. In reflecting on the decree, however, it teaches that the wisdom behind some losses may never become visible in this world. The ship’s owners might discover the reason for its damage that very day; the boy’s parents could not know what their son would have become unless Allah disclosed it. Not every question will receive its answer in this life.

Verse 81

So we hoped that their Lord would give them another in his place, more righteous and tender-hearted.

“So we hoped that their Lord would give them another in his place, more righteous and tender-hearted.” The loss was not an everlasting emptiness; a replacement lay beyond it. Accounts relate that Allah gave the parents a daughter, or a righteous son from whose descendants a prophet came. These reports should not be asserted with certainty. The text is sufficient: greater righteousness and tenderness. There is a beautiful connection here. At the killing, Moses had spoken of “an innocent soul”; in the explanation of the unseen comes “more righteous”. The Arabic expressions draw on the same root of purity and righteousness. What looks pure from the present outward appearance is not the whole measure; Allah knows what a soul will become. A few words cannot take away the pain of bereavement. The account invites the believer to look beyond the particular loss. Goodness with Allah embraces faith, the final outcome, and mercy.

Verse 82

As for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and there was a treasure under it that belonged to them. Their father was a righteous man, so your Lord willed that they should reach their maturity and retrieve their treasure[39], as a mercy from your Lord; I did not do it of my own accord. This is the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.”

The wall belonged to two orphan boys, and their treasure lay beneath it. “Their father was a righteous man”: righteousness in the past continued to protect children in the future. One of the early Muslims is reported to have increased his worship in the hope that his child would thereby be protected. It has also been said that the father mentioned was a more distant ancestor. If established, that would show the effects of good deeds extending across generations. Then comes: “so your Lord willed that they should reach their maturity and retrieve their treasure[39], as a mercy from your Lord”. One interpretation connects this mercy specifically with the third event; Ibn Kathir and others understand it to encompass all three. This is a deeply beautiful understanding: mercy lay behind the hole in the ship, behind the boy’s death despite its terrible appearance, and behind the repair of the wall. This is more than detached wisdom. It is mercy, some of which may become visible in this world while some is deferred to the Hereafter. The three stories show different levels: a worldly defect preserves a greater worldly good; a grievous loss protects faith; and past righteousness preserves a future livelihood. Then Khidr closes the door to false claims: “I did not do it of my own accord.” No one can claim knowledge of the unseen by treating himself as another Khidr. His expressions also show courtesy in attributing actions. Of the ship he said, “I wanted to make it defective”, attributing the defect to himself. In the case of unalloyed good he said, “your Lord willed”; of the boy, “So we hoped”. The Arabic behind this last expression uses aradnā, a first-person plural form of the same verb of willing used in the ship and wall passages. The published English “So we hoped” remains distinct from that Arabic gloss. Commentators discuss several aspects of the courtesy reflected in these different attributions. The story ends by training reason to submit to Allah: “Indeed, 'if' opens the way before the devil to act.” [Muslim: 2664]. Repeatedly brooding over how things would have turned out if one had acted differently, after the decree has already taken effect, may produce regret rather than knowledge. This is not a rejection of useful planning or learning from experience. When another event like the ship, the boy or the wall comes into your life and you do not know its explanation, do not invent a story merely to reassure yourself. Acknowledge that Allah has wisdom and mercy you cannot encompass, and be patient. The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, said: “How astonishing the believer's affair is! Indeed, all of his affairs are good, and this is for none but the believer. If something good happens to him, he is grateful, which is good for him; and if something bad happens to him, he shows patience, which is good for him.” [Muslim: 2999]. The three stories differ in when and how much of the wisdom anyone can see. Some of it may become clear soon, and some may remain unseen. A sufferer has no promise of seeing the benefit of every calamity before death. The ship’s people could recognise its preservation from seizure when the danger passed. The boy’s parents could not know the future he would otherwise have had. The treasure’s benefit was delayed until the orphans reached maturity.

Verse 83

They ask you about Dhul-Qarnayn[40]. Say, “I will tell you something about him.”

Unlike the transitions between the earlier stories, there is no long interval between the end of the account of Moses and Khidr and the beginning of Dhul-Qarnayn’s. Al-Biqa‘i draws attention to a beautiful connection: a righteous servant travels the earth seeking knowledge, then another righteous servant travels it striving in Allah’s cause and establishing justice. Sound knowledge is not meant to remain an intellectual pleasure. It prepares a person to act with understanding in the real world. The story begins with a question: “They ask you about Dhul-Qarnayn[40].” Reports link the question to Quraysh consulting the People of the Book. They differ over whether those who prompted it were Jewish or Christian, and the verse does not settle their identity. The Quran places the answer about the spirit in Surat al-Isra’, separately from the stories of the Cave and Dhul-Qarnayn. This invites reflection without requiring us to claim a definitive explanation. Then comes the courtesy of answering: “I will tell you something about him.” Something about him, rather than his entire life. The Quran does not turn the account into an entertaining encyclopaedia of his name and dates. It selects what serves as a reminder. Someone calling others to Allah may face a question intended to test him or draw him into unhelpful detail. He need not answer immediately or tell everything he knows. He can choose a truthful part of the account that offers guidance, say “I do not know” where the facts are unknown or unestablished, and keep his listeners’ attention on the lesson.

Verse 84

We established him on earth and gave him the means to achieve everything[41].

The first thing the Quran says about the ruler who reached east and west is not that he conquered or triumphed: “We established him on earth and gave him the means to achieve everything[41].” This is how a believer reads history: power is granted by Allah, and He can take away the dominion a person holds just as He gave it. “You give dominion to whom You will and take it away from whom You will” [Al ‘Imran: 26]. The extent of a kingdom alone does not prove Allah’s approval. Under the patterns governing worldly life, means may be given to a believer or a disbeliever, and every nation has its appointed term. The reader therefore need not settle whether Dhul-Qarnayn was Alexander, a king from Yemen, or a ruler from China. The safer description is a believing, just ruler whose story Allah has told us to the extent we need. Of messengers themselves He says, “messengers We have not mentioned to you” [an-Nisa’: 164]. How, then, could we imagine that we possess the whole of history? The Arabic word sabab, rendered here through “the means”, is what enables someone to reach a goal. Its meanings include knowledge, capacity and tools. Empowerment did not come by abolishing means. Allah gave him knowledge, strength and resources, which he would then be required to use. Knowing that Allah grants power does not produce passivity. It keeps us from worshipping the means while we use them.

Verse 85

He pursued a course[42],

“He pursued a course[42]”: he did not sit back and assume that, since Allah had empowered him, results would come by themselves. He brought together the knowledge, capacity and tools he had received, organised them and acted towards a goal. This is a decisive point. The modern world may possess enormous means yet lose sight of purpose: knowledge, technology, economic resources and power, but where are they leading? Prosperity itself becomes a goal without an answer about what lies beyond it, and the means turn into competition and consumption. The Arabic word sabab, rendered here as “a course[42]” and in verse 84 as “the means”, can evoke a rope held to reach water in a well: its value lies in helping you reach a goal. The Quran’s passages about power are therefore connected with purpose. In Surat al-Hajj, whose name itself carries the sense of intending a destination, permission to fight appears; power is not for its own sake. Dhul-Qarnayn knows his direction and employs what he has for Allah. He used those means to their full extent, reaching the setting place of the sun, its rising place, and the pass between the two mountain barriers. Ask yourself not only what talents you have, but also what purpose your knowledge and energy serve.

Verse 86

until when he reached the far west, he found the sun setting in a dark body of water, and he found some people nearby. We said, “O Dhul-Qarnayn, either punish them or treat them with kindness.”

“until when he reached the far west, he found the sun setting in a dark body of water” describes the scene as an observer looking towards the horizon would see it. Today we likewise speak of seeing the sun set into the sea without meaning that it physically enters the water. Ordinary human language is a natural way of describing what is seen, rather than a scientific mistake. More importantly, at the far west “he found some people nearby”. The account does not say he found a mineral deposit, treasure or wealth. His real concern was people, that they should worship Allah and be freed from injustice. This distinguishes a mission of calling people to Allah and using power well from colonial exploitation that treats land as a store of resources and human beings as a means of profit. He was then given a choice about how to deal with them. Two possibilities are mentioned: inspiration or a message conveyed by a prophet accompanying him. His own prophethood cannot be established from this address. The account draws attention to what he did with the power he held, while the precise way the message reached him remains uncertain.

Verse 87

He said, “As for one who does wrong[43], we will punish him, then he will be brought back to his Lord, and He will punish him grievously.

Dhul-Qarnayn’s first clear statement reveals his understanding of rule: “As for one who does wrong[43], we will punish him, then he will be brought back to his Lord, and He will punish him grievously.” Many commentators explain wrongdoing here as persisting in disbelief after the truth has been made clear, because the next verse sets against it the person who believes and does righteous deeds. The Arabic expression about future punishment has also been understood to allow time for calling them to the truth before punishment. His power was not an explosion of anger, but an ordered responsibility: the truth is presented, and those who persist in aggression and disbelief after clarification face what Allah has authorised. He then directs them towards the Hereafter, lest they suppose that a king’s punishment is the ultimate thing to fear. However severe worldly punishment may be, the Lord’s judgment still lies ahead. A believing leader does not make himself the final centre of fear. He uses what he possesses to turn people back to Allah.

Verse 88

But he who believes and does righteous deeds, he will have the best reward[44], and we will enjoin upon him to do what is easy.”

On the other side: “But he who believes and does righteous deeds, he will have the best reward[44], and we will enjoin upon him to do what is easy.” The order is deeply significant. First comes the greatest reward, “the best reward[44]” with Allah; then comes ease in Dhul-Qarnayn’s treatment of him. A believer does not embrace faith in pursuit of state privileges or closeness to a ruler. He believes for Allah, and the ease brought by just treatment follows as a worldly effect. With wrongdoers, Dhul-Qarnayn began with the immediate punishment and then reminded them of the Hereafter, because a weak heart may first respond to a nearby fear. With the believer, he directs sincerity from the outset towards the reward with Allah. In this way, administration becomes a means of nurturing a purpose higher than the ruler himself, rather than purchasing loyalties.

Verse 89

Then he pursued another course,

Again, “Then he pursued another course”. When the western task was complete, he moved on to another instead of lingering over his achievement. There is room between tasks for the rest the body needs. This meets the lesson of “So whenever you are free[5], strive in worship”, followed by “and turn to your Lord in devotion.” [ash-Sharh: 7–8]. Over a lifetime, worship can lead into calling others to Allah, knowledge into action, and lawful earnings into service. Each can bring us closer to Him. A person who lives this way does not wait for the perfect lifetime project before beginning. He moves from one field of service to another, knowing that true permanence belongs to Allah and that his time is limited capital.

Verse 90

until when he reached the far east, he found the sun rising on a people for whom We provided no shelter from it[45].

He reached the rising place of the sun and found people “for whom We provided no shelter from it[45]”. Outwardly, their way of life was extremely simple and their resources few. Yet he did not ask what he could gain from people who lacked even buildings to protect them from the sun. Human beings remained the focus of his mission, rather than the amount of wealth he could extract from them. This offers a counterexample to colonial experiences that made weak communities sources of raw materials and labour while denying them the capacity to escape dependency. Dhul-Qarnayn’s power, by contrast, spreads worship of Allah alone and establishes justice. The following verse begins, “So it was”. One interpretation is that he treated the people of the east as he had those of the west: a consistent method, unchanged by whether the people were strong or weak. Another is that matters were simply as described. Justice that changes according to an opponent’s strength is not justice. A mission that respects the wealthy and exploits the weak is not Dhul-Qarnayn’s mission.

Verse 91

So it was, and We had full knowledge about him[46].

“We had full knowledge about him[46]” stands above the whole scene of empowerment. One interpretation concerns Allah’s encompassing knowledge of the place Dhul-Qarnayn reached. Another, with a powerful lesson for the heart, concerns everything Dhul-Qarnayn himself possessed: equipment, soldiers, knowledge and faith. As a servant’s fame and power expand, so does his need to remember that he remains within Allah’s knowledge and under His watch. Success does not cancel accountability. Allah said to His Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, “If We willed, We could surely take away what We have revealed to you” [al-Isra’: 86]. What, then, of anyone else? The verse has also been understood as an honour: Allah knew the righteousness that fitted him for this position. On either interpretation, empowerment never falls outside divine knowledge. Allah knows best where to place His favour and how its recipient uses it. Remembering this encompassing knowledge should remind a famous or influential person that expanding victories do not reduce the watch over him. Even what people know of his actions and words falls short of Allah’s knowledge of his intentions and resources. In the exhilaration of success, heedlessness may lead someone to attribute favour entirely to himself, although Allah’s knowledge was present before his rise, throughout it and afterwards.

Verse 92

Then he pursued another course,

For the third time: “Then he pursued another course”. The repetition itself draws his character. This was no lucky stroke in the west or single campaign that made his name, but a sustained method: a purpose, then means, then arrival, then dealing justly with people, then renewed movement. In the preceding story, Moses set out in search of knowledge: “I will not give up until I reach”. Here the setting out is for action and justice. The surah seeks a believer whose principles turn into movement, rather than one who becomes briefly enthusiastic and then returns to spiritual idleness. The field of service differs according to what Allah makes possible for each person.

Verse 93

until when he reached [a valley] between the two mountains, he found beyond them a people who could barely understand a word[47].

At the two mountain barriers he found a people “who could barely understand a word[47]”. Even communication was difficult. This was not an advanced city promising political gain, but a community with few resources to help itself. Even so, he stopped to address its need. A person with material power who has lost the guidance of revelation often asks what use such people are to him. A believer asks what his duty towards them is. Both sides needed to make an effort to understand one another. That itself offers a lesson for calling people to Allah and helping communities develop: a different language or limited education is no reason to leave people out of account. It is a reason to seek a means of bringing justice and knowledge to them.

Verse 94

They said, “O Dhul-Qarnayn, Gog and Magog are spreading corruption in the land. Can we give you some payment in return for you to construct a barrier between us and them?”

The people explained their problem clearly: “Gog and Magog are spreading corruption in the land. Can we give you some payment in return for you to construct a barrier between us and them?” Their diagnosis was sound: aggression required a barrier. Yet their first idea for a solution was to purchase a ruler’s service. The idea of building a barrier was not itself wrong. What Dhul-Qarnayn would change was the way it was carried out and the community’s relationship to the project. Within the surah’s structure there is a connection with the trials of the Dajjal and Gog and Magog at the end of time. Not every trial is dealt with in the same way. The Dajjal is confronted until Jesus, peace be upon him, kills him. When Gog and Magog emerge, however, Allah commands Jesus and the believers to seek safety at Tur; Allah then destroys them [Muslim]. Likewise, some trials in life call for confrontation, while others call for a barrier between you and the danger, or for moving away. Sound judgment means recognising the kind of struggle before you, rather than making confrontation an automatic answer to every problem.

Verse 95

He said, “What my Lord has given me is better. But help me with manpower, I will construct a barricade between you and them.

He said, “What my Lord has given me is better.” He did not see empowerment as a way to collect money from the weak. This recalls Solomon, peace be upon him: “Do you seek to appease me with wealth? What Allah has given me is far better than what He has given you.” [an-Naml: 36]. Nor did Dhul-Qarnayn do everything while they watched. He said, “But help me with manpower”. This changes the relationship from paying someone to make something for you to joining in building it. Good leadership does not create a dependent community that cannot work for itself. It shares expertise and makes use of people’s abilities. Much backwardness begins with repeatedly buying a finished product instead of learning the knowledge that produces it. The people already had the idea of a barrier. He respected the sound idea and supplied the knowledge and organisation needed to make it real. Then he promised, “I will construct a barricade between you and them.” The Arabic word radm, rendered here as “barricade”, can in some linguistic uses suggest something stronger than a simple barrier, as though the gap were filled and firmly sealed.

Verse 96

Bring me iron blocks” – until when he leveled between the two mountainsides, he said, “Blow [with bellows],” until when he fired them up to extreme heat, he said, “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.”

The Quran describes the stages of the work in unusual detail for a short story: “Bring me iron blocks”, “Blow [with bellows]”, and “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.” It could simply have said that he built them a barrier, but the detail establishes that faith does not conflict with technology, engineering and precision. These details can themselves become worship when directed towards what Allah wants. This recalls David, peace be upon him: “And We made iron malleable for him” and “Make coats of mail, and maintain balance in the linking rings” [Saba’: 10–11]. A prophet with whom mountains and birds glorify Allah is then commanded to measure the links of armour precisely. There is no conflict between humble night prayer and hours spent on calculation, equations and experimentation. The project also shows a division of labour: the community brings iron and blows the bellows; Dhul-Qarnayn directs the knowledge and design; then another material is used to strengthen the construction. A sound intention does not replace good execution.

Verse 97

Thus they could not climb over it nor could they pierce it[48].

The outcome is expressed in two parts: “Thus they could not climb over it nor could they pierce it[48].” They could neither get over the top nor make a breach through it. The two Arabic ability forms اسطاعوا and استطاعوا both mean were able, corresponding to could in each part of the published English. Scholars of Arabic have reflected on the shorter ability form اسطاعوا in the clause about “climb over it” and the fuller form استطاعوا in the clause about “pierce it[48]”, connecting the former with the lighter action of climbing and the latter with the harder action of boring through. They have seen in the extra form a correspondence with extra difficulty. This is a rhetorical observation, not something on which the basic meaning depends. The modern illusion of having discovered every point on earth and every page of history is an illusion the surah challenges from the story of the Cave onwards.

Verse 98

He said, “This is a mercy from my Lord. But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass[49], He will raze it to the ground. The promise of my Lord is ever true.”

After the story’s greatest feat of engineering, he did not claim it simply as his own achievement. He said, “This is a mercy from my Lord.” Here the powerful contrast with the owner of the two gardens becomes clear. That man looked at his achievement and said, “I do not think that this will ever perish”; Dhul-Qarnayn employed knowledge, effort and soldiers, then attributed the result to his Lord’s mercy. This is the very meaning the garden owner should have expressed: “This is by Allah’s Will; there is no power except with Allah.” Dhul-Qarnayn went further, refusing to imagine that the barrier would last forever: “But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass[49], He will raze it to the ground. The promise of my Lord is ever true.” A believer builds with the greatest possible skill, then knows that the lifespan of every structure rests in Allah’s hands. This does not weaken his work; it protects him from turning it into an inward idol. Even the supplication after food refers power back to Allah. A person says: “Praise be to Allah who fed me and provided me with this food through no might or power on my part”. Our effort is real, but behind it lie the empowerment and mercy without which that effort would bear no fruit.

Verse 99

On that Day[50] We will let some of them surge like waves over others, and the Trumpet will be blown, then We will gather them all together.

“On that Day[50] We will let some of them surge like waves over others” is among the passages where interpretations differ according to the referents of its pronouns. One view takes the people to be Gog and Magog and the day to be that of the barrier’s opening, when they surge among other people or among themselves. Another understands them as having been left after the barrier’s construction to multiply and surge among one another until the promised time. A third takes the people to be all creation and the day to be the Day of Resurrection, an interpretation preferred by a number of commentators. These are interpretive alternatives; the published note here identifies the day with the barrier’s destruction. The context then makes its transition explicit: “and the Trumpet will be blown, then We will gather them all together.” After the journey of a ruler possessing an army, a barrier and territories, the scene ends with a gathering that only Allah controls. However great a human gathering may be, it stands before a single blast that will end the whole earthly order.

Verse 100

On that Day We will show Hell to the disbelievers clearly[51],

“On that Day We will show Hell to the disbelievers clearly[51]”: what had been an account of the unseen will stand openly before them, impossible to turn their eyes away from. An authentic report describes Hell as having seventy thousand reins, with seventy thousand angels at each rein drawing it [Muslim]. This restores the Hereafter’s true scale in the heart after desires have made it seem small. The surah is full of astonishing scenes: a cave, a ship and a barrier. Yet these must not become, in our minds, the most wondrous of Allah’s signs in a way that makes us forget the Fire, the bridge over it and the meeting with Him. Every effort a person made to keep the Hereafter far from thought collapses when the Fire is plainly displayed before his eyes.

Verse 101

those whose eyes were blind to My signs[52] and they were unable to hear [the truth][53].

The verse explains why they had failed to see before: “those whose eyes were blind to My signs[52]”. The Arabic image places their eyes within a covering, rather than merely describing a cover laid across them. It suggests enclosure by desire, doubt and distraction, so that the signs in creation and the Quran pass before them daily without being seen for what they are. A beating heart, renewing cells, the sun, plants and revelation surround them, yet the person within the covering asks where the signs are. After the eyes comes hearing: “they were unable to hear [the truth][53].” One interpretation is that their absorption in disbelief prevented them from listening; another is that this was the result of the sealing imposed as a punishment. They may be understood as cause and consequence: the heart chose distraction and aversion until it became unable to hear in a beneficial way. One of the gravest forms of trial is failing to realise that you are being tried because your very means of perception have come within the covering.

Verse 102

Do the disbelievers think that they can take My slaves as allies instead of Me? We have prepared Hell as a dwelling place for the disbelievers.

“Do the disbelievers think that they can take My slaves as allies instead of Me?” One interpretation supplies an unspoken conclusion to the question: do they think they can do this and escape punishment? Another understands the question as asking whether those allies will benefit them. Both destroy the illusion of relying on anyone apart from Allah. Then comes: “We have prepared Hell as a dwelling place for the disbelievers.” The Arabic word nuzul, rendered here as “a dwelling place”, can refer to what is prepared for a guest upon arrival. The expression therefore carries a bitter reversal. Someone who imagined having assembled a network of allies to welcome him into safety finds that what has been prepared for him is Hell. Companionship and allegiance recur throughout the surah: young companions are saved, a believing companion offers counsel, Khidr teaches, and supposed partners fail to answer. Choose whom you take as an ally before the moment when you truly need one.

Verse 103

Say, “Shall we inform you of the greatest losers in terms of their deeds?

“Shall we inform you of the greatest losers in terms of their deeds?” It does not describe people who do nothing. They have deeds, perhaps a great many, and lives full of activity and achievement. The surah has shown tremendous effort in the service of truth: young believers leaving their homeland, Moses travelling for knowledge, and Dhul-Qarnayn crossing east and west and building a barrier. That same energy can be spent on a false path. The gravest loss, therefore, is not laziness alone. It can be intense activity in the wrong direction. The number of projects, qualifications, writings and campaigns does not by itself answer the question of value. Where did you learn which path to take, and towards what end are you moving?

Verse 104

Those whose efforts in the life of this world are wasted, while they think that they are doing good[54].”

These are “Those whose efforts in the life of this world are wasted, while they think that they are doing good[54].” A person may persist on a path after Allah has repeatedly made things clear to him, and so come under the pattern expressed in “We will leave him to what he has chosen[83]” [an-Nisa’: 115] and “We will make easy for him the way of perdition.” [al-Layl: 10]. This is not because he was given no opportunity. He continued choosing until falsehood became attractive to him. Delusion can reach the point where someone asks Allah to support him against the truth, as is reported of Abu Jahl’s supplication before Badr while he imagined himself more faithful to kinship and more entitled to prevail. Feeling sincere is not enough. Protection from this fate appears in two things at the end of the surah: Allah’s signs, which govern the path, and the meeting with Allah, which governs the goal. Someone who continually examines himself in the light of revelation and remembers the reckoning does not easily become complacent about his own achievements. A person may have formidable intelligence, argumentative skill and ability to act, yet the clear proofs of truth may be hidden from him, while someone with less wealth and intelligence is enabled to see them. Guidance is not a prize obtained by mental power alone. Fear that error may become attractive to us calls especially for the supplication of the Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, in his night prayer: “Guide me, by Your permission, to the truth about which people differ. Verily, You guide whom You will to the straight path!” [Muslim: 770]. In this way, someone engaged in work or a project remains dependent on his Lord’s guidance while reviewing his direction, rather than reassured merely by the feeling that he is doing well.

Verse 105

It is those who disbelieve in the verses of their Lord and their meeting with Him; their deeds will be nullified, and We will give no weight to them on the Day of Resurrection[55].

The cause of the loss is revealed: “It is those who disbelieve in the verses of their Lord and their meeting with Him; their deeds will be nullified”. Both source and goal have been lost. The signs would have taught them how to act, and the meeting would have taught them for whom and why to act. When revelation and the Hereafter disappear from view, activity itself can produce catastrophe. Concerning “We will give no weight to them on the Day of Resurrection[55]”, one interpretation is that neither they nor their deeds have weight because of their utter insignificance; another is that their deeds will be weighed and their scales found light. Both end in a lack of value before Allah, however impressive their worldly appearance. This is the final correction to the temptation of having more and the temptation of possessing means. True weight is not what the public sees, but what Allah’s balance establishes.

Verse 106

That is their recompense: Hell, because of their disbelief and taking My verses and messengers in ridicule.

“That is their recompense: Hell, because of their disbelief and taking My verses and messengers in ridicule.” The expression “taking My verses and messengers in ridicule” is stronger than a passing lapse into laughter. Mockery has become a stance, a habit, or even a trade from which a person makes a living. Ridiculing religion, the Hereafter and the messengers can become content, a job and a social pattern. The heart learns to meet every admonition with a joke so that awe cannot enter it. The surah that opened with “What a monstrous word” ends here by returning to responsibility for words. Words may become a reminder that gives life, or ridicule that forms a covering over the eyes until its speaker arrives at the very thing he mocked.

Verse 107

As for those who believe and do righteous deeds, they will have gardens of Paradise[56] as a dwelling place[57],

On the other side: “As for those who believe and do righteous deeds, they will have gardens of Paradise[56] as a dwelling place[57]”. The surah does not set idleness against misguided deeds. It sets righteous deeds directed by faith against them. The person performing them may be unknown to others; not every hero’s name is preserved in books. Unnamed soldiers at Yarmuk and al-Qadisiyyah, someone giving charity in secret, a mother nurturing her children, and an uncelebrated student of knowledge can all come within righteous action when they are sincere. Al-Firdaws, identified here in the published note on “Paradise[56]”, occurs by this name in the Quran only here and in Surat al-Mu’minun. The hadith states: “So, when you ask Allah (for something), ask for Al-firdaus which is the best and highest part of Paradise.” [al-Bukhari: 2790]. The report also mentions the Throne of the Most Merciful above it; the sub-narrator expresses uncertainty when recalling this statement. After all the surah’s movement, from a cave to travel to striving in Allah’s cause, this is the destination worth moving towards.

Verse 108

abiding therein forever, never desiring to leave.

“abiding therein forever, never desiring to leave.” Almost the entire surah is movement. The young believers leave and are later awakened; the man enters his garden and it then collapses; Moses travels, retraces his steps and travels with Khidr; Dhul-Qarnayn reaches west, east and the two mountain barriers. In this world, a believer’s ideas become forces that move him. The light of revelation is a light “with which he walks among people” [al-An‘am: 122], and the early call to proclaim the message was “arise and warn” [al-Muddaththir: 2]. Then, at the end, comes rest: they never desire to leave, because they have reached a place where no deficiency drives them to look elsewhere. Someone seeking final rest in this world will keep moving from one attraction to another. Someone who acts for Allah here reaches a station from which he will never wish to move.

Verse 109

Say, “If the ocean were ink for [writing] the Words of my Lord, the ocean would surely run dry before the Words of my Lord[58] are finished, even if We brought its like to resupply it.”

After all the surah’s wonders, you are told not to imagine that you have seen the end of Allah’s signs. “If the ocean were ink for [writing] the Words of my Lord, the ocean would surely run dry before the Words of my Lord[58] are finished, even if We brought its like to resupply it.” Even if someone wrote throughout his life, he would need only a limited amount of ink. Now imagine the seas themselves becoming ink: they would run out before Allah’s words. This includes His knowledge, which creation cannot encompass, His signs in the universe, and the meanings of His revelation that He opens to His servants as He wills. This meets the teaching of Ayat al-Kursi: “while they encompass nothing of His knowledge, except what He wills.” [al-Baqarah: 255]. The stories of the Cave, Khidr and Dhul-Qarnayn are not all the wonders there are. They are drops Allah has chosen from an inexhaustible sea, teaching us how to look at the rest of life.

Verse 110

Say, “I am only a man like you; it has been revealed to me that your God is One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him do righteous deeds and associate none in the worship of his Lord.”

The conclusion removes every exaggeration concerning the bearer of the message: “I am only a man like you; it has been revealed to me”. The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, was known for truthfulness and trustworthiness before prophethood. Yet revelation was the distinction by which he became Allah’s Messenger. If people turn away from revelation, humanity by itself does not produce guidance. The revelation is then summed up in its great motivating truth: “your God is One God.” In the Quran, worshipping Allah alone is no lifeless theological debate. It is the force that made the young believers leave their homeland, the believer confront the owner of the two gardens, Moses travel in search of guidance, and Dhul-Qarnayn cross the earth to establish justice. Noah, peace be upon him, devoted nearly a thousand years among his people to this foundation. Worshipping Allah alone is worth a lifetime. Then comes the goal that protects action from going astray: “So whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord”. Someone who lives in closeness to his Lord longs to meet Him. Yet hope is not wishful thinking: “let him do righteous deeds”, rather than simply many deeds of any kind. Then comes the final safeguard: “and associate none in the worship of his Lord.” Many commentators include showing off within this warning. You may know the right action yet lose its worth because your purpose is divided between Allah and people. The surah’s ending returns to the two keys lost by those who lose most: Allah’s inexhaustible signs, and the meeting with Allah for which one hopes. Whoever preserves the source and the goal, and acts righteously with sincerity, takes light from Surat al-Kahf, rather than merely a story.

Simplified reflection

Surat al-Kahf — Reflection

Verses 1–110

A balanced version for a first reading — enough context, with each idea presented once, without repetition or unnecessary length.

Verse 1

All praise be to Allah Who has sent down upon His slave the Book, and has not allowed any crookedness therein[1],

The surah begins by praising Allah for the blessing of revelation. Intelligence and experience alone do not enable a person to see trials and life as they truly are; he needs a Book from his Creator to guide him where matters become confusing. Describing the Prophet ﷺ as “His slave” honors him and affirms that even the highest of creation remains a servant in need of Allah’s guidance. The perfection of this blessing includes the Book having no “crookedness”: no defect or contradiction undermines its standard. Other ideas and standards are therefore assessed against it, rather than making it subject to them.

Verse 2

an upright Book, to warn of severe punishment from Him[2], and to give glad tidings to the believers who do righteous deeds, that they will have a good reward,

The Book that has no crookedness is also “an upright Book”: it puts a person’s understanding and actions right and assesses standards that may become confused away from revelation. From the beginning of the surah, its two purposes appear in the heart: warning of Allah’s severe punishment and giving good news to believers who translate their faith into righteous deeds. The Quran is more than intellectual knowledge; it is guidance that moves a person to take a position and act.

Verse 3

wherein they will abide forever;

The “good reward” promised to believers is not a temporary blessing like worldly blessings, but an enduring abode, “wherein they will abide forever”. Here one of the surah’s great standards begins: whatever belongs to this world may disappear, however stable it seems, whereas the permanence of what is with Allah gives it its true worth.

Verse 4

and to warn those who say, “Allah has begotten a son[3].”

After the good news comes a specific warning against those who attribute offspring to Allah, because this touches the foundation of a servant’s knowledge of his Lord. Its appearance after describing the Messenger ﷺ as “His slave” establishes the standard: being chosen, receiving prophethood, and attaining high rank do not take anyone beyond the reality of servanthood. Allah, glorified is He, is far above any need for offspring.

Verse 5

They have no knowledge of it, nor did their forefathers[4]. What a monstrous word that comes out of their mouths! They say nothing but lies.

“They have no knowledge of it” reveals that the claim “Allah has begotten a son[3]” rests on no evidence, whether from those who make it or the forefathers from whom they inherited it. Imitation does not turn falsehood into truth, and inheriting a claim does not provide proof for it. Hence the force of “What a monstrous word”: the danger lies in attributing something to Allah without knowledge. The verse then affirms that what comes from their mouths in this matter is nothing but lies.

Verse 6

Perhaps you will grieve yourself to death [O Prophet] over them[5] if they do not believe in this message[6].

The Prophet ﷺ grieved deeply over those who turned away from the Quran, to the point that the verse pictures this grief as almost consuming him in their wake. It gives someone calling people to Allah a balanced standard: show compassion and do all you can to convey the message, but do not destroy yourself because some people choose to turn away. Guidance and the opening of hearts are in Allah’s hands.

Verse 7

We have made all that is on earth as an adornment for it, so that We may test them as to which of them is best in deeds.

What is on earth has been made an “adornment” and a test, not the final measure of worth. Wealth, beauty, and strength attract the eye, but the Quran’s question is not who possesses more; it is who is “best in deeds” with what he has been given. Adornment may be a trust that leads to obedience, or become a veil when its true nature as a test is forgotten.

Verse 8

And We will surely turn all that is on it into a barren land[7].

To prevent adornment from deceiving the eye, the verse shows its end: what appears full of life can become “a barren land[7].” Sound guidance here means seeing a thing together with its outcome, rather than judging it by its present glitter. Apparent stability in this world does not guarantee permanence.

Verse 9

Do you think that the people of the Cave and the Inscription were of Our only wondrous signs[8]?

Before beginning the account of the people of the Cave, the Quran draws attention to the fact that their story, remarkable as it is, is not Allah’s most remarkable sign. The signs of His power are too extensive to be reduced to this one event. Disputed details, such as al-Raqim (the Inscription), their number, and their location, must therefore not swallow up the clearer meaning: the account is a sign of Allah’s guidance, protection, and power.

Verse 10

When the youths took refuge in the Cave, and said, “Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself, and show us the right way in our ordeal[9].”

The youths flee to a forbidding cave to preserve their religion, but their movement is, in truth, a turning to Allah for refuge. Their prayer therefore brings together all their needs: “mercy” encompassing fear and pain, and “the right way,” guiding them to act rightly amid trial. Calling them youths highlights the strength of faith in young people who resisted the pressure of their surroundings and chose the safety of their religion over the safety of a place.

Verse 11

Then We caused them to fall into a deep sleep in the cave for many years.

The first form of their protection was a long sleep that Allah caused to overcome them in the cave. Their deliverance did not require visible strength or a military fortress; Allah made sleep itself a means of protection. This reminds us that the means of deliverance are not confined to what a person expects or regards as powerful.

Verse 12

Then We awakened them so that We may see which of the two parties would be more precise in calculating the length of their stay.

After the long years, Allah awakened them from their sleep, revealing their differing estimates of how long they had remained and, with that, the limits of human calculation before Allah’s knowledge. What seemed to them a short period was, in fact, a long one. The account itself thus becomes a witness to the limits of human perception of time and reality.

Verse 13

We relate to you their story in truth. They were youths who believed in their Lord, and We gave them more guidance.

When the Quran begins to recount their story in detail, it does not introduce them through their names or lineages, but through their most important reality: “They were youths who believed in their Lord, and We gave them more guidance.” They began with the step of faith, and greater guidance and firmness followed. The journey toward Allah begins with the servant responding to the truth he knows; Allah then opens for him guidance that he did not possess before taking action.

Verse 14

We strengthened their hearts when they stood up and proclaimed, “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and earth. We will never call upon any god other than Him, or we would be uttering an outrageous lie[10].”

The faith in their hearts became a public stand: they stood up and declared that the Lord of the heavens and earth was their Lord, and that they would call on no god besides Him. At that moment, “We strengthened their hearts.” Steadfastness is not the absence of fear, but the heart remaining firm through Allah when confronting what frightens it, without compromising the foundation of His oneness.

Verse 15

These people of ours have taken besides Him other gods. Why do they not bring clear proof about them? Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates lies against Allah?

After declaring Allah’s oneness, the youths asked their people for proof of the partners they worshiped: “Why do they not bring clear proof about them?” A claim concerning Allah is not accepted merely because it is widespread or its supporters are powerful. “Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates lies against Allah?” A believer’s weakness in worldly power does not prevent him from possessing evidence and distinguishing a claim from its proof.

Verse 16

When you have distanced yourselves from them and what they worship other than Allah, take refuge in the cave; your Lord will extend His mercy to you and make for you an easy way out of your ordeal.”

When remaining among their people became dangerous to their religion, the youths chose to withdraw to the cave, hoping that Allah would extend His mercy over them and provide “an easy way out”. The place was not comfortable by nature, but Allah’s mercy turned it into a place of deliverance. What appears cramped may offer the heart more spaciousness than a comfortable place that threatens faith.

Verse 17

You would have seen the sun when it rose, it would incline away from their Cave to the right; and when it set, it would turn away from them to the left[11], while they were in its open space. That was one of the signs of Allah. Whoever Allah guides is rightly guided, and whoever He causes to stray, you will never find for him a protecting guide.

The Quran reveals that the youths’ protection extended to the details of their surroundings, even the sun’s movement in relation to the cave. All the forces of the natural world are under Allah’s management, and He prepares from them whatever He wills for His servants. The verse then returns to the foundation of the account: “Whoever Allah guides is rightly guided.” The sound guidance they asked for is a gift from Allah before it is a result of circumstances.

Verse 18

You would have thought they were awake, although they were asleep. We turned them over on their right and left sides, when their dog stretched its forelegs at the entrance. If you had seen them, you would have fled away from them, filled with horror[12].

They were asleep, but their appearance suggested wakefulness. Allah turned them over, their dog was at the entrance, and He made the sight of them inspire such dread that people kept away. The youths did what they could when they fled to preserve their religion. Allah then provided protection beyond their control while they were unconscious. Their deliverance combined their use of available means with Allah’s care, which extended beyond their ability.

Verse 19

Similarly, We awakened them so that they might question one another. One of them said, “How long have you been here?” They said, “We have been here for a day or part of a day.” They said, “Your Lord knows best how long you have been here. Send one of you with these silver coins of yours to the city, let him see which food is purest and bring you some provision from it. And let him be cautious and let no one know about you.

When they awoke, they did not know what had changed outside the cave. They shortened the discussion about the length of their stay by saying, “Your Lord knows best how long you have been here.” Then they turned to the practical need for food. They sent one of their number and instructed him to act carefully and discreetly. Faith does not remove the need for caution, and collective work does not require inflated roles: the person suited to the task carries it out.

Verse 20

For if they find out about you, they will stone you or force you back to their religion, and then you will never succeed.”

Their insistence on a careful approach arose from a real fear: if their people discovered their location, they might stone them or force them back to their religion. What they feared most was not bodily harm, but losing their religion. Returning to the worship of others besides Allah would be the loss with which no success could coexist.

Verse 21

This is how We made their case known so that the people might know that the promise of Allah is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. When they were disputing among themselves[13] concerning their matter [of resurrection], they said, “Build a structure over them. Their Lord knows best about them.” Those who prevailed in their matter said, “We will surely build over them a place of worship.”

Allah made the youths’ case known after the times had changed. Their long sleep and subsequent awakening became tangible evidence that Allah’s promise is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. This proof of resurrection is the greater purpose of their discovery, rather than people turning away from the sign toward attachment to the place and what is built around it.

Verse 22

Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” – merely guessing at the unseen. And others will say, “They were seven, their dog was the eighth.” Say, “My Lord knows best their number; no one knows about them except a few[14]. So do not argue about them except in a brief manner, nor ask anyone about them.”

After the lesson has become clear, some people become absorbed in the question of the youths’ number: three, five, or seven. Much of this discussion is conjecture about the unseen; the verse describes the first two proposals as “merely guessing at the unseen.” The Quran returns knowledge of their number to Allah and discourages argument that yields neither guidance nor action. Do not let details for which no certainty has been given drain you while the account’s purpose is clear.

Verse 23

And never say about anything, “I will surely do this tomorrow[15],”

After the lesson about the unseen past comes the proper attitude toward the future: do not speak about something you will do tomorrow as though you control its occurrence. You plan and work, but the future is not in your hands. Servanthood requires keeping this truth present even while using practical means.

Verse 24

without adding, “if Allah wills.” And remember your Lord if you forget, and say, “My Lord will hopefully guide me to something closer to what is right[16].”

“if Allah wills” connects a human plan to Allah’s will, beyond which nothing can pass. If forgetfulness occurs, remembering Allah returns a person to his dependence upon Him. He then goes beyond making up for the omission and asks, “My Lord will hopefully guide me to something closer to what is right[16].” Seeking sound guidance is an ongoing journey, not a state completed once and for all.

Verse 25

They remained in their cave for three hundred years, plus nine[17].

The Quran states the length of their stay after teaching the reader not to speculate about the unseen. What the youths had thought was “a day or part of a day” was “three hundred years, plus nine[17].” This reveals the gulf between a person’s limited perception of time and Allah’s all-encompassing knowledge.

Verse 26

Say, “Allah knows best how long they remained. To Him belongs [the knowledge of] the unseen of the heavens and earth. How well He sees and how well He hears[18]! They have no protector besides Him, and He shares His command with none.”

Alongside the mention of the duration, the context immediately returns to the foundation: “Allah knows best how long they remained.” To Him belongs the unseen of the heavens and earth. Creation has no protector besides Him, and He has no partner in His command. The account thus ends by returning the heart from curiosity about the unseen to Allah’s knowledge, protection, and judgment.

Verse 27

Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord. None can change His Words, and you will never find any refuge except with Him[19].

After the account of the youths, the instruction returns to the revelation that disclosed its meaning: “Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord.” No one can change Allah’s words; they are a place of firmness when the standards around a person change. The cave was a temporary means of deliverance, but the refuge for which there is no substitute is Allah and His guidance.

Verse 28

Be patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face [pleasure]. Do not turn your eyes away from them, desiring the adornments of the life of this world. And do not obey one whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desires and whose affairs [deeds] are at loss.

Allah commands His Prophet ﷺ to “Be patient with those who call upon their Lord” sincerely, and not to turn his eyes beyond them in pursuit of worldly adornment. Wealth and position are not the standard for companionship. Believing companions themselves help a person remain firm, just as the youths faced their trial as a group. Conversely, one whose heart has become heedless and who follows his desires until his affairs are lost must not be obeyed.

Verse 29

Say, “The truth is from your Lord. Whoever wills may believe, and whoever wills may disbelieve.” We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire which will encompass them like the walls. If they cry for relief, they will be relieved with water like boiling oil that will scald the faces. What a terrible drink, and what a terrible resting place!

“The truth is from your Lord.” Its worth does not change with people’s wealth or poverty. “Whoever wills may believe, and whoever wills may disbelieve” does not put the two paths on an equal footing, because the outcome is mentioned immediately: a Fire that surrounds the wrongdoers, and a cry for relief answered with water like boiling oil that scalds faces. The closing “what a terrible resting place!” contrasts with the ease Allah prepared for the youths.

Verse 30

Those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will not let the reward of those who do good deeds to go to waste.

On the other side, Allah affirms that He does not let the reward of someone who believes and does good be lost. This corrects the standard of visible results. A believer may make an effort without seeing its fruit in this world, just as the youths left their country and disappeared into a cave. Sincere work is nevertheless preserved with Allah; its worth is not measured by fame or immediate results.

Verse 31

It is they who will have Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow. They will be adorned therein with golden bracelets and will wear green garments of fine silk and brocade, reclining on adorned couches. What an excellent reward, and what a pleasant resting place!

The preserved reward appears in gardens of permanent residence, rivers, adornment, clothing, and rest that will never disappear. The problem is not adornment or comfort themselves, but selling what endures for what passes away. Hence “resting place” returns here in praise after appearing in the description of the Fire in blame. What was mistaken for comfort proves a mirage; here are lasting settlement and security.

Verse 32

Give them an example of two men[20]: to one of them We gave two gardens of grapevines and surrounded them with palm trees, and placed between them crops[21].

After the warning against turning beyond poor believers toward worldly adornment comes the example of two men, one of whom was given two gardens with everything they needed. From the beginning of the description, the blessing is repeatedly attributed to Allah: “We gave,” “surrounded them,” and “placed between them crops[21].” Before the garden owner speaks, the Quran establishes who brought into being the scene that will captivate him.

Verse 33

Each garden yielded its produce and did not fall short in the least, and We caused a stream to flow through them.

The gardens enjoyed complete blessing: they yielded their fruit without any shortfall, and a stream was placed between them. Ingratitude did not arise from deprivation or deficiency, but followed abundant provision. This makes the distortion clearer when the blessing is attributed to oneself and its Giver is forgotten.

Verse 34

And he had abundant fruit. He said to his companion, as he was conversing with him, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers.”

The first thing we hear from the garden owner is a comparison: “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in followers.” The blessing has changed from something to be grateful for into a standard by which he measures his own and his companion’s worth. Here the trial of adornment begins: what a person owns becomes his definition of himself and a reason to place himself above others.

Verse 35

He entered his garden, having wronged himself[22], and said, “I do not think that this will ever perish,

He entered his garden “having wronged himself[22]” and said, “I do not think that this will ever perish”. The flourishing scene filled his eyes until he treated the present as a guarantee of the future, although the surah had already established that all earthly adornment would end. The fault is not in seeing beauty, but in forgetting that it can pass away.

Verse 36

nor do I think that the Hour will ever come. Even if I am brought back to my Lord, I will surely find something better than this[23].”

His pride extended from imagining the garden would endure to doubting the Hour, then to believing that if he returned to his Lord he would find something better. Material success became, for him, proof that he always deserved good. Yet worldly blessing is no certificate that one’s belief is correct or that Allah is unconditionally pleased; it is part of the test.

Verse 37

His companion said, while conversing with him, “Do you disbelieve in He Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then fashioned you into a well-proportioned man[24]?

His believing companion confronted his pride by recalling the One Who “created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then fashioned you into a well-proportioned man[24]”. The man boasting of having more had not given himself his own existence. Remembering the beginning returns a person to his true proportions. Here companionship did not prevent frank advice when the words became disbelief and arrogance.

Verse 38

As for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I do not associate anyone with my Lord.

After challenging his companion, the believer clearly declares his standard: “As for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I do not associate anyone with my Lord.” Having little wealth did not make him redefine himself by the rich man’s standards. His worth and security are connected to his relationship with his Lord, rather than the amount he owns compared with others.

Verse 39

If only you had said, when you entered your garden, ‘This is by Allah’s Will; there is no power except with Allah.’ Even though you see me as inferior to you in wealth and children,

On entering the garden, he should have said: “This is by Allah’s Will; there is no power except with Allah.” These words return the blessing to Allah’s will and power to its source, instead of allowing present abundance to become an illusion of independence and permanence. The blessing is still recognized, but its attribution is corrected.

Verse 40

it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden, and send upon your garden a thunderbolt from the heaven, turning it into a barren land.

The believer answers his companion’s contempt for his poverty by affirming that having fewer possessions and children now does not confine Allah’s favor: “it may well be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden.” Conversely, the garden itself may become “a barren land” if Allah sends punishment upon it from the sky. What has been given can change, and its owner possesses no guarantee that it will remain.

Verse 41

Or its water may sink, so you will never be able to get it back.”

A blessing may disappear without an obvious catastrophe. It is enough for its water to sink beyond its owner’s reach. A person sees the fruit and trees, yet does not control many of the hidden causes on which their continued existence depends. This breaks the illusion of complete control over what is in one’s hands.

Verse 42

Eventually, all his produce was destroyed and he started wringing his hands [in dismay] over what he had spent on it, as it had collapsed on its trellises, and he said, “Oh, would that I had not associated anyone with my Lord!”

What the believer had warned of occurred: “all his produce was destroyed.” The garden owner wrung his hands in regret as it lay ruined, saying, “Oh, would that I had not associated anyone with my Lord!” The truth became clear to him after the adornment fell away. Sound guidance is to recognize this truth while things are flourishing, before losing what one had relied upon.

Verse 43

He had none to support him against Allah, nor could he support himself.

At the time of affliction, the other support of which he boasted, being “superior in followers,” also fell away. He had no group to support him against Allah, nor could he save himself. Wealth and numbers may give a sense of strength, but they cannot repel Allah’s command when it comes.

Verse 44

In that situation[25], the authority only rests with Allah, the True God. He is best in reward and best in outcome.

“In that situation[25], the authority only rests with Allah, the True God.” When the means fall away, true authority is revealed as belonging to Allah alone. His sovereignty did not begin at that moment; the veil created by adornment was removed. “He is best in reward and best in outcome.” What matters is therefore not what appears stronger now, but where things end.

Verse 45

Give them the example of the life of this world: it is like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky, but soon they turn into chaff scattered by the winds. Allah has full power over all things.

After the man’s two gardens, the Quran extends the scene to the whole of worldly life: water falls, plants grow green, and then they become dry chaff scattered by the wind. What happened to the garden is not an exception, but a concentrated picture of the law of passing away that a person sees throughout the world around him. Allah can transform circumstances, however settled they appear to the eye.

Verse 46

Wealth and children are the adornments of the life of this world, but the lasting righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward and better in hope.

Wealth and children are among the most prominent material and human supports upon which a person relies, but the Quran calls them “the adornments of the life of this world.” Adornment is not blameworthy in itself; it can acquire worth when used in obedience. True permanence, however, belongs to “the lasting righteous deeds” and their reward with Allah. Hence they are “better with your Lord in reward and better in hope.”

Verse 47

And [beware of] the Day when We will move the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare[26]; We will gather them all and not leave behind anyone of them.

After explaining what remains and what perishes comes the Day on which even the mountains disappear, although they are among the clearest images of stability to the human eye. The earth is laid bare and all creation is gathered. The limits of every worldly stability become clear, while the deeds a person has sent ahead remain.

Verse 48

They will be presented before your Lord in rows, “Now you have come to Us as We created you the first time[27], although you claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.”

People are presented before their Lord in rows and come as they were created the first time, after titles, possessions, and the things relied upon have fallen away. Those who denied the appointment are confronted with its reality: what they thought would never arrive is now present, and a person stands there with only himself and his deeds.

Verse 49

And the record [of deeds] will be placed before them, and you will see the wicked scared of what it contains. They will say, “Woe to us! What is this book that leaves nothing, small or big, except that it has recorded it?” They will find all what they did before them, and your Lord does not wrong anyone.

The record of deeds is placed before them, and the wicked are terrified because it has recorded everything, small and great. Each person finds what he did present before him. Here the meaning of what lasts becomes clear: in the end, what remains with you is your work, under perfect justice that loses no good deed and adds nothing to anyone’s wrongdoing.

Verse 50

And [remember] when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all prostrated except Iblīs, who was one of the jinn, but he disobeyed the command of his Lord. Will you then take him and his progeny as protectors instead of Me[28], even though they are your enemies[29]? What a terrible exchange for the wrongdoers!

After the scenes of reckoning, the surah recalls an ancient root of pride: Iblis was commanded to prostrate before Adam, refused, and then became an enemy of Adam’s descendants. Hence the rebuke: how can he and his progeny be taken as protectors instead of Allah when they are your enemies? Someone who began his course with disobedience and arrogance is unfit to guide anyone toward deliverance.

Verse 51

I did not call them to witness the creation of the heavens and earth, nor even the creation of their own selves, and I would not take those who misguide others as helpers[30].

Those who mislead and are taken as protectors did not witness the creation of the heavens and earth or the creation of their own selves. Allah did not take them as helpers. They possess neither knowledge that qualifies them to rival the Creator’s guidance nor power that makes relying upon them a substitute for relying upon Allah.

Verse 52

And [beware of] the Day when He will say, “Call upon those whom you claimed to be My partners.” So they will call them, but they will not respond to them[31], and We will make them share the same doom.

On the Day of Resurrection, those who associated partners with Allah are told to call upon the partners they claimed. They call, but receive no response. The verse speaks of “the same doom”; the Arabic expression also evokes destruction or a barrier of punishment between them. At the moment of real need, the imagined support of worldly life is exposed as false. Relationships built upon associating partners with Allah end in a severance that offers no help.

Verse 53

The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and they will find no escape from it.

After the supposed partners fail them, the wicked see the Fire and become certain that they will fall into it, finding no escape. Here the possibilities of evasion and delay familiar in worldly life end. The scene has become visible, and there is no path that changes the outcome after it has arrived.

Verse 54

We have surely diversified in this Qur’an every kind of example for people[32], but man is the most quarrelsome of all beings[33].

Allah has diversified “every kind of example for people[32]” in the Quran, varying accounts, arguments, and images so that meaning reaches the heart from several directions. Yet “man is the most quarrelsome of all beings[33].” Argument can change from a way of seeking truth into a means of resisting what is clear and refusing to respond to it.

Verse 55

Nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking forgiveness from their Lord except that the fate of the earlier nations should come to them, or that the punishment should come before their eyes.

After guidance has arrived and explanation has taken many forms, the obstacle is not always a lack of evidence. It may be obstinacy, as though a person is waiting for the fate of earlier deniers to descend upon him or for punishment to confront him directly. Demanding further signs while turning away from the guidance already present does not necessarily lead to faith; it may itself be a form of obstinacy.

Verse 56

We do not send the messengers except as bearers of glad tidings and warners. But those who disbelieve seek to undermine the truth with false arguments, and they take My verses and warnings in ridicule.

After exposing the obstinacy of those who demand further signs, the context defines the messengers’ task: bringing good news and warning, rather than meeting obstinate demands. The problem is not always absent evidence. A disbeliever may use false arguments not to reach truth, but to “undermine the truth,” shaking its firmness in people’s hearts. Such argument may then develop into mockery of the signs and their warnings.

Verse 57

Who could be more wrong than one who is reminded of the verses of his Lord, but he turns away from them and forgets what his hands have sent forth? We have placed veils on their hearts so that they cannot understand it, and deafness in their ears. Even if you call them to guidance, they will never be guided.

The gravest wrongdoing is for a person to be reminded of his Lord’s signs, then turn away and forget what his hands have sent ahead. Repeated turning away changes the problem from a passing attitude into a veil between the heart and understanding, and between the ear and benefiting from guidance. The covering that prevents useful hearing and insight is not a deficiency in the signs, but the fruit of a path its possessor chose until it became firmly established within him.

Verse 58

But your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Lord of Mercy. If He were to take them to task for what they did, He would have hastened their punishment. Yet there is an appointed time for them from which they will find no escape.

Despite all the preceding turning away and argument, your Lord remains “the All-Forgiving, Lord of Mercy.” Punishment not descending immediately is neither inability nor approval of falsehood, but respite and mercy. If people were immediately taken to account for all they earned, punishment would quickly arrive. Yet they have an appointed time; when it comes, they will find no refuge from it.

Verse 59

Those are the towns We destroyed when they persisted in wrongdoing, and We set an appointed time for their destruction.

The towns Allah destroyed before are practical witnesses that respite does not mean neglect. Their people continued until the time Allah had appointed for their destruction. The passage thus brings together mercy in delay and the reality that wrongdoing has a term beyond which it will not pass.

Verse 60

And [remember] when Moses said to his servant, “I will not give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, or I travel for ages.”

The journey of Moses, peace be upon him, begins with clear resolve: “I will not give up until I reach.” He had been told that one of Allah’s servants possessed knowledge he had not been given, so he sought to reach him and learn from him [Sahih al-Bukhari 3401]. Despite Moses’s high rank, he did not refrain from a long journey to acquire knowledge he lacked. True knowledge makes its possessor more humble and more willing to learn from someone who has an advantage in a field he does not know.

Verse 61

But when they reached the junction of the two seas, they forgot their fish, which made its way into the sea, slipping away.

Moses and his young companion reached the place where the sign of the meeting had been appointed, but they forgot the fish, which made its way into the sea. The verse recalls human limits even during a great act of obedience. A person may reach the intended place and still fail to notice the sign, remaining in need of Allah’s reminder and enabling help.

Verse 62

When they traveled further, he said to his servant, “Bring us our morning meal; this journey has truly exhausted us.”

After passing the place, Moses asked for food and said that the journey had exhausted him. The hadith relates that this severe tiredness did not affect him until he had gone beyond the place where he was commanded to seek Khidr [Sahih al-Bukhari 3401]. The long journey was made easy up to its intended point; the fatigue appeared after they passed it.

Verse 63

He said, “You remember when we rested by the rock, I forgot the fish; it was none who made me forget to mention it except Satan, and it made its way into the sea amazingly.”

When the young man mentioned the rock, he remembered the fish and attributed his forgetting to Satan. The incident reveals Satan’s eagerness to interrupt the path of knowledge and delay the meeting of righteous people. It also reveals a recurring meaning in the surah: a person forgets and therefore needs renewed reminders to preserve the light and sound guidance he has learned.

Verse 64

Moses said, “That is exactly what we were looking for.” So they turned back, retracing their footsteps.

Moses did not turn the situation into prolonged reproach. He immediately said, “That is exactly what we were looking for.” Then they retraced their footsteps. Once the point of error becomes clear, the priority is to correct it and return to the path, rather than consume time exchanging blame over what has passed.

Verse 65

There they found one of Our slaves[34] upon whom We bestowed Our mercy[35] and We taught him from Our Own knowledge[36].

Moses found a servant whom Allah described through two things: mercy from Him and knowledge directly bestowed by Him. Placing mercy before knowledge establishes an important standard for a teacher. The nature of this knowledge also opens a window onto wisdom behind events whose reality is not apparent at first sight. The account does not grant people knowledge of the unseen, but teaches them not to confine Allah’s decree to what they see before them.

Verse 66

Moses said to him, “May I follow you so that you may teach me some knowledge that you have been taught?”

Moses addressed Khidr with a learner’s courtesy: “May I follow you”. He made himself a follower in this particular field, although he was one of the prophets of firm resolve. He did not seek knowledge merely to collect information. In asking to learn “some knowledge that you have been taught”, he sought rushdan, right guidance. The purpose of learning is to lead us towards what is right and bring us closer to Allah, rather than making knowledge a value detached from guidance.

Verse 67

He said, “You will never be able to have patience with me.”

Khidr answered plainly from the start: “You will never be able to have patience with me.” An honest teacher does not make a path look attractive when he knows it will be hard for the student, or promise what he cannot deliver. He explains the nature of the journey before it begins.

Verse 68

How can you have patience with that which you have no knowledge about?”

Khidr explains why patience will be difficult: “How can you have patience with that which you have no knowledge about?” Much of the pain in this story comes from seeing part of an event without knowing its outcome. The story therefore trains us to submit to Allah when the full wisdom is hidden from us. It does not train us to suspend reason or deny what events outwardly show.

Verse 69

Moses said, “You will find me patient, if Allah wills; and I will not disobey any of your orders.”

Moses answered with determination, but did not attribute patience to himself alone: “You will find me patient, if Allah wills”. He combined sincere resolve with dependence on Allah’s will, then committed himself not to disobey Khidr within the bounds of this educational companionship.

Verse 70

He said, “Then if you follow me, do not ask me about anything until I mention it to you.”

Khidr set a clear condition for their companionship: “do not ask me about anything until I mention it to you”. Mature educational relationships need expectations and boundaries understood from the outset. The teacher knows the nature of what will happen, and the student has agreed to wait for the explanation instead of imposing his own timing on the teacher.

Verse 71

So they both set out, until when they boarded a ship, he made a hole in it. Moses said, “Did you make a hole in it to drown its people? You have done something terrible!”

In the first test, Khidr made a hole in the ship. Moses saw only harm being done to its people, so he objected: “Did you make a hole in it to drown its people?” His objection arose from what the event outwardly showed and his concern for others. The wisdom to be revealed later had not yet become apparent to him. Here the practical experience of the difference between what can be seen and how a matter will turn out begins.

Verse 72

He said, “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?”

Khidr did not begin explaining early. Instead, he reminded Moses of their previous agreement: “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?” A teacher’s steadiness in following the agreed method keeps a lesson from changing with every passing reaction, especially when an explanation is already planned for its proper time.

Verse 73

Moses said, “Do not take me to task for what I forgot, and do not make it too difficult for me to follow you.”

Moses acknowledged that he had forgotten, and asked Khidr not to hold it against him or make this companionship too difficult. He did not try to justify the breach or turn it into a quarrel. He admitted what had happened and asked for room to continue, so the journey resumed.

Verse 74

Then they proceeded until they met a boy, and the man killed him. Moses said, “Did you kill an innocent soul who killed none? You have done something monstrous!”

The second scene was more severe. Khidr killed a boy who had killed no one. Moses objected strongly, describing it as “something monstrous”. The stronger objection matched the gravity of what he saw. The verse does not ask a believer to lose the natural impulse to object to apparent wrongdoing. It reveals that this particular event involved a special command from Allah, which its later explanation would make clear.

Verse 75

The man said, “Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?”

The second reminder was stronger: “Did I not tell you”. This time the Arabic adds laka, meaning to you, explicitly addressing Moses, although the published English dialogue has the same wording as in verse 72. Repeating the breach brought the earlier condition into sharper focus. Even so, Khidr did not reveal the wisdom before the time he had set.

Verse 76

Moses said, “If I ask you about anything after this, do not keep me in your company, for then you would be excused concerning me.”

Moses saw that his repeated questioning suggested he might be unable to continue this companionship under its condition, so he set himself a final limit: if he asked again, Khidr should no longer keep him in his company. In saying, “for then you would be excused concerning me”, he acknowledged that the other person would be justified in ending the relationship after repeated breaches of what they had agreed to avoid.

Verse 77

Then they went on until they came to the people of a town[37]. They asked its people for food, but they refused to offer them hospitality. They found there a wall that was about to collapse, but he repaired it. Moses said, “If you wished, you could have taken some payment for it.”

The two entered a town and asked its people for food, but the people refused them hospitality. Khidr then found a wall about to fall and repaired it without payment. After the townspeople’s stinginess, Moses thought that accepting wages for the work could have met their needs. His question arose from hunger and fatigue, and from the strangeness of showing kindness to people who had refused kindness to them.

Verse 78

The man said, “This is the parting of ways between me and you. I will inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.

As they had agreed, the third question brought their companionship to an end. Khidr said, “This is the parting of ways between me and you.” Yet he did not leave Moses before explaining the events he had been unable to bear patiently. Parting does not prevent clarity. Part of honouring a companionship is closing the matter by explaining what the other person needs to understand.

Verse 79

“As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people who worked at sea. I wanted to make it defective because there was a king ahead of them who seized every [good] ship by force.

The ship belonged to poor people who worked at sea. Ahead of them was a king who seized sound ships by force, so Khidr caused a small defect to preserve the ship itself for its owners. What looked like an immediate loss protected them from a greater one. In life, a limited worldly loss may sometimes avert a worse worldly harm that a person cannot see when the loss occurs.

Verse 80

“As for the boy, his parents were believers, and we feared that he would overburden them with his rebellion and disbelief[38].

The boy’s parents were believers. The danger revealed to Khidr was not merely worldly hardship, but that the boy would overburden them with rebellion and disbelief. Here the benefit rises from protecting wealth, as with the ship, to protecting faith. The event was therefore more severe and still further beyond an ordinary person’s ability to understand from appearances alone.

Verse 81

So we hoped that their Lord would give them another in his place, more righteous and tender-hearted.

For the parents, losing the boy was not the end of the story. Allah intended to give them another in his place, “more righteous and tender-hearted”. The verse teaches that some losses may be followed by a gift a person cannot see at the moment of loss. The measure of goodness extends beyond the thing itself to its effects on faith, mercy and life.

Verse 82

As for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and there was a treasure under it that belonged to them. Their father was a righteous man, so your Lord willed that they should reach their maturity and retrieve their treasure[39], as a mercy from your Lord; I did not do it of my own accord. This is the interpretation of that which you could not bear with patience.”

The wall belonged to two orphan boys, with a treasure beneath it, and their father had been righteous. Their wealth was preserved until they could grow up and retrieve it themselves. Here the effects of good deeds extend beyond the person who performed them. The story then closes with Khidr’s explanation that his actions were commanded by Allah, rather than based on his personal opinion. The phrase “as a mercy from your Lord” reveals, on the broader interpretation, mercy behind all three events, although the time at which it becomes apparent differs.

Verse 83

They ask you about Dhul-Qarnayn[40]. Say, “I will tell you something about him.”

Dhul-Qarnayn’s story begins with a question, but the answer does not turn it into a detailed historical biography: “I will tell you something about him.” What is related serves as a lesson and reminder. It does not include everything that might be said about his name, location and era. The Quran’s purpose is guidance through this example, rather than satisfying curiosity about details that have not been established.

Verse 84

We established him on earth and gave him the means to achieve everything[41].

Dhul-Qarnayn’s story rests on Allah having established him on earth and given him the means he needed. A sabab, a means, is something that enables a person to reach a goal: knowledge, capacity or a tool. Empowerment is neither wishful thinking nor an unexplained moment. It is a blessing from Allah embodied in means that can be employed for a sound purpose.

Verse 85

He pursued a course[42],

Dhul-Qarnayn did not merely possess the means: “He pursued a course[42]”. Action distinguishes someone who possesses resources from someone who benefits from them. He brought together the knowledge, power and tools he had received, organised them and moved towards a clear goal.

Verse 86

until when he reached the far west, he found the sun setting in a dark body of water, and he found some people nearby. We said, “O Dhul-Qarnayn, either punish them or treat them with kindness.”

He reached as far west as he could and, from the observer’s perspective, saw the sun as though setting in dark, muddy water. He then found a people nearby. What matters in his journey is that he was less concerned with the earth’s treasures than with meeting people among whom he could establish justice and whom he could call to Allah. Human beings were the focus of his mission, rather than merely resources to exploit.

Verse 87

He said, “As for one who does wrong[43], we will punish him, then he will be brought back to his Lord, and He will punish him grievously.

When given a choice about how to deal with the people, he made the criterion clear. The person who does wrong and persists in disbelief after clarification is punished, then returns to Allah, with whom lies the greater punishment. He did not make power an end in itself. He placed it within a framework of faith, justice and accountability before Allah.

Verse 88

But he who believes and does righteous deeds, he will have the best reward[44], and we will enjoin upon him to do what is easy.”

On the other side, for the person who believes and does righteous deeds, the verse mentions “the best reward[44]” with Allah first and Dhul-Qarnayn’s easy treatment of him second. This order of mention turns the believer’s heart towards the real reward from his Lord. A ruler’s kindness and concessions must not become the purpose for which he believes and acts.

Verse 89

Then he pursued another course,

After completing the task, he did not linger over the achievement: “Then he pursued another course”. The repeated expression traces his way of life: work followed by work, continually making use of what Allah had given him, without turning past successes into an excuse for idleness.

Verse 90

until when he reached the far east, he found the sun rising on a people for whom We provided no shelter from it[45].

This time he reached the far east, to a people “for whom We provided no shelter from it[45]”. The journey’s extension from the far west to the far east shows that he used the means available to him as fully as he could, instead of confining his responsibility to a comfortable area nearby.

Verse 91

So it was, and We had full knowledge about him[46].

However far Dhul-Qarnayn’s activity extended, and however much knowledge and means he gathered, Allah’s knowledge encompassed everything he possessed. This short statement keeps empowerment from becoming an illusion of all-encompassing knowledge. The servant works with what he has received; his Lord knows him, the limits of his capacity and what lies beyond them.

Verse 92

Then he pursued another course,

For the third time, “he pursued another course”. The repetition is part of the meaning. Achievement was neither a lucky stroke nor an isolated episode, but a consistent method of gathering means and moving with them towards what he saw as upholding truth and establishing justice.

Verse 93

until when he reached [a valley] between the two mountains, he found beyond them a people who could barely understand a word[47].

He reached the pass between the two mountain barriers and found a people “who could barely understand a word[47]”. Communication with them was difficult, yet he did not treat their weakness as a reason to ignore them. When a leader reaches a community with little power to help itself, he should consider its needs and what he can offer, rather than what he can take.

Verse 94

They said, “O Dhul-Qarnayn, Gog and Magog are spreading corruption in the land. Can we give you some payment in return for you to construct a barrier between us and them?”

The people explained that Gog and Magog were spreading corruption in the land, and proposed paying for a barrier to be built. They had a sound idea for protection, but conceived the solution as purchasing a service from someone powerful. In the next verse, that relationship would change from payment to participation.

Verse 95

He said, “What my Lord has given me is better. But help me with manpower, I will construct a barricade between you and them.

Dhul-Qarnayn declined to make the project a financial deal. He said that what his Lord had given him was better, then asked them to help him with their strength. He neither removed their responsibility nor produced a solution that would leave them helpless afterwards. He involved them in the work, bringing leadership and expertise together with the energy of the community the barrier would protect.

Verse 96

Bring me iron blocks” – until when he leveled between the two mountainsides, he said, “Blow [with bellows],” until when he fired them up to extreme heat, he said, “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.”

The Quran describes the building stages in striking detail: blocks of iron, levelling the gap between the mountains, blowing until the iron glows like fire, then pouring molten copper over it. Technical precision does not conflict with faith. When the purpose is sound, mastery of materials, engineering and the use of knowledge becomes part of the work through which a person draws closer to Allah.

Verse 97

Thus they could not climb over it nor could they pierce it[48].

The barrier’s practical success had two aspects: they could neither climb over it nor pierce it. This success was the fruit of the means brought together in the story: knowledge, capacity, tools, cooperation and skilful execution, rather than merely wishing for protection.

Verse 98

He said, “This is a mercy from my Lord. But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass[49], He will raze it to the ground. The promise of my Lord is ever true.”

After a great achievement, Dhul-Qarnayn did not attribute it to his own genius. He said, “This is a mercy from my Lord.” Nor did he imagine that what he had made would endure forever: when Allah’s promised time came, He would raze it to the ground. Here lies the difference between him and the owner of the two gardens, who said, “I do not think that this will ever perish”. A believer makes his utmost effort, then sees the blessing as coming from Allah and knows that its continuance depends on His will.

Verse 99

On that Day[50] We will let some of them surge like waves over others, and the Trumpet will be blown, then We will gather them all together.

Several interpretations are related concerning “We will let some of them surge like waves over others”. One takes this to mean Gog and Magog as they emerge and mingle; another takes it to mean creation on the Day of Resurrection. Then the scene explicitly moves to the blowing of the Trumpet and the gathering of everyone. After the greatest displays of power and construction, the ending returns to the day when Allah alone gathers creation.

Verse 100

On that Day We will show Hell to the disbelievers clearly[51],

Hell will be displayed plainly to the disbelievers, after having been part of the unseen about which they were told in this world. At that moment, there will be no possibility of denying what revelation warned them of. The fate that had been an account will become a scene present before their eyes.

Verse 101

those whose eyes were blind to My signs[52] and they were unable to hear [the truth][53].

The cause of heedlessness was not a shortage of signs. Their eyes, described here as “blind to My signs[52]”, are pictured in the Arabic as enclosed within a covering, rather than briefly obscured by something passing over them. With persistence in disbelief, hearing too ceased to fulfil its purpose. They benefited neither from the Quran’s reminder nor from the signs spread throughout creation.

Verse 102

Do the disbelievers think that they can take My slaves as allies instead of Me? We have prepared Hell as a dwelling place for the disbelievers.

The disbelievers imagined that taking Allah’s servants as allies apart from Him would benefit them. At the end, the verse destroys that illusion: no ally can save anyone independently of Allah, and Hell has been prepared for them as “a dwelling place”. The Arabic word nuzul is also used for what is prepared for a guest. Here it forms a bitter reversal of the welcome a person expects, showing how their hopes will be overturned.

Verse 103

Say, “Shall we inform you of the greatest losers in terms of their deeds?

Before describing those who lose most, the Quran does not say that they did no work. It asks, “Shall we inform you of the greatest losers in terms of their deeds?” The gravest loss may be suffered by someone full of activity and effort. Much work is not enough when the direction itself is unsound.

Verse 104

Those whose efforts in the life of this world are wasted, while they think that they are doing good[54].”

These people made efforts in the world, but those efforts went astray while they thought they were doing well. Through repeatedly rejecting clarification, a person may find the path he has chosen made easy and attractive to him, so that he continues confidently. Feeling sincere or hardworking is therefore not enough. He needs a standard that guides him.

Verse 105

It is those who disbelieve in the verses of their Lord and their meeting with Him; their deeds will be nullified, and We will give no weight to them on the Day of Resurrection[55].

The verse reveals the standard they had lost: they disbelieved in their Lord’s verses and in meeting Him. The signs govern the path, and remembering the meeting with Allah governs the goal. If the source that guides a person is rejected, along with the day on which he will be held accountable, many deeds can turn into wasted effort with no weight before Allah.

Verse 106

That is their recompense: Hell, because of their disbelief and taking My verses and messengers in ridicule.

The recompense of this path is Hell. Its cause is not disbelief alone, but making Allah’s verses and messengers objects of ridicule. The wording “taking My verses and messengers in ridicule” suggests more than a passing slip. Mockery has become a repeated stance and a way of dealing with revelation.

Verse 107

As for those who believe and do righteous deeds, they will have gardens of Paradise[56] as a dwelling place[57],

After those who lose most in their deeds comes the opposite: people who believe and do righteous deeds. Mere movement is not the measure. What matters is the faith that directs it and righteous action consistent with that faith. For these people are the gardens of al-Firdaws, “gardens of Paradise[56] as a dwelling place[57]”. This is the goal worthy of turning a person’s principles from ideas into movement throughout life.

Verse 108

abiding therein forever, never desiring to leave.

After the surah’s continual movement, from the cave to the garden, from Moses’s journey to the earth’s east and west, comes complete rest. They abide in al-Firdaws forever, “never desiring to leave”. Someone who moved through this world for Allah reaches a station he does not wish to exchange for another. Its blessings have none of the deficiencies that continually drive people to search elsewhere.

Verse 109

Say, “If the ocean were ink for [writing] the Words of my Lord, the ocean would surely run dry before the Words of my Lord[58] are finished, even if We brought its like to resupply it.”

After all the wonders related in the surah, the reader should not imagine having encompassed Allah’s signs. If the entire ocean became ink, its waters would run out before the Lord’s words, even if another like it were brought to replenish it. Allah’s knowledge and signs are vaster than the stories that have astonished us. Human beings encompass the meanings of His revelation only to the extent He wills.

Verse 110

Say, “I am only a man like you; it has been revealed to me that your God is One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him do righteous deeds and associate none in the worship of his Lord.”

The conclusion returns the heart to the foundation of the message. The Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, is human, distinguished by Allah’s revelation to him that there is One God. Wishful thinking is not enough for someone who hopes to meet this Lord. He must do righteous deeds and protect his worship from associating partners with Allah. Thus the conclusion brings together what keeps our striving from going astray: revelation that guides the path, the meeting with Allah that determines the goal, and worship of Allah alone that makes every deed for Him.
